# Nominal Group Technique

A small group meets to answer one question, but for most of the session its
members work as individuals: they write their ideas down alone, read them out
one at a time, clarify what each means, and then rank the list privately. The
group's priorities are the totals of those private rankings. "Nominal" refers
to that: it is a group in name, while the ideas and the judgements come from
each person separately.

Andre Delbecq and Andrew Van de Ven described the procedure in 1971 as a way
to involve clients, staff and experts in programme planning without letting
status decide the outcome. In a 1974 study they compared it with Delphi and
with ordinary discussion groups, and in 1975, with David Gustafson, they
published a practical guide to both techniques. It has since become a standard
consensus method in health services research, programme evaluation and
education.

## When to use

- A committee, conference or working group must leave one meeting with a
  ranked shortlist: which needs come first, which proposals to fund, which
  problems to tackle
- Status or confidence differs a lot within the group, and in open discussion
  the chair, the expert or the longest-serving member sets the order
- The people who must accept the result are in the room and their
  preferences should count equally
- You want both a full list of contributions and a transparent, countable
  priority order to report afterwards

## When *not* to use

- When the problem is tangled and needs real exploration and argument before
  anyone can sensibly rank options; the procedure deliberately keeps debate
  short. A [Fishbowl](Fishbowl.md) or a [World Café](World_Cafe.md) gives
  discussion more room
- When the only aim is a long list of ideas with no ranking; use
  [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md)
- When the experts are scattered across organisations or should stay
  anonymous to each other, or when you have weeks rather than an afternoon;
  use the [Delphi Method](Delphi_Method.md)
- When you need to hear from a large crowd quickly and converge through
  conversation rather than a count; use [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md)
- When the options are already known and must be compared on explicit,
  weighted criteria; use a [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md)
- When the group must adopt a single proposal it can live with rather than an
  order of priorities; use [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md)
- In a small team of three or four that already speaks openly, where the
  formal steps add ceremony without changing who is heard
- When a lone dissenting view could be decisive, for example a safety concern:
  adding up ranks will outvote it, so give such points a separate hearing

## Facilitation outline (about 2 hours, 5 to 9 people per table)

Preparation: write the question as a single, concrete sentence (for example
"Which measures would most improve attendance at our school next year?").
Decide in advance how many items each person will rank and how points are
scored. Groups larger than about ten are split into parallel tables that run
the same steps and pool their tallies at the end.

1. (5 min) Introduce the question, the steps and what will happen to the
   result. Make clear that the final order comes from everyone's private
   ranking, not from the discussion.
2. (10–15 min) Silent writing. Each person writes as many answers to the
   question as they can, in short phrases, without talking.
3. (20–30 min) Round-robin recording. Going round the table, each person
   reads out one idea at a time and the facilitator writes it on the flip
   chart, numbered, in the speaker's words. No comments or debate yet. People
   may pass; continue until everyone's list is exhausted.
4. (20–30 min) Clarification. Take the items in order and ask whether anyone
   has a question or comment about each. The aim is shared understanding,
   not persuasion; anyone may explain an item, not only its author. Merge
   duplicates only with the agreement of the people who proposed them.
5. (10–15 min) Private ranking. Each person picks their top five (or another
   agreed number) and scores them, for example 5 points for the most important
   down to 1. Collect the sheets.
6. (10–15 min) Tally. Record each item's total points and the number of
   people who chose it, and show the resulting order to the group.
7. (10 min, optional) Discuss the result, especially items that split the
   group sharply. If the order is very close, a second ranking of the top
   items can follow.

Online, the same steps work with a shared document for the list and a private
poll or form for the ranking; keep cameras on and the round-robin strict.

## Common pitfalls

- A vague or double question ("problems and solutions"), which produces lists
  nobody can rank meaningfully
- Letting the round-robin slide into debate, which hands the floor back to the
  most vocal members
- The facilitator rewording or merging ideas without asking, so contributors
  no longer recognise them
- Asking for rankings by show of hands, which removes the privacy that makes
  the method work
- Reporting only the top items and dropping the rest, including views that a
  minority held strongly
- Treating the tally as the decision when the group has no authority to make
  it; say up front who will act on the result
