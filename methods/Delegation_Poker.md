# Delegation Poker and Delegation Board

Agree, one area of decisions at a time, how much authority sits with a team and
how much stays with its leader. Instead of the yes-or-no question "is this team
empowered?", every recurring kind of decision gets a level on a seven-step
scale. Leader and team choose their level in secret, reveal at the same moment,
talk about the gaps, and record the agreed level on a board that stays visible.

The seven levels, from the leader holding all authority to the team holding all
of it:

| Level | Name | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tell | The leader decides and announces the decision. |
| 2 | Sell | The leader decides, then explains the reasons and seeks buy-in. |
| 3 | Consult | The leader asks the team for input first, then decides. |
| 4 | Agree | Leader and team decide together; neither can settle it alone. |
| 5 | Advise | The team decides; the leader offers an opinion beforehand. |
| 6 | Inquire | The team decides and tells the leader afterwards. |
| 7 | Delegate | The team decides; the leader does not need to hear about it. |

Jurgen Appelo set out the levels in *Management 3.0: Leading Agile Developers,
Developing Agile Leaders* (Addison-Wesley, 2010), and the card game and the board
are taught as practices within his commercial Management 3.0 programme. The idea
of a graded scale between "the manager decides" and "the group decides" is much
older: Tannenbaum and Schmidt described such a continuum of leadership behaviour
in the *Harvard Business Review* in 1958. What the game adds is a way for both
sides to state their expectations at the same moment, and a record of the result
for each area of work.

## When to use

- A leader says the team should take more ownership while the team feels its
  decisions are routinely overturned, and both are frustrated
- An organisation has announced self-organisation or empowerment, and nobody
  can say what it covers in day-to-day matters
- A new leader takes over a team, or a team is newly formed, and expectations
  about who decides what have not been stated yet
- A leader wants to hand over authority gradually and needs a way to show which
  areas have moved and which have not

## When *not* to use

- When the competence is fixed by law, statute, regulation or contract. In
  public administration many sign-offs are assigned to an office or an official
  by law and cannot be handed to a team by agreement. Mark these areas as fixed
  before you start, and only play the room left inside them.
- When trust between leader and team is low. People will pick the card they
  expect the leader wants, and the board will record a fiction. Address the
  relationship first.
- When the question is who drives and who signs off one particular decision —
  use the [DACI framework](DACI_Matrix.md). This method sets standing levels for
  whole categories of decisions, not roles for a single case.
- When the question is who carries out which tasks in a project; a
  responsibility-assignment matrix answers that, not a delegation level.
- When the group needs a procedure for reaching a decision together. Level 4
  says the team and the leader decide jointly, but not how; for that, see
  [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md).
- When the issue is one employee's development in a one-to-one conversation —
  [GROW](GROW_Model.md) fits better.

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

1. (10 min) Explain the purpose and the seven levels. Stress that a low level
   is not a verdict on anyone's ability; some areas are rightly kept with the
   leader.
2. (15 min) Collect the recurring decision areas the team actually meets:
   hiring, budget below a set amount, tools, working hours, priorities within a
   sprint or term, contact with external partners. Aim for eight to fifteen.
   Mark the ones fixed by law or policy and set them aside.
3. (45 min) For each area, read it out as a short, concrete situation.
   Everyone, the leader included, picks a level privately (cards numbered 1–7,
   or a show of fingers, or a simultaneous poll online) and reveals it at the
   same time. If the choices are close, agree the level and move on. If they
   are far apart, ask the highest and the lowest to explain their reasoning,
   then choose again. The leader states the level they can live with; if it
   differs from the group's, say so openly and record why.
4. (10 min) Put the results on a board: decision areas down the side, the
   seven levels across the top, one card per area in its agreed column. Keep it
   on the team's wall or in its shared workspace.
5. (10 min) Agree when to review the board — after a set period, after a
   change of leader, or whenever someone is unsure whether they were allowed to
   act. Name who calls the review.

For a team new to the idea, a warm-up round with invented situations helps
people learn the scale before discussing real ones.

## Common pitfalls

- Using vague areas such as "strategy" or "quality". Each area must be concrete
  enough that someone can tell whether a particular choice falls inside it.
- Treating level 7 as the goal for everything. The aim is clarity, and many
  areas belong at levels 3 to 5.
- The leader revealing first or commenting before the reveal, which anchors the
  team.
- Agreeing a level and then overruling it at the first disagreement. One broken
  agreement undoes the board's credibility; if a level has to change, change
  it openly.
- Never revisiting the board. Authority should move as the team gains
  experience, and the record should show it.
