# Mystery Shopping (Mystery Calls)

Send trained testers through a real service encounter as if they were ordinary
customers or citizens: a visit to the counter, a phone call, an e-mail, a
web-chat or contact-form enquiry. Each follows a prepared scenario, and as soon
as the contact is over records against a short checklist what actually
happened. Across many such contacts a picture emerges of how the service is
delivered in practice, as opposed to how the charter, the training manual or
the monthly report says it is delivered.

The technique comes from retail and market research, where it has been in use
since about the 1940s, and it is now common in banking, hospitality, health
care and public administration. Telephone tests are often called *mystery
calls*; in German the usual terms are *Testkauf*, *Testkunde* and *Mystery
Call*. Regulators use it to check what advice firms actually give, councils
and housing providers train residents as testers, and patient organisations
use it to see how easy it is to register with a doctor or get through on the
phone. The professional codes of the market research associations set out the
ethical ground rules summarised below.

## When to use

- The organisation has service promises (reply times, phone reachability, what
  must be explained or asked) and wants to know whether they are kept in
  ordinary contacts, not just when a manager is watching
- Complaints suggest that people get wrong, incomplete or inconsistent
  information, while internal figures look fine, and you need evidence that
  both sides will accept
- Many branches, offices or call agents should deliver the same thing, and you
  want to see where practice differs, on identical scenarios
- Accuracy matters and customers cannot judge it themselves: legal or financial
  advice, a required safety question in a pharmacy, the correct fee or deadline
  named by a public office
- After training or a new standard, to check whether it has reached the
  front line
- Public sector: a municipality, tax office, housing department or school
  authority that wants to know whether citizens who phone, write or come to the
  counter get through and get a usable answer

## When *not* to use

- **To catch or discipline individuals.** The market research codes are clear
  that results should serve training and improvement, and that individual
  staff should not be identifiable unless they were told in advance exactly
  how results would be used. A programme that ends in warnings for named
  people destroys trust and teaches staff to hunt for testers.
- **When staff have not been told it might happen.** Announce in advance,
  through the staff handbook, a team meeting or a circular, that service may
  be checked from time to time by mystery shoppers, what will be looked at, at
  what level results will be reported and whether anything will be recorded.
  You do not announce dates or scenarios. Involve the staff council or union
  where there is one.
- **To find out why a service fails.** Mystery shopping shows *what* happens
  in the encounter. The causes (staffing, IT, rules, workload) need
  conversations with the staff, for example a [Gemba Walk](Gemba_Walk.md) or
  a [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) on a recurring failure.
- **To see whether people can find their way through a form, letter or
  website on their own.** That is [Usability Testing](Usability_Testing.md):
  real users try the thing in front of you, openly. Mystery shopping tests the
  encounter with staff, under cover.
- **To understand the whole sequence of contacts.** A
  [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md) follows everything a person
  goes through from first enquiry to outcome; mystery shopping checks single
  encounters as they really happen. The two combine well: the map shows which
  touchpoints are worth testing.
- **When the service is already overwhelmed.** Test calls to a line that
  people already cannot get through to add load and tell you what you know.
  Weigh whether the exercise is proportionate.
- **As a substitute for asking customers.** Testers judge against a checklist;
  they are not the people who actually depend on the service. Pair the results
  with complaints, surveys or interviews.

## Facilitation outline (about 3 to 6 weeks, 2 to 8 working days of effort)

1. **(1 to 2 h) Set the question.** Write down which promise or behaviour you
   want to check, in which channels and at which sites, and what you will do
   with the result. Decide who sees the results and at what level (service,
   site, channel; not named individuals).
2. **(Before any test) Inform staff.** Tell staff that mystery shopping will
   take place in the coming months, what it covers, how results will be used
   and that it is about the service, not about catching people. Agree this with
   staff representatives.
3. **(Half a day) Write scenarios.** Two to five realistic, simple situations
   that a real customer or citizen would bring, each designed to test a
   specific behaviour (for example: "I am moving in next month; what do I need
   to register, and what does it cost?"). Prepare a believable back-story and
   answers to likely follow-up questions, plus a polite way to end the contact
   early. Avoid anything illegal, unsafe for the tester, or costly for the
   service, such as a real booking.
4. **(Half a day) Build the checklist.** Mostly factual, yes/no or timed items
   the tester can remember: Was the call answered within five rings? Was the
   fee given correctly? Was a reply received within two working days? Add one
   or two overall judgements at most. Keep it short enough to recall until the
   tester is out of sight.
5. **(2 to 3 h) Recruit and brief testers.** Choose people who plausibly fit
   the scenario: residents, volunteers, staff from another unit or an agency.
   Rehearse the scenarios, the checklist and how to behave naturally without
   sounding scripted. Run one or two pilot contacts and adjust.
6. **(1 to 3 weeks) Carry out the contacts.** Spread them across sites,
   channels, weekdays and times of day, according to a plan. Testers complete
   the checklist immediately after each contact. A coordinator checks returns
   for completeness and consistency.
7. **(Half a day) Analyse.** Pool the results and look for patterns: which
   items fail often, in which channel, at what times. Report aggregated
   results; quote examples only in a way that does not identify a person.
8. **(1 to 2 h) Feed back and act.** Share the findings with the teams
   concerned, ask them what gets in the way, agree changes to training,
   information material, staffing or procedures, and repeat a smaller round
   later to see whether the changes took hold.

## Common pitfalls

- Using results to name and blame individuals, or letting it look that way;
  it breaks the trust the method depends on
- Scenarios that are too elaborate or unusual, so staff spot the tester and
  the contact stops being ordinary
- Checklists full of subjective ratings ("Was the employee friendly? 1 to 10")
  that vary more between testers than between sites
- Too few contacts, then treating one bad visit as the truth about a whole
  office
- Testers who are clearly unlike the real customers, for instance always
  confident native speakers, when the service mostly fails for people who are
  not
- Running the same testers and scenarios round after round, so that staff
  learn to recognise them
- Delivering a score but no follow-up; staff experience it as surveillance
  without any benefit
