# PDCA / PDSA Cycle

Test a change small, predict its effect before you start, compare the result with
the prediction, and decide what to do next. Then go round again, a little wider
each time, until the change is either proven or dropped.

1. **Plan** — the change, the conditions of the trial, and a written prediction
   of what will happen and how you will see it.
2. **Do** — run the trial on a small scale and note what actually happened,
   including the surprises.
3. **Study** (or **Check**) — set the observations against the prediction. Where
   they differ, that is what you learned.
4. **Act** — adopt the change, adapt it for another round, or abandon it.

The loop goes back to Walter Shewhart's work on statistical quality control at
Bell Labs in the 1920s and 1930s. W. Edwards Deming taught it in Japan from 1950,
where it became known as PDCA and later part of Toyota's way of working. Deming
himself came to prefer "Study" over "Check", because checking suggests
inspection against a standard, while the point is to learn why the result came
out as it did. The PDSA form most used today is embedded in the Model for
Improvement (Langley, Moen, Nolan and colleagues, *The Improvement Guide*),
which puts three questions in front of the cycles: what are we trying to
accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, and what change can we
make that will result in improvement. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement
spread that model through health care; the Carnegie Foundation carried it into
school improvement networks.

## When to use

- An idea for improvement exists and people are about to introduce it everywhere
  at once, with no evidence that it works in this setting
- Improvements get discussed and decided, but nobody checks afterwards whether
  anything changed
- The change can be tried on a small slice — one class, one shift, one office,
  one day — and a simple measure will show an effect within days or weeks
- In school development and public-service improvement, where a failed
  large-scale rollout is costly for pupils or citizens and hard to reverse

## When *not* to use

- When the cause of the problem is still unknown — find it first with
  [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) or an [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md),
  then test countermeasures in cycles
- When the delay or waste is spread across a whole process you have not yet
  seen end to end — map it with [Value Stream Mapping](Value_Stream_Mapping.md)
- For an organisation-wide transformation that needs sponsorship, a coalition
  and sustained leadership attention — that is [Kotter's 8 steps](Kotter_Eight_Steps.md);
  cycles can then test the individual changes inside it
- When the change cannot be tried small (a legal deadline, an irreversible
  investment) — stress-test the plan with a [Pre-Mortem](Pre_Mortem.md) instead
- When only a single past event needs to be understood — use an
  [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md)

## Facilitation outline (first cycle, 60 min planning + trial + 45 min review)

**Planning session (60 min)**

1. (10 min) State the aim in one sentence: what should improve, for whom, by how
   much, by when.
2. (10 min) Agree one or two measures that will move quickly enough to show an
   effect during the trial.
3. (15 min) Choose the change to test and shrink the trial until it is almost
   uncomfortably small: one teacher, one counter, one morning.
4. (15 min) Each participant writes a prediction privately — what will happen
   and why — before the group agrees on a shared one. Record both.
5. (10 min) Settle who runs the trial, who records observations, and when the
   review takes place.

**Trial (hours to two weeks)** — run it as planned and log what happens,
including problems and side effects, not only the measure.

**Review session (45 min)**

1. (15 min) Lay out the data and observations next to the prediction.
2. (15 min) Discuss the gaps: where were we wrong, and what does that say about
   how the change works here?
3. (10 min) Decide: adopt, adapt or abandon. If adapting, define the next
   cycle — usually a slightly larger scale or different conditions.
4. (5 min) Write a short cycle record so later cycles and other teams can build
   on it.

## Common pitfalls

- Running one cycle and calling it done; the method only works as a sequence
- Skipping the prediction, which leaves nothing to learn from when the result arrives
- Starting with a trial that is too large, so failure is expensive and people
  defend the change instead of studying it
- Choosing a measure that will not move for months
- Treating "Act" as "implement everywhere" after a single favourable trial
- Running one cycle and calling it done; a systematic review of published PDSA
  projects found that fewer than one in five documented a sequence of iterative
  cycles, and few kept to small-scale tests or tracked data often enough to
  steer the next cycle (Taylor et al., 2014)
