# Personas

Condense what you learned from real users into a handful of fictional but
research-based archetypes, each standing for one distinct group: what they are
trying to achieve, the situation they are in, what they can and cannot do, and
what gets in their way. The team then checks design, priority and wording
decisions against those named people instead of against "the user", a figure
everyone pictures differently and who usually looks a lot like the person
speaking.

Alan Cooper introduced personas into software design and popularised them in
*The Inmates Are Running the Asylum* (1999). Pruitt and Grudin (2003) described
how a large product organisation built and used them, and stressed that their
value comes from the data behind them and from keeping them in active use.
Public bodies use them too: the UK Government Digital Service published a set
of personas of people who need help with online services, built from
statistics and some seventy interviews, for departments to design against.

## When to use

- The team designs for an imagined average user, or each member argues from a
  different mental picture of who the users are
- Research (interviews, observation, surveys, usage data) exists, but it is not
  shaping everyday decisions
- A service has clearly different user groups whose needs pull in different
  directions, and you need to decide which group a feature or change is for
- New colleagues, agencies or testers need a quick, shared briefing on who the
  service is for

## When *not* to use

- When you have no research and no way to get any. Personas written from the
  team's assumptions ("proto-personas") can be a useful way to surface those
  assumptions, but only if they are labelled as guesses and tested; presented
  as findings, they turn existing prejudice into an official-looking document
- When the risk of stereotyping outweighs the benefit. Names, photos, age,
  gender and origin make a persona memorable, but they also invite
  caricature and quietly suggest who the "normal" user is (Turner and Turner,
  2011; Marsden and Haag, 2016). Leave out any trait that does not change a
  design decision, and check whether the set erases groups such as disabled
  users or people with little digital experience
- To decide the sequence of steps someone goes through across a service; that
  is a [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md), which can be drawn per
  persona
- To learn what progress a customer was trying to make when they switched to
  your product; interview for that with [Jobs To Be Done](Jobs_To_Be_Done.md)
- To work out which organisations or decision-makers can block or carry a
  project; that is a [Stakeholder Map](Stakeholder_Map.md), not a model of users
- When the question is how users group and label content; run a
  [Card Sorting](Card_Sorting.md) study
- As a substitute for testing with real people: a persona tells you whom to
  recruit and what to watch for, not whether the design works

## Facilitation outline (one-day synthesis workshop, after the research)

Preparation: interviews or observations with users from each suspected group
(often 5 to 10 per group), plus whatever survey or usage data exists. Everyone
attending reads or listens to part of the raw material beforehand.

1. (30 min) State the decision the personas must support (for example, the
   redesign of an enquiry service). Personas built for no particular purpose
   end up too general to use.
2. (90 min) Pull observations out of the research onto cards: goals, tasks,
   context of use, skills, attitudes, frustrations. One observation per card,
   each traceable to its source.
3. (60 min) Look for the dimensions along which users really differ, such as
   confidence with the technology, how often they use the service, or whether
   they act for themselves or someone else. Place each research participant on
   those scales and look for clusters.
4. (90 min) Turn each cluster into a draft persona: a name, a short summary of
   their situation, three to five goals, the main frustrations, the context in
   which they use the service, and a quote taken from the research. Keep
   demographic detail to what affects the design.
5. (45 min) Challenge the set: does each persona rest on several real
   participants? Are any two the same? Who from the research fits none of
   them? Which one is the primary persona for this decision?
6. (45 min) Write two or three short scenarios per persona for the decision
   at hand, and agree who keeps the personas current and when they will be
   revisited against new research.

## Common pitfalls

- Inventing details to make a persona vivid and then forgetting which parts
  are evidence and which are fiction
- Building a persona for every stakeholder's favourite customer until the set
  is too large to guide anything
- Reaching for stock photos and clichés that turn a user group into a
  stereotype
- A research team producing personas alone and handing them over; people who
  were not involved rarely trust or use them
- Never updating them, so the personas describe the users of five years ago
