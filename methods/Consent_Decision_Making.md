# Consent Decision-Making

A proposal goes ahead when nobody in the group has a reasoned objection to it.
The question each person answers is not "is this what I would choose?" but
"can we try this without harming what we are here to do?" The bar is a decision
that is good enough for now and safe enough to try until a set review date.

The procedure comes from sociocracy, the governance approach Gerard Endenburg
developed in his family's Dutch engineering firm from the late 1960s, building
on ideas Kees Boeke had used at his school in Bilthoven. Endenburg called its
core rule "no objection". Sociocracy 3.0 later published it as a free-standing
pattern that any group can use without adopting the rest of sociocracy.

## Consent is not consensus

| | Consensus | Consent |
|---|---|---|
| Question asked | Does everyone agree this is the right choice? | Does anyone see a risk to our purpose that we should avoid? |
| What blocks it | Any lack of support, often a preference | Only an objection with a reason the group can examine |
| What happens to a block | Discussion continues until it goes away | The proposal is amended until the objection is resolved |
| Standard | The best answer everyone can endorse | An answer that is safe to try and will be reviewed |

Consent still gives every member the power to stop a proposal; what changes is
that this power has to be used with an argument, and the argument becomes part
of the next version. Concerns that are not objections are recorded and can
shape the review, but they do not hold the decision up.

## When to use

- Meetings keep running over because the group waits for everyone to agree
- Majority votes have left an outvoted side that reopens or ignores decisions
- Policies and working agreements that can sensibly be revisited after a trial
- Teams that manage themselves and have nobody to settle a deadlock for them
- Committees, boards and councils that want the minority to stay on board;
  a Dutch municipality used a consent-based process to move its council away
  from majority-versus-minority voting (Romme et al., 2018)
- Schools: staff teams settling shared policies, and classes or student
  councils setting their own rules, the setting where the approach began

## When *not* to use

- When the open question is who decides — clarify roles first with
  [DACI](DACI_Matrix.md)
- Irreversible or one-off choices that no review could correct
- When options have to be compared on money or measured value — use a
  [cost-benefit analysis](Cost_Benefit_Analysis.md) and bring its result as
  the proposal
- When the group has not yet explored the issue; consent decides a concrete
  proposal, so explore first with [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md)
- Emergencies, where one accountable person has to act immediately

## Facilitation outline (45 min)

1. (5 min) Read out the purpose the decision serves. If the group does not
   accept the purpose, stop: there is nothing to test objections against.
2. (5 min) The author presents one concrete proposal, including who does what
   and a review date.
3. (10 min) Clarifying questions only, one round. No opinions yet. The author
   may adjust wording to remove misunderstandings.
4. (5 min) Quick reaction round: each person says in a sentence or two how
   they see it. No discussion.
5. (5 min) Objection round: each person says either "no objection" or names
   an objection and its reason. The facilitator records objections verbatim.
6. (10 min) For each objection, first check it: does it show a harm to the
   purpose or a clearly better way, or is it a preference? Then amend the
   proposal with the objector until it no longer applies, and run the
   objection round again on the amended version.
7. (5 min) Record the decision, the review date and any concerns that were
   not objections, so the review can check them.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating it as consensus, so that "I would prefer something else" stops the
  decision
- Facilitators dismissing awkward objections as preferences instead of testing
  them with the objector
- Skipping the review date, which removes the reason it is safe to accept a
  first version
- Bringing a vague idea instead of a proposal; consent works on something
  concrete enough to object to
- Reading silence as consent; ask every person in the objection round,
  including those on a call or answering asynchronously
