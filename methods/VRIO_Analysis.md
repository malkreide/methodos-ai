# VRIO Analysis

Take the resources and capabilities an organisation believes it is good at and
test each one against four questions, in order. Only those that pass all four
are candidates for a lasting advantage; the rest either keep the organisation
level with others or are not worth defending at all.

| Question | Asks | If the answer is "no" |
|---|---|---|
| **V**aluable | Does it let us serve people better or more cheaply, seize an opportunity or fend off a threat? | A weakness or a cost, not a strength |
| **R**are | Do only a few others have it? | Parity: necessary to keep up, no edge |
| **I**nimitable | Would it be difficult or costly for others to obtain, build or substitute? | A head start that others will close |
| **O**rganised | Do our structures, processes, incentives and decisions actually put it to use? | An advantage left on the table |

The framework comes from the resource-based view of strategy. Jay Barney
(1991) argued that a firm gains a sustained competitive advantage from
resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and without
equivalent substitutes (often abbreviated VRIN). In a 1995 article for
practising managers he recast the test as four questions on value, rarity,
imitability and organisation, which is the form now known as VRIO.
Resources are understood broadly: physical assets and money, but also
reputation, relationships, culture, routines and the knowledge in people's
heads. Intangible ones usually do best on the imitation question, because they
grew out of a particular history, nobody can say exactly how they work, or
they depend on many people working together.

## When to use

- A strategy is being built on claimed strengths ("our people", "our
  expertise", "our reputation") and nobody has tested whether others have the
  same
- Deciding which capabilities to invest in or protect, and which simply need to
  be kept at an acceptable level
- After a broader stocktake has produced a long list of strengths that now has
  to be narrowed to the few that are truly distinctive
- When an organisation outside business faces real rivals for the same
  contracts, grants, students or mandate, and has to say what it offers that
  others cannot

## When *not* to use

- For a first, broad look at an organisation's position, inside and out: start
  with a [SWOT analysis](SWOT.md) and use VRIO afterwards on its strengths
- To understand the competitive pressure in a whole sector, use
  [Porter's Five Forces](Porters_Five_Forces.md); for trends in the wider
  environment, [PESTEL](PESTEL_Analysis.md)
- To learn how others actually do the same work better, use
  [Benchmarking](Benchmarking.md); VRIO asks whether others *could* match you,
  not how they work
- When the organisation is the only provider of a service and nobody else
  could take its funding or remit. A statutory body or a local school with a
  fixed catchment can still ask what it is distinctively good at, but "rare"
  and "hard to imitate" then have no competitor to be measured against, and
  the result is easily a flattering self-portrait. Bryson, Ackermann and Eden
  (2007) adapt the resource-based view to public organisations around
  distinctive competencies and stakeholder value rather than rivalry; that
  framing is usually the more honest one there.
- When the question is which new markets or offers to pursue: see the
  [Ansoff Matrix](Ansoff_Matrix.md) or the
  [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md)

## Facilitation outline (3 hours)

1. (20 min) **Frame.** Agree whose choice the analysis is meant to serve and
   who the relevant "others" are: competitors, other providers, other
   applicants for the same funds. If nobody can be named, stop and reconsider
   whether this framework fits (see above).
2. (30 min) **List candidates.** Collect resources and capabilities on cards,
   phrased concretely ("a tutor team that has worked together for ten years",
   not "good staff"). Include tangible and intangible ones. Cluster duplicates
   and keep at most 12–15.
3. (60 min) **Test, one card at a time.** Ask the four questions in order and
   stop at the first "no". For every "yes", ask for evidence: who else has
   this, how long would it take them to get it, what would it cost. Where the
   group cannot agree, mark the card and note what information would decide it.
4. (30 min) **Read the result.** Sort the cards into five outcomes: not
   valuable, parity, temporary advantage, unused advantage (V, R and I pass, O
   fails), and lasting advantage. Unused advantages are often the most
   actionable finding.
5. (30 min) **Decide.** For each lasting advantage, name what would protect or
   deepen it. For each unused advantage, name the structural change that would
   let the organisation use it. For parity items, agree a "good enough" level
   instead of trying to excel. Assign owners.
6. (10 min) **Check.** Ask one or two people who know the field from outside
   (a funder, a customer, a partner) to challenge the cards rated as lasting
   advantages before acting on them.

## Common pitfalls

- Rating everything as rare and hard to copy. Insiders overrate what they
  know well; an outside view in step 6 is the usual corrective.
- Vague cards. "Quality" or "commitment" cannot be tested; a specific
  relationship, routine or body of know-how can.
- Forgetting substitutes. A rival does not need the same resource if a
  different one does the same job for the people served.
- Treating the result as permanent. Advantages erode as others learn and
  circumstances change; repeat the exercise when either shifts.
- Using it as the whole strategy. VRIO says where an organisation is strong
  relative to others, not what it should do next or whether its environment
  still values those strengths.
- Borrowing competitive language uncritically in a public body or charity,
  where collaboration with the "rivals" may serve people better than
  outdoing them.

## Limits worth knowing

The resource-based view is among the most cited theories in management and
also among the most criticised. Kraaijenbrink, Spender and Groen (2010) review
the critiques: the notions of "resource" and "value" are so broad that the
theory risks explaining success by restating it, the four criteria are neither
strictly necessary nor sufficient for an advantage, and the view is largely
static. Szymaniec-Mlicka (2014) finds in a literature review that the
approach is still little used in public management and that evidence of its
benefit there is thin. Treat VRIO as a structured way to challenge claims of
distinctiveness, not as a measurement.
