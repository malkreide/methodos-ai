# Tuning Protocol

A small group helps one person improve a piece of work that is still in
progress. The person presenting sets the terms: they explain what the work is
meant to achieve and ask one focusing question. The group then examines the
work and talks about it among themselves, naming both where it already serves
its purpose and where it may fall short, while the presenter listens without
replying. Only at the end does the presenter speak again, to think aloud about
what they heard. The name comes from the image of an instrument: the work is
not judged good or bad but brought more closely "in tune" with its own goals.

The protocol was devised in the Coalition of Essential Schools in the early
1990s. Joseph McDonald developed it for the Coalition's Exhibitions Project,
in which five high schools were building new forms of student assessment
(exhibitions, portfolios, design projects) and needed a way to get disciplined
feedback from one another; it was first tried in 1992. David Allen later
revised it, and the School Reform Initiative carried it into its protocol
library, which is now published by the Center for Leadership and Educational
Equity (CLEE). Schools mostly use it for student work, unit plans and
rubrics, but nothing in the structure is specific to teaching: any draft with
a clear purpose can be tuned.

## When to use

- A piece of work is far enough along to show, but not yet fixed: a project
  assignment and its marking criteria, a course unit, a service or product
  design, a programme plan, a grant application, a draft policy or guideline
- The author wants critique on a specific question, not a general opinion
- Previous review rounds were either too polite to help or became a debate in
  which the author defended each choice
- A team wants a regular, safe routine for looking at one another's work, so
  that standards are discussed against real examples

## When *not* to use

- **When the work is finished and cannot change.** Feedback the presenter
  cannot act on wastes the group's time and the presenter's goodwill
- **When the issue is a person's situation rather than a piece of work** (a
  stuck conflict with a pupil, a client, a colleague). Use
  [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md)
- **When a decision-maker needs the strongest case against a plan.** The
  tuning protocol improves work on its own terms; it does not argue for an
  alternative. Use a [Red Team Review](Red_Team_Review.md)
- **When the aim is to design and observe one lesson together.** Use
  [Lesson Study](Lesson_Study.md)
- **When feedback is about one person's behaviour.** Give it directly, one to
  one, with [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md)
- **When the presenter is being assessed.** If someone in the circle writes the
  presenter's appraisal, the probing comments soften and the protocol turns into
  a performance

## Facilitation outline (about 60 minutes, 4 to 10 people)

Roles: one **presenter**, one **facilitator** who keeps time and holds the
phases, everyone else a **participant**. Before the session the presenter
chooses a manageable sample of the work (a few pages, a few screens, three or
four pieces of student work rather than a whole class set) and writes the
focusing question. Agree two norms: comments are about the work, not the
person, and once the presenter rejoins, the feedback is over.

1. (5 min) **Introduction.** The facilitator states the purpose, the phases and
   the norms. With a group that is new to protocols, say plainly that the
   presenter will be silent for part of the time and why.
2. (10–15 min) **Presentation.** The presenter explains the context, what the
   work is supposed to achieve, the criteria they use to judge it, and the
   focusing question, which is written up where everyone can see it (for
   example "Does the task actually require the reasoning the rubric rewards?"
   or "Where will an applicant get lost in this form?"). Participants listen
   without interrupting.
3. (5 min) **Clarifying questions.** Participants ask short factual questions
   the presenter can answer in a sentence. The facilitator stops anything that
   is really a suggestion or a verdict in question form ("Have you considered…?").
4. (10–15 min) **Examining the work.** Everyone studies the material quietly,
   noting where it seems aligned with the stated goals and where there may be
   gaps, always with the focusing question in mind. End with two minutes of
   silence in which participants decide what they want to say.
5. (10–15 min) **Discussion while the presenter listens.** The presenter moves
   slightly back from the circle and takes notes. Participants talk to one
   another, not to the presenter. They usually begin with appreciative
   observations about what serves the goals and should be kept, then turn to
   probing ones: possible gaps, mismatches, questions the work raises,
   suggestions tied to the presenter's aims. After that the two kinds can
   alternate. The facilitator re-reads the focusing question if the talk
   drifts.
6. (5–10 min) **Presenter reflection.** The presenter rejoins and reflects
   aloud on what struck them, what they will change and what they will leave as
   it is. This is not a reply to each comment and not a defence. Participants
   do not respond.
7. (5–10 min) **Debrief.** The whole group talks about the process: did the
   focusing question help, were the clarifying questions really clarifying, was
   the balance between appreciative and probing comments right? Note one thing
   to do differently next time.

Short versions (15 to 30 minutes, often used with students critiquing each
other's projects) keep the same order with smaller samples and tighter times.
Remote sessions work with the material in a shared document and the presenter's
camera turned off during the discussion.

## Common pitfalls

- **A focusing question that is too broad** ("What do you think?"). Without a
  real question the discussion becomes a tour of everyone's preferences
- **Bringing too much.** A full unit, a whole report or thirty samples cannot
  be examined in fifteen minutes; choose a representative slice
- **Clarifying questions that smuggle in advice.** The facilitator has to stop
  them, or the presenter starts defending before the discussion begins
- **Only appreciation, or only criticism.** Groups new to the format either
  stay safely positive or treat the probing phase as licence to dismantle the
  work. Both lose the point: comments are measured against the presenter's goals
- **The presenter breaking in.** The silence is the mechanism. If the presenter
  answers each comment, the session reverts to attack and defence
- **Skipping the debrief.** It is where the group gets better at the protocol
  itself, and where problems with tone or trust surface early
