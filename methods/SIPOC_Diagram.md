# SIPOC Diagram

A one-page table that fixes the scope of a process before anyone tries to
improve it. Its five columns give the method its name: **S**uppliers,
**I**nputs, **P**rocess, **O**utputs, **C**ustomers. The process column holds
only four to six high-level steps; the point is not detail but agreement on
where the process begins and ends, what it depends on, what it produces and
for whom.

The table was in use in total quality management programmes by the late 1980s
and became a standard tool of Six Sigma, where it is usually drawn at the start
of a DMAIC project (most sources place it in the Define phase; some in
Measure) or at the start of a kaizen event. A reversed form, COPIS, fills the
columns starting from the customer to keep attention on what recipients need.
Public bodies use it too: the City of Tulsa's improvement playbook includes it
in the Define phase with a permit example, and National Highways in England
recommends it at the start of any process that involves several parties.

## When to use

- At the start of an improvement project, when the people around the table
  describe different versions of "the process"
- When it is unclear who the process is really for, or what those recipients
  require from it
- When a process crosses several units and each one knows only its own part of
  what comes in and goes out
- As the first page of a project charter, to show a sponsor what is in scope
  and what is not
- To give newcomers, or people whose picture of the process is out of date, a
  quick overview

## When *not* to use

- To find out where work waits, loops back or gets stuck. Four to six steps
  are far too coarse for that; draw a [Value Stream Map](Value_Stream_Mapping.md)
  once the scope is agreed.
- To understand what the process feels like for the person going through it;
  that is a [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md).
- To settle who does which task. SIPOC names steps, not responsibilities; use a
  [RACI Matrix](RACI_Matrix.md).
- Without the people who do the work. A table drawn by managers alone records
  how they believe the process runs; check it against the real work, for
  example on a [Gemba Walk](Gemba_Walk.md).
- As proof that something will improve. Its value rests on practitioner
  experience in quality programmes; there is little controlled evidence of its
  effect on its own.

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

Prepare a wall or digital board with five labelled columns and invite people
from each part of the process, including those who supply it and those who
receive its results.

1. (10 min) Name the process in a short phrase ("enrolling a new pupil") and
   agree on its trigger and its end point. Write both at the top. Most of the
   argument happens here, and that is the point.
2. (20 min) Fill the **Process** column with four to six steps, each a verb and
   an object ("check documents", "assign class"). Resist sub-steps; park them
   on a separate sheet.
3. (15 min) Fill **Outputs**: what the process produces, including documents,
   decisions and information, not only the main product.
4. (15 min) Fill **Customers**: everyone who receives an output, internal or
   external. If time allows, note one or two requirements per customer, marked
   as assumptions to be checked later.
5. (15 min) Fill **Inputs** and then **Suppliers**: what the steps need and who
   provides it. Inputs that often arrive late or incomplete get a mark.
6. (15 min) Read the table aloud from left to right. Note what was moved out
   of scope, which customer requirements need verifying and who will confirm
   the table with the sponsor and with staff who were not in the room.

Many practitioners fill the columns in this order (process, outputs,
customers, inputs, suppliers) because the steps are the easiest to agree on
and give the other columns something to attach to.

## Common pitfalls

- Letting the process column grow into a detailed flowchart; that discussion
  belongs to the next tool
- Listing "the public" or "management" as the customer instead of naming who
  receives which output
- Leaving out the people who do the work, or the suppliers and recipients
  themselves
- Treating customer requirements written in the room as facts rather than as
  assumptions to check with those customers
- Filing the table with the project papers and never returning to it when the
  scope starts to drift
