# Ladder of Inference

A picture of how a person gets from something they noticed to something they
do, drawn as a ladder they climb without noticing. At the bottom is everything
that could be observed. Each rung adds a step of the person's own making:

1. **Observable data**: what a camera or a recording would have caught.
2. **Selected data**: the few details this person happened to notice.
3. **Meanings**: what those details signify to them, coloured by their
   culture, role and experience.
4. **Assumptions**: gaps filled in, including guesses about other people's
   motives.
5. **Conclusions**: a verdict about what is going on or why.
6. **Beliefs**: a more general view of the person, the group or the situation.
7. **Actions**: what they do on the strength of that belief.

The climb takes seconds and feels like seeing, so the conclusion at the top
seems obvious and the steps below it are rarely mentioned. A reinforcing loop
makes it faster next time: the belief at the top decides which details the
person picks out the next time a similar situation comes up. Two people who
selected different details, or read them differently, can then argue
earnestly about verdicts while never comparing what each of them saw.

The ladder is used in two directions. Alone, a person climbs down their own
ladder before acting ("what did I actually see, and what did I add?"). In a
conversation, people state their own reasoning from the bottom up and ask the
other person to do the same ("what did you see that led you there?").

The model comes from Chris Argyris's work on action science and organisational
learning, notably *Overcoming Organizational Defenses* (1990). It reached a
wide practitioner audience through Rick Ross's chapter on the ladder in Peter
Senge, Art Kleiner, Charlotte Roberts, Richard Ross and Bryan Smith,
*The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook* (1994). It is now taught in management and
leadership development, in coach development in sport and in conflict
education.

## When to use

- Someone is about to act on a firm judgement about a colleague, a team or
  another department that rests on a handful of incidents
- Two people or two camps hold opposite verdicts about the same person,
  decision or result and keep restating them
- A team keeps reading hostile motives into another group's messages
- In a review or a coaching conversation, to look back at a reaction that
  went wrong and see at which step the reasoning left the facts
- As shared vocabulary that a team agrees on, so that anyone can ask
  "what did you see?" without it sounding like an attack

## When *not* to use

- To tell one person how a specific behaviour of theirs affected you: that is
  a feedback conversation, see [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md); the ladder can
  help you prepare it by checking what you actually observed
- To learn how others see you and what you keep to yourself, as a way of
  building trust in a group: use the [Johari Window](Johari_Window.md)
- To find the cause of a failure that everyone agrees happened: use
  [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md)
- When the facts are agreed and the parties want different things: that is a
  negotiation, see [Principled Negotiation](Principled_Negotiation.md)
- After an incident that hurt people, where harm has to be acknowledged and
  amends agreed: use a [Restorative Circle](Restorative_Circle.md)
- When a dispute has already hardened into open hostility, threats or
  coalition-building: the ladder alone will not de-escalate it; bring in a
  mediator

## Facilitation outline (60 min, team session)

Suitable for 3 to 15 people. Draw the ladder large on a flip chart or board
with the rungs labelled, and leave room beside each rung for notes.

1. (10 min) **Introduce the ladder.** Explain the rungs with a neutral example
   that has nothing to do with the team, such as a stranger who did not hold a
   door open. Point out the reinforcing loop.
2. (10 min) **Practise separating data.** Ask the group to describe an object
   or a short scene using only observable data. Each time a statement goes
   beyond what a camera would record ("he was annoyed"), ask which rung it
   belongs on.
3. (20 min) **Climb down a real case.** Pick a recent situation the group
   agrees to examine, ideally one in which they formed a quick view of
   another group or a decision. Start at the top: write down the action taken
   and the conclusion behind it, then go down rung by rung. At each rung ask
   "what led to this?" At the bottom, list what was actually observed and
   what other details were available but not picked up.
4. (10 min) **Climb back up differently.** From the same data, build one or
   two other plausible conclusions. Name what would have to be checked, and
   with whom, to tell them apart.
5. (10 min) **Agree on use.** Agree on two or three questions the team will use
   in future discussions ("what did you see or hear?", "how did you get from
   that to your conclusion?", "what else could it mean?") and one next step for
   the case examined, such as asking the other party directly.

For individual use, the same steps take a few minutes: write the conclusion
you are about to act on, then the data under it, and ask how you might be
wrong before you reply or decide.

## Common pitfalls

- Pointing at other people's ladders instead of one's own; the tool works when
  each person offers their own reasoning first
- Arguing about where exactly a statement sits on the ladder; the rungs are a
  prompt for checking, not a classification exercise
- Treating "it's only an assumption" as proof that the conclusion is wrong; a
  quick inference can be right, and the point is to test it
- Collecting only data that supports the original conclusion when climbing
  down, which repeats the reinforcing loop on paper
- Stopping at insight: if nobody goes to ask the colleague or the other
  department what happened, the session changes nothing
