# Appreciative Inquiry

Start change from the moments when things went well, not from the list of what
is broken. People interview one another about peak experiences in their work,
the group distils what made those experiences possible, imagines a future in
which they are the norm, and then designs the concrete steps to get there.

The approach goes back to David Cooperrider and Suresh Srivastva, who argued in
1987 that organisations move in the direction of the questions they ask most
persistently ("Appreciative inquiry in organizational life", *Research in
Organizational Change and Development*, vol. 1). Cooperrider and Diana Whitney
later turned it into the widely used 4-D cycle:

- **Discover** — paired interviews about times the work was at its best, and
  what made them possible
- **Dream** — the group imagines what the organisation would look like if those
  conditions were everywhere
- **Design** — it writes concrete, affirmative statements of how it will
  organise itself to get there
- **Destiny** (sometimes "Deliver") — people volunteer for the initiatives that
  carry the design into everyday work

Many practitioners add a preceding **Define** step to choose the topic, giving a
5-D cycle. The topic is always phrased as something to grow ("exceptional
parent partnerships"), not as a problem to remove ("too many complaints").

## When to use

- A group is stuck in complaint and blame, and further problem analysis would
  only deepen the gloom
- Staff have stopped volunteering ideas because every initiative starts from
  their shortcomings
- After a merger or a conflict, when two groups need to discover what each does
  well before they can build something together
- When a community, school or service wants a vision owned by the people who
  will carry it, not written by a consultant

## When *not* to use

- For an acute failure, a safety incident or a compliance breach: those need a
  root-cause analysis such as [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) or an
  [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md), not a search for strengths
- When people need to be heard about something that has genuinely hurt them.
  Critics point out that a strictly positive frame can push legitimate negative
  voices out of the room (Fitzgerald, Oliver & Hoxsey, 2010; Grant & Humphries,
  2006). If grievances are fresh, address them openly first
- When leadership has already decided the outcome and wants the workshop as
  decoration; participants notice, and trust drops further
- When nobody has the time or authority to follow through on the Destiny step

## Facilitation outline (one-day workshop, about 6 hours)

1. (30 min) **Define.** Introduce the affirmative topic the planning group chose.
   Agree how the day's results will be used, and by whom.
2. (75 min) **Discover.** Pairs, ideally people who rarely work together,
   interview each other for 30 minutes each with three or four prepared
   questions: "Tell me about a time when ... was at its best. What happened?
   What did you, others and the organisation contribute?" Then 15 minutes in
   tables of six to collect the conditions that keep recurring across stories.
3. (60 min) **Dream.** Tables imagine the organisation three to five years
   from now, with those conditions everywhere, and present it in a creative
   form: a newspaper front page, a skit, a drawing. Harvest the common themes.
4. (90 min) **Design.** Groups pick a theme and write one or two
   "provocative propositions": present-tense statements of how the organisation
   works when it is at its best, stretching but grounded in the stories heard.
   Test each one with the plenary.
5. (60 min) **Destiny.** People sign up for initiatives that bring a
   proposition to life. Each initiative leaves with an owner, a first step and
   a date to report back.
6. (15 min) Close with what each person will do differently next week.

A whole-system "AI Summit" runs the same cycle over two to four days with
everyone affected in the room; a single team can compress it into an afternoon
with shorter interviews.

## Common pitfalls

- Banning any mention of problems. The point is to reframe them into what
  people want more of, not to forbid them; a forced cheerfulness breeds cynicism
- Writing interview questions that are positive in tone but generic ("What do
  you like here?"). Good questions ask for one concrete, specific story
- Leaving the Design step at slogans. A proposition nobody could act on is a
  poster, not a design
- Treating the workshop as the change. Without owners, check-ins and visible
  support from leadership, the Destiny initiatives fade within weeks
- Expecting it to be transformational by default. In Bushe and Kassam's review
  of 20 cases, the ones that changed most were those that produced new ideas
  and left people free to act on them, not those with the most uplifting summit
