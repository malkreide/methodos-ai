# Empathy Map

A team takes one person, or one closely defined group, it is about to design
for and sorts everything it knows about them onto a single canvas: what they
**say**, what they **think**, what they **feel** and what they **do**. Many
versions add what the person **sees** and **hears** around them, and two
summary areas for **pains** (fears, frustrations, obstacles) and **gains**
(hopes, what success would mean to them). The point is not the poster but the
conversation: in under an hour, people who have each heard a different part of
the story arrive at one shared picture of how things look from that person's
side, and see where that picture is still thin.

The canvas comes from the visual-thinking consultancy XPLANE. According to the
firm's own account, its creative director Scott Matthews drew large cartoon
heads in the mid-2000s to collect ideas about the people a project was for;
XPLANE's founder Dave Gray turned this into the empathy map published in
*Gamestorming* (Gray, Brown and Macanufo, 2010). Osterwalder and Pigneur
included a version, credited to XPLANE, in *Business Model Generation* the same
year, and Gray later published a revised canvas that starts from the person's
goal. The Nielsen Norman Group describes the four-quadrant form (says, thinks,
does, feels) that most user-experience teams use today. Public bodies such as
Washington State's customer-experience programme recommend it to their staff,
and Cairns et al. (2021) tried it in communication-skills teaching for
first-year medical students.

## When to use

- At the start of a design or improvement project, when the team holds
  interview notes, observations or support requests about a group but has not
  yet looked at them together
- When colleagues talk about the people they serve mainly in terms of their
  own work ("uncooperative", "doesn't read the letter") and need to look at the
  situation from the other side before proposing changes
- When what people say and what they actually do seem not to fit, and the team
  wants to see the mismatch laid out
- As a quick check on how much the team really knows: an area that stays
  nearly empty is a research question
- After each of a few research sessions, one small map per person, as raw
  material that is later combined

## When *not* to use

- When nobody has spoken to or watched the people concerned. A map filled from
  the team's assumptions can be a useful way to surface those assumptions, but
  only if it is labelled as guesswork and followed by real conversations
- When you need a lasting set of profiles that stand for the different user
  groups of a service and guide decisions over months: that is
  [Personas](Personas.md). An empathy map is a quick snapshot of one person or
  group, made in a single session; several maps are often the raw material
  from which personas are built, but they do not replace them
- When the question is what happens step by step over time, across forms,
  offices and channels: draw a [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md).
  The empathy map has no time axis; it shows a state of mind, not a sequence
- To learn what progress someone was trying to make when they started or
  stopped using a service, and what they compared it with: interview for that
  with [Jobs To Be Done](Jobs_To_Be_Done.md)
- To find out whether a form, website or sign actually works for people: watch
  them use it in a [Usability Test](Usability_Testing.md). An empathy map
  describes the person, not whether the design succeeds
- To look at a plan of your own from several angles (dreaming, realism,
  criticism), use the Walt Disney Method; to get peer advice on a personal
  work challenge, Troika Consulting; to set out how an intervention is meant
  to produce its results, a Logic Model

## Facilitation outline (60 min)

Preparation: the research the team already has (interview notes, recordings,
observation notes, support tickets, comment cards), a large sheet or digital
board divided into the areas, and sticky notes. Everyone reads or listens to
part of the material beforehand.

1. (5 min) Name exactly whom the map is about: one participant, or a narrowly
   defined group ("parents enrolling their first child", not "parents"). Write
   at the top what the team wants to understand or decide, for example the
   redesign of an appointment letter.
2. (15 min) Silently and individually, each person writes notes from the
   material, one point per note, and places them in the areas. Says: their own
   words, as quotes. Does: what was observed, including workarounds. Thinks and
   feels: what the material suggests about beliefs and emotions, written so
   that it can be traced back ("worried about losing the benefit: said twice
   she might 'get into trouble'"). Mark anything that is a guess.
3. (15 min) Read the map together. Cluster duplicates, and discuss notes that
   contradict each other or sit oddly between areas. Pay most attention to
   gaps between what the person says and what they do.
4. (10 min) If you use them, fill pains and gains by summarising the clusters:
   what this person fears or struggles with, and what would count as a good
   result for them.
5. (10 min) Agree on two or three insights the team now shares, and list the
   open questions, especially for the areas that stayed empty. Date the map,
   note its sources, and decide whether it feeds the next research round,
   a set of personas or a design session.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with "thinks" and "feels" and writing what the team imagines; begin
  with quotes and observations and infer from them
- Mapping "our users" in general, so that the notes describe nobody in
  particular and contradict each other
- Letting the most senior person fill the canvas while the others watch;
  writing silently first keeps everyone's knowledge on the map
- Treating the finished map as research results rather than as a summary of
  what the team knew on that day, including its blind spots
- Pinning it to the wall and never changing it as new conversations come in
