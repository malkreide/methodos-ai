# Backcasting

Most planning starts from today and asks what comes next. When the present
trend is itself the problem, that question produces plans that improve the
trend slightly and still miss the target. Backcasting reverses the direction:
the group first describes the future state it has committed to, in enough
detail to recognise it, and then works backwards through the decades and years
to ask what must already have happened at each point for that state to be
possible. The output is a path of dated milestones that ends at a short list
of things that have to start now.

```
2040   end state        school buildings run without fossil heating; most pupils
                        come on foot, by bike or public transport
  ^
2035   milestone        last oil and gas boilers replaced; safe cycle routes to all sites
  ^
2030   milestone        heating replaced in half the buildings; mobility plan in every school
  ^
2027   milestone        renovation programme funded; first two pilot schools converted
  ^
now    first steps      energy survey of all buildings; budget request for the pilots;
                        talks with the transport authority about routes and timetables
```

## Origin

John B. Robinson proposed "energy backcasting" in 1982 as a policy-analysis
method for the energy debates of the time: instead of forecasting demand and
building supply to match, analysts described a desirable energy future and
asked which policies would lead to it. He later set out the approach more
generally (Robinson, 1990). In Sweden, Karl-Henrik Robèrt's organisation
The Natural Step and John Holmberg developed a variant that
backcasts from a small set of sustainability principles rather than from a
detailed picture of the future (Holmberg & Robèrt, 2000); The Natural Step
teaches it as a four-stage process: build awareness, assess the baseline
against the principles, generate creative solutions, then devise a plan with
early steps that keep later options open. Dreborg (1996) described when
backcasting is the right choice, and Quist and Vergragt (2006) traced its shift
from expert studies to processes that involve stakeholders. The UK
Government's Futures Toolkit includes it as a workshop tool for policy teams.

## When to use

- A long-term target has been set, by law, by a council vote or by the board,
  and extrapolating current plans clearly will not reach it
- The change needed is structural (buildings, infrastructure, supply chains,
  habits) and some measures take years before they show any effect
- Several parties must contribute and need a shared picture of the path, not
  only of the goal
- After a scenario exercise, to plan the route towards the future the group
  has decided it wants

## When *not* to use

- When the end state is wishful, vague or disputed. Backcasting assumes real
  agreement on where to go; without it the workshop becomes a negotiation of
  the vision, and the resulting plan has no owner
- When the main difficulty is uncertainty about the environment, not the
  choice of destination. Backcasting plans towards one future; to see which
  choices survive several possible futures use
  [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md), ideally before backcasting
- For short-term or operational planning. Within a year or two, forecasting
  from today is cheaper and more accurate; set quarterly results with
  [OKR](OKR.md) or schedule a fixed-date project with the
  [Critical Path Method](Critical_Path_Method.md)
- When you need to explain how an existing programme produces its effect. That
  is a [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md), which also reasons backwards
  but from an outcome to the activities already funded
- When you want to know what a single decision will set off. That is a
  [Futures Wheel](Futures_Wheel.md)
- As a promise of accuracy. Milestones fifteen years out are reasoned guesses;
  most of the published evidence consists of case studies in energy and
  sustainability planning, not controlled comparisons

## Facilitation outline (two workshops, about 5 hours)

Before the first session, collect the agreed target, the relevant baseline
figures and any scenario or vision work that already exists.

**Workshop 1 (about 2.5 hours)**

1. (30 min) Describe the end state. Read out the agreed target and turn it
   into a concrete picture: what would a visitor see, measure or hear in that
   year? Do not reopen *whether* to pursue it; note disagreements for later.
2. (60 min) Name the gaps. For each area (for example buildings, mobility,
   procurement, skills, rules, funding), write how the end state differs from
   today. A checklist such as political, economic, social, technological,
   legal and environmental factors helps avoid blind spots.
3. (60 min) Work backwards. Place the end state at the right of a long
   timeline and today at the left. For each gap, ask: "What must be true five
   years before the end? And five years before that?" Continue until you reach
   the present. Mark events that everything else depends on.

**Workshop 2 (about 2.5 hours)**

4. (30 min) Check the path. Walk the timeline forwards from today. Where does
   a step depend on something that has no earlier step leading to it? Fill
   the gaps.
5. (40 min) Sort by control. Mark each critical event as within your control,
   partly within it, or outside it. For events outside it, name who decides
   and how you could influence them.
6. (50 min) Choose first steps. For the next one to two years, pick the actions
   that the later milestones depend on, favouring steps that keep options open
   and pay back early.
7. (30 min) Agree owners, the first review date and the indicators that will
   show whether the path is on track.

A short version for groups new to the approach: split into two teams, one
building a timeline forwards from today and the other backwards from the end
state, then compare. The points where the two do not meet are the decisions
that need attention.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing a vague end state ("sustainable", "excellent") that cannot be
  recognised when reached, so nothing can be traced back from it
- Letting today's constraints creep back in, so the "backwards" path is the
  old forward plan written in reverse
- Treating the distant milestones as fixed commitments instead of revising
  them as conditions change
- Leaving out what is beyond the group's control because it is uncomfortable;
  those dependencies are often the ones that decide success
- Producing an attractive timeline with no first step that someone owns
