# Cynefin Framework

Classify the situation first, then choose how to act. Most bad plans come from
applying a complicated-domain response to a complex-domain problem.

| Domain | Relationship between cause and effect | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | obvious to everyone | sense → categorise → respond; apply best practice |
| **Complicated** | knowable with expertise | sense → analyse → respond; consult experts, good practice |
| **Complex** | only visible in hindsight | probe → sense → respond; safe-to-fail experiments |
| **Chaotic** | none discernible | act → sense → respond; stabilise first, analyse later |
| **Confused** | you do not know which applies | break the situation into parts and place each one |

## When to use

- A group is stuck because members are solving different kinds of problem
- Someone demands a detailed up-front plan for something genuinely unpredictable
- Early in an incident, to decide whether to stabilise or to diagnose

## When *not* to use

- As a decision technique in itself — it tells you *how* to decide, not what
- Without a facilitator who has run it; the classification debate can swallow the session
- For routine work that is obviously in the Clear domain

## Facilitation outline (2 hours)

1. (15 min) Write the situation, or several situations, one per card.
2. (45 min) Place each card. Expect disagreement — that disagreement *is* the finding.
3. (20 min) For contested cards, split them: parts often belong in different domains.
4. (40 min) Choose the matching response per domain, and name who runs it.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the domains as a maturity ladder to climb rather than as descriptions
- Classifying complex as complicated because a plan is politically expected
- Arguing classification for an hour without ever reaching step 4
- Forgetting that domains shift; a stabilised chaotic situation becomes complex
