# Peer Assist

Before a team starts something that is new to it, it invites a few people who
have already done something comparable elsewhere and spends a structured
working session learning from them. The host team explains what it is about to
do; the visitors ask, discuss among themselves and then tell what worked and
what did not in their own case. The host team listens, keeps the decisions, and
afterwards says what it will change.

BP pioneered its formal use as a management tool, as the "learning before
doing" part of a cycle that also covers learning during the work and after it.
Chris Collison and Geoff Parcell, who worked in BP's knowledge-management team,
described it in *Learning to Fly* (Capstone, 2001; second edition 2004). The
Asian Development Bank and the Overseas Development Institute later published
their own short guides to it.

A peer assist is not a peer review. A review judges a piece of work, is
usually set up by someone else and reports upwards. A peer assist is called by
the team that needs help, which chooses the people it wants to hear, and its
notes go only to that team.

## When to use

- A team is about to start a project, a service or a launch that is new to it,
  and someone in another unit, site or organisation has done something similar
- Early enough that the plan, the budget and the commitments can still change
- When written reports from earlier projects exist but leave out the practical
  detail that only the people involved can give
- As a way to build contacts the team can come back to once the work is running

## When *not* to use

- When nobody has done anything really comparable; then the session produces
  opinions, not experience. Test the plan's assumptions instead, for example
  with a [Pre-Mortem](Pre_Mortem.md)
- When the plan is already decided and the team is looking for endorsement;
  the visitors notice and the session turns into a defence
- When you want to compare figures and performance with other organisations
  rather than hear how a specific piece of work went: see
  [Benchmarking](Benchmarking.md)
- When the work is over and the aim is to learn from what just happened: use an
  [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md)
- When one person is stuck in a difficult case in their daily work: use
  [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md)

## Preparation (two to six weeks before)

1. Write down in a few sentences what the team is about to do and what it
   wants to learn. The aim is to learn, not to get a plan approved.
2. Find out who has done something similar: ask around, look at earlier
   project lists, ask neighbouring organisations. Pick a few people, typically
   four to six (the ADB guide finds more than five makes an in-depth discussion
   hard), from across rather than above you in the hierarchy, and include at
   least one who is likely to see things differently.
3. Agree on a facilitator who is not part of the host team, and on who takes
   notes.
4. Send the visitors a short briefing on the goal, the context and the current
   plan in advance.
5. Set the date early enough that what you learn can still change the plan.

## Facilitation outline (one day, about 6 hours of working time)

Sessions run from half a day to two days depending on how complex the task
is; one day is common. The guides divide the time into roughly four parts. For
a half-day session, halve each block.

1. (30 min) Welcome, introductions and ground rules: the host team is there to
   listen and learn, the visitors to share experience, not to judge. Talk about
   the work, not about people.
2. (60 min) The host team presents its goal, context and plan, briefly. Be
   prepared for the visitors to redefine the problem.
3. (90 min) The visitors ask questions, then discuss among themselves what
   surprised them and what they expected to hear but did not. The host team
   stays in the background and listens; it may even leave the room.
4. (90 min) The visitors work out, still among themselves, what they would
   pay attention to and which options they see, drawing on their own cases.
5. (60 min) The visitors report back: what they have learned, what options
   they see, what worked elsewhere. They tell what happened in their case
   rather than say "you should". The host team asks clarifying questions only.
6. (30 min) The host team thanks the visitors and commits to a date by which
   it will send back what it intends to do differently. Visitors say what they
   take away for their own work.

Afterwards the host team writes up the options and lessons, shares the draft
with the visitors, and keeps it where later teams doing similar work can find
it.

## Common pitfalls

- Holding it too late, when contracts are signed and nothing can change
- Inviting senior people who end up approving or rejecting instead of sharing
- Inviting the same few experts every time, so the ideas stop being fresh
- A host team that presents for half the day and defends every choice
- Visitors handing over a solution instead of options and their own stories
- No follow-up: the host team never says what it changed, and the visitors'
  goodwill is spent
