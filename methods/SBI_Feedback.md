# SBI Feedback Model (Situation, Behavior, Impact)

A three-part way to say one thing to one person about something they did, so
that they can recognise it, understand why it mattered and decide what to do
with it. It replaces labels ("you're careless"), guesses about motives ("you
obviously don't care") and the habit of hiding criticism between two
compliments.

- **Situation**: when and where it happened, as precisely as you can. "In
  Thursday's planning meeting, when we got to the budget" is useful; "lately"
  is not.
- **Behavior**: what the person did that you could see or hear. Their words,
  actions or omissions, described as a camera would have recorded them, with
  no adjectives about their character and no interpretation of why.
- **Impact**: what it caused: your own reaction, what others did next, what
  happened to the work. Speak for yourself ("I stopped following the thread")
  rather than for the whole team unless you know.
- **Intent** (optional fourth step, written SBII): ask what the person was
  trying to achieve, then listen. The effect you saw and the effect they
  wanted are often different, and the conversation about that gap is where
  most of the learning happens.

The model was developed at the Center for Creative Leadership (CCL), a
leadership-development institute in the United States, and is the core of its
short guidebook *Feedback That Works: How to Build and Deliver Your Message*
(first edition 2000, credited to Sloan R. Weitzel; second edition 2019, which
adds a chapter on giving feedback remotely). CCL now teaches the intent
question as a standard extension and markets the model under a trademark; this
entry describes the publicly documented structure only. It is also used outside
the private sector: a performance-management bulletin of the US Army's
installation management command recommends it to supervisors of civilian
staff.

The category here is *facilitation*: SBI gives shape to one short conversation
so that it reaches its purpose. It does not analyse a system or decide
anything, and the decision about what to change stays with the person who
receives the feedback.

### How it differs from its neighbours

- [GROW Model](GROW_Model.md) is a coaching conversation in which the other
  person sets the topic and finds their own way forward. SBI starts from
  something *you* observed and want them to hear. The two combine well: deliver
  the observation with SBI, then use GROW if the person wants to work out what
  to do about it.
- [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) and
  [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) are group formats that look
  back at an event or a period of team work. SBI is between two people and
  about one person's behaviour.

## When to use

- You want to tell someone that a specific thing they did had an effect, good
  or bad, and you notice your draft sentences contain words like "always",
  "attitude" or "unprofessional"
- Praise that has become generic ("great job, everyone") and no longer tells
  anyone what to repeat
- Feedback to a peer or to your own manager, where you have no authority and
  must rely on being clear and fair
- Shortly after the event, while both of you still remember it, and in
  ordinary daily work rather than only at review time
- Preparing written comments, for example after a lesson observation or a
  case review, so that each comment points to something checkable

## When *not* to use

- **Systemic or performance-management problems.** If poor results come from
  workload, unclear roles or missing resources, telling one person about one
  moment puts the problem in the wrong place. If performance has fallen below
  a required standard over time, the organisation's formal performance process
  (with documentation, support and timelines) is the right channel, and an
  informal SBI talk should not stand in for it.
- **Harassment, discrimination, safety violations or other misconduct.** These
  are not matters of "here is the impact on me, what was your intent?". Follow
  the reporting and investigation route; asking the person to explain their
  intent can pressure the affected person and compromise a later procedure.
- **When trust is very low** or the relationship is in open conflict. A
  carefully structured sentence from someone the listener distrusts still
  sounds like an attack; mediation or a third party may be needed first.
- **Patterns rather than incidents.** A single example can trivialise a
  problem that has gone on for months ("it was only one meeting"). If it is a
  pattern, say so, give two or three dated examples and treat it as a
  conversation about the pattern, not as a one-situation remark.
- **When you have not seen it yourself.** Second-hand reports cannot be
  described as observed behaviour; either find out first or say honestly that
  you are passing on what others told you.

## Facilitation outline (one-to-one, about 15 minutes)

1. (Before, 3 min) **Prepare alone.** Write one sentence for each part. Check
   the Behavior line: could a video recording confirm it? Remove every
   adjective about the person and every guess about why they did it. Check the
   Impact line: is it something that actually happened, stated in your own
   name?
2. (1 min) **Open and ask.** Find a private moment soon after the event. Say
   what you want to talk about and ask whether now is a good time.
3. (2 min) **Situation and Behavior.** State when and where, then what you saw
   or heard. Keep it to one example. Stop there briefly so the person can
   place the moment.
4. (2 min) **Impact.** Say what it led to: for you, for others, for the work.
   For appreciation, this is the step that turns "thanks" into something the
   person can repeat on purpose.
5. (5 min) **Intent.** Ask an open question about what they were aiming for,
   for example "What were you hoping would happen at that point?". Listen
   without rebutting. If their intent was reasonable, the conversation moves
   naturally to how they could reach it with a different effect.
6. (2 min) **Close.** For critical feedback, agree whether anything will
   change and, if it matters, when you will look at it again. For
   appreciation, simply stop; do not attach a "but".

Feedback in the other direction or between peers follows the same steps; the
speaker just takes more care at step 2 to ask permission rather than set the
time.

## Common pitfalls

- A judgement disguised as a behaviour: "you were rude" or "you didn't seem
  committed" is an interpretation; describe what was said or done instead
- Impact stated as a threat or a sanction, which turns feedback into a warning
- Stacking five examples to prove the point; the listener defends against the
  pile instead of hearing any one of them
- Skipping the intent question and treating your reading of their motives as
  fact
- Saving everything for the annual review, when nobody remembers the
  situation any more
- Using it only for criticism, so that people learn to brace whenever they hear
  "Situation"
- Wrapping it in the sandwich anyway; the listener stops trusting the praise
  and waits for the "but"
