# Bridges' Transition Model

A change is something that happens to people: two schools become one, a
department is reorganised, someone takes on a new role. A transition is what
people go through inside before they can work in the new situation. The change
can be done on a set date; the transition cannot. This model is a way of
planning for the second, so that the first does not stall in disappointment,
anger and confusion.

```
ending ────────────┐
   (what is lost)   in-between ──────────┐
                      (old gone,           new start
                       new not working)      (committing to the new)

the phases overlap, and different people are in different phases at once
```

## Origin

William Bridges distinguished between change and transition and described
transition in three phases. His book *Managing Transitions: Making the Most of
Change* (first edition 1991) applied the idea to organisations; a revised
fourth edition, written with Susan Bridges, appeared in the UK in 2017. The
website of William Bridges Associates names the phases **Endings**, the
**Neutral Zone** and **New Beginnings**. It describes the neutral zone as the
time when the old is gone but the new is not yet fully working, and argues that
a change only succeeds if leaders deal with the transition people go through.
Many accounts, among them Miller (2017), call the first phase "Letting Go".

## The three phases

1. **Ending, losing, letting go.** Every transition starts with something
   being given up: a team, a building, a title, a way of working, a sense of
   competence. Reactions such as sadness, anger, denial and anxiety are normal.
   The leader's job is to find out who is losing what, say it out loud, be
   clear about what is over and what continues, and treat the past with
   respect rather than running it down to sell the future.
2. **The in-between.** The old arrangement has gone and the new one does not
   work yet. People feel uncertain and disoriented, and work can slow down. It is
   also a period in which new ideas can take hold, because little is fixed. Leaders
   shorten the gap between announcements, set up temporary roles, routines and
   check-ins to carry people through, and keep asking how it is going.
3. **New beginning.** People start to identify with the new situation and put
   energy into it. Guidance used by the University of Manchester's change team
   frames what people need here as four Ps: the **purpose** of the change, a
   **picture** of how it will look, a **plan** of what happens when, and the
   **part** each person plays. Early successes are celebrated, and the
   contribution people made is recognised.

The phases are not a timetable. A management team that planned the merger
for a year may already be at the new beginning while staff hearing about it
for the first time are only at the ending.

## When to use

- A merger, reorganisation, relocation or closure has been decided, and the
  question is how to bring people through it, not whether to do it
- The plan for the change is sound, but people are grieving, withdrawn or
  angry, and managers respond by repeating the benefits
- Someone is taking on a new role, or leaving one, and wants to understand
  why it feels harder than the promotion or move suggests
- In schools, administrations and hospitals, where identity is tied to a
  particular school, office or ward, and a merger takes that away
- Alongside a programme plan, to make sure the human side has owners and dates

## When *not* to use

- To decide whether a change should happen, or to design it. The model starts
  once the decision is made
- To steer an organisation-wide programme from start to finish. It does not
  sequence the leadership work; use
  [Kotter's 8-Step Change Model](Kotter_Eight_Steps.md) for that
- To find out why an agreed change has stalled. Use
  [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md) to name what is holding it back
- When the problem is that people have lost belief in improvement in general,
  rather than grieving a specific loss. [Appreciative Inquiry](Appreciative_Inquiry.md)
  builds from strengths instead
- As a substitute for dealing with real grievances. If people lose jobs, pay,
  status or fair treatment, acknowledging their feelings does not fix that, and
  treating legitimate objections as grief is manipulative
- As proven science. The model is widely taught, but published applications
  are mostly single-site case reports. A study using the framework with young
  people leaving foster care reported encouraging results and stressed that,
  without a comparison group, they cannot be attributed to the framework
  (Nesmith, 2025)

## Facilitation outline (leadership workshop, 2.5 hours)

For the managers who will lead people through the change. Repeat a shorter
version at each milestone. Where the change affects a single person, the same
questions work in a one-to-one conversation.

1. (15 min) State the change in one or two sentences: what is different, from
   when, for whom. Keep this apart from how people will feel about it.
2. (35 min) Losses. For each affected group, list what they are losing:
   colleagues, routines, status, a building, a sense of being good at their
   job. Include losses that look small or irrational from the top. Mark which
   ones are real, material losses that need a material answer.
3. (20 min) What is over and what continues. Write two lists. Be explicit;
   uncertainty about what still counts is a major source of anxiety.
4. (20 min) Marking the ending. Decide how the old will be acknowledged and
   left with respect: a last assembly in the old building, a record of the
   school's history, time for teams to say goodbye.
5. (30 min) The in-between. Agree the temporary arrangements: interim roles,
   a weekly short check-in, a named contact for questions, a way for staff to
   suggest improvements while things are still fluid.
6. (20 min) New beginning. Draft the purpose, picture, plan and part for each
   group. Check that every person will know their own part.
7. (10 min) Owners and dates for each action; agree when to ask staff again
   where they stand.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the announcement as the end of the work; the transition begins there
- Leaders who have already come to terms with the change expecting everyone
  else to be where they are
- Selling the future by criticising the past, which insults people's work
- Rushing through the in-between period, or leaving it empty with no interim
  structure
- Calling every objection "resistance" or "grief" so it need not be answered
- Treating the three phases as stages to tick off on a project plan
