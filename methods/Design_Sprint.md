# Design Sprint

Take one big, contested question, lock the people who own it in a room for a
week, and end the week by watching real people use a convincing fake of the
answer. The sprint trades months of debate for five days of structured work and
one day of evidence.

```
  Mon        Tue         Wed          Thu          Fri
  Map   ->  Sketch  ->  Decide  ->  Prototype ->  Test
  goal,     many        one          a realistic  five one-to-one
  target    solutions   storyboard   facade       interviews
```

## Origin

Jake Knapp began running week-long sprints with product teams at Google around
2010 and refined the format from 2012 at GV (then Google Ventures), the
company's venture arm, together with colleagues such as John Zeratsky and
Braden Kowitz. GV used it with start-ups in its portfolio, and the three set
it out in *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five
Days* (Simon & Schuster, 2016). The format borrows from design thinking,
facilitation practice and usability testing; the reasoning behind testing
with five people comes from usability research, which found that a small round
of observed tests already shows most of the problems a design has (Nielsen,
2000). Shorter three- and four-day variants are now common, and government
teams in the UK, among others, have adapted it for services and policy.

## When to use

- A large, expensive decision about a new product, service or format is about
  to be taken, and the discussion has stalled between competing opinions
- The stakes are high and time is short: building the real thing first would
  take months before anyone could see whether it works
- A team is plainly stuck, and the people who could unblock it have never sat
  down together on the question
- Public sector: before a department commissions a new online service or
  information format, to see how residents or service users react to a
  realistic mock-up. UK government teams have used sprints both for digital
  services and, at the Department for Education, for a careers-guidance idea
  that young people rejected in testing, which saved the budget for something
  they did want
- Education: an education authority or school leadership deciding on a new
  way of informing or enrolling families, with a few parents as Friday's
  testers

## When *not* to use

- The question is small, well understood or cheap to reverse; just decide, or
  try it out in a small trial ([PDCA Cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md))
- Nobody yet knows what the people concerned struggle with. A sprint starts
  from an existing body of knowledge; without it, Monday's map is guesswork.
  Run the research first, for example in the discovery half of a
  [Double Diamond](Double_Diamond.md)
- There is no decision-maker willing to take part, or the core team cannot be
  freed for the full week. A sprint run around people's normal calendars loses
  most of its value
- You need to know whether enough people will want or use the offer. Five
  interviews show comprehension and usability problems, not demand, take-up
  or willingness to pay
- The decision has in fact already been made and the week is meant to bless
  it. That is innovation theatre, and participants will notice
- In public bodies, check in advance whether procurement rules, data
  protection or legal constraints limit who may build the prototype, what may
  be shown to residents and how test participants may be recruited and paid

## Roles

- **Decider**: the person who has authority over the decision (a head of
  department, a product owner, a founder). Takes part in the key moments and
  makes the final calls on Monday and Wednesday.
- **Facilitator**: runs the agenda and the clock, and stays neutral on content.
- **Team**: up to about seven people from different functions, for example
  someone who knows the budget, someone from operations or the front line,
  a designer, someone who can build, and someone who talks to customers.
- **Interviewer**: one person who runs Friday's tests while the others watch.

## Facilitation outline (five days, about 10:00 to 17:00)

Before the week: book the room and the five test participants, clear
calendars, agree the decider, and collect the facts and data that exist.

**Monday: map and target**

1. (1 h) Agree a long-term goal: what should be true in one or two years if
   this works? Then list the questions that could make it fail, phrased as
   questions to be answered ("Will parents trust a message that comes from the
   school rather than the authority?").
2. (1 h) Draw a simple map of how a person moves from first contact to the
   outcome, with the people involved on the left and the end on the right.
3. (2 h) Short interviews with in-house experts (front-line staff, legal,
   finance). Everyone notes opportunities on sticky notes as they listen.
4. (1 h) Cluster the notes, vote, and let the decider pick one target: the
   part of the map the week will focus on.

**Tuesday: sketch**

5. (1 h 30 min) Lightning demos: each person shows existing solutions from
   anywhere, including other sectors, that could be borrowed.
6. (3 h) Sketch individually and silently: notes, rough ideas, rapid
   variations, then one detailed solution sketch in three panels, anonymous
   and self-explanatory. Drawing skill does not matter.

**Wednesday: decide**

7. (2 h) Hang the sketches up, read them silently, mark promising parts with
   dots, discuss each briefly, then hold a straw poll. The decider casts the
   deciding votes.
8. (3 h) Turn the winning ideas into a storyboard of about ten to fifteen
   frames: the exact sequence a test participant will go through.

**Thursday: prototype**

9. (5 h) Build only the surface people will see: clickable screens, a mock
   letter, a brochure, a role-played counter conversation. It should look real
   enough for honest reactions and no more.
10. (1 h) Trial run, then finish the interview guide.

**Friday: test**

11. (5 h) Five one-to-one interviews of about an hour each with people from the
    target group. One person interviews and asks participants to think aloud;
    the rest watch from another room or on a video call and note what they see.
12. (1 h) Look for patterns across the five sessions, check them against the
    Monday questions, and let the decider state what happens next: proceed,
    change course, or stop.

## Common pitfalls

- A decider who appears only on Monday and Friday, then overrules the week
- Treating Friday's five interviews as proof of demand rather than as evidence
  about usability and comprehension
- Picking a target that is too broad to prototype in a single day
- Testing with colleagues or friends instead of people from the actual target
  group
- Polishing the prototype: the more effort it shows, the less honestly
  participants criticise it
- Ending the week without an owner for the next step, so the result fades
  like any other workshop
- Running it remotely without preparation; the UK Department for Work and
  Pensions found that calendar blocking, tool trial runs and daily check-ins
  with the core team were what made the remote version work
