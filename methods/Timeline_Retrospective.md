# Timeline Retrospective

Before a group that worked together over a long period can learn from it, it
has to agree on what actually happened. A timeline retrospective spends the
first part of the session rebuilding that history: participants put events on
one line running from the start of the period to its end, add how each phase
felt to them, and only then step back to look for patterns across the whole
stretch and decide what to keep and what to change.

The timeline exercise, together with a companion "seismograph" drawn along
the same period, goes back to Norman Kerth's *Project Retrospectives: A
Handbook for Team Reviews* (Dorset House, 2001). Esther Derby and Diana Larsen
included a timeline activity in *Agile Retrospectives* (Pragmatic Bookshelf,
2006). Researchers at Lund University
later combined the participants' memories with a timeline prepared in advance
from project records, to reduce the distortions of recall (Bjarnason et al.,
2012).

## When to use

- At the end of a project, programme or school year that lasted many months
- When people joined or left along the way and each knows only part of the story
- When the discussion of "what went wrong" keeps circling around the last few
  weeks and the early phases have been forgotten
- When the group suspects that how people felt at certain points mattered as
  much as the decisions taken, and wants to see both side by side
- At a handover, so that successors understand how the current situation came about

## When *not* to use

- For a single event such as an exercise, an incident or a launch; an
  [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) compares plan and outcome more directly
- For the regular short review at the end of a sprint or a term's routine; use
  [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) or a
  [Sailboat Retrospective](Sailboat_Retrospective.md)
- When one person wants to think through an experience of their own; use
  [Gibbs' Reflective Cycle](Gibbs_Reflective_Cycle.md)
- When the aim is to hear from the people a programme serves what changed in
  their lives; use [Most Significant Change](Most_Significant_Change.md)
- When trust in the group is too low for anyone to mark a bad phase honestly;
  deal with that first

## Preparation

- Agree the period to be covered and who should be in the room. Try to include
  people from every phase, not only those still there at the end.
- Mark the start and end on a long strip of paper or a wide online board, with
  months, terms or project phases as divisions. Add a handful of anchor events
  everybody will recognise (start date, staff changes, holidays, key deadlines).
- Ask participants to look through calendars, minutes, e-mails or the project
  plan beforehand. If you can, add dated facts from those records to the line
  so that memories can be checked against them.

## Facilitation outline (2 hours)

1. (10 min) Open. Explain that the purpose is to understand, not to assign
   blame, and that different memories of the same months are expected and useful.
   Walk the group briefly along the empty line and its anchor events.
2. (30 min) Rebuild the events. Everyone writes the events they remember, one
   per note with an approximate date, and places them on the line. Use one
   colour for events that helped, another for those that hurt, a third for
   neutral ones. Encourage people to start with the phase they know best.
3. (15 min) Walk the line from left to right as a group. Ask the people who
   were there to tell what happened in each phase. Correct dates, merge
   duplicates and fill gaps; note open questions rather than settling them by vote.
4. (15 min) Add the emotional layer. In a band below the events, each person
   draws their own line of energy or satisfaction across the whole period, or
   places a mark per phase. Look at where the lines agree and where they diverge.
5. (25 min) Look for patterns. Ask: Where do several low points cluster, and
   what came before them? Which decisions had effects much later? What
   surprises you? What kept us going in the hard phases? Collect insights on a
   separate sheet.
6. (20 min) Draw conclusions under a few headings: what worked and should not
   be forgotten, what we learned, what we would do differently, and what still
   puzzles us. Choose a small number of actions and give each an owner.
7. (5 min) Close. Agree who photographs or writes up the timeline and where it
   will be kept for the next team.

For very long periods, run steps 2 and 3 phase by phase rather than for the
whole period at once, or spread the session over two meetings.

## Common pitfalls

- Letting the reconstruction run so long that no time is left for patterns and actions
- Treating the finished line as fact; it is a shared memory and may still be wrong
- Allowing the most senior or most vocal person to narrate the whole history
- Forcing people to share feelings they are not comfortable with; offer
  anonymous marks or a phase-by-phase rating instead
- Ending with a long list of observations and no owner for any of them
