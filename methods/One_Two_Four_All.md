# 1-2-4-All

Put one question to the whole group, then widen the conversation in steps:
each person thinks alone, then talks it over with one other person, then two
pairs merge into a four, and only then does the full group hear what came out.
By the time anyone speaks to the room, every participant has already said
something to somebody, and the ideas that arrive in the plenary have been
tested twice on the way.

The structure was developed by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless as one
of the Liberating Structures, a collection of small, rule-bound formats meant
to replace the default meeting patterns (presentation, open discussion, status
report) that let a few people do all the talking. It is the first structure
most people learn from that collection, and it is described in their 2014 book
*The Surprising Power of Liberating Structures*. Teachers will recognise its
close relative think-pair-share, a classroom routine described by Frank Lyman
in the early 1980s; 1-2-4-All adds the group-of-four step, which is what lets
it work with very large groups.

## When to use

- A few people dominate discussion and the rest stay silent or defer to rank
- You want ideas, questions or reactions from everyone, not a decision yet
- Right after a presentation, to turn passive listeners into contributors
- A discussion has stalled or gone round in circles and needs a reset
- Large groups (a conference room, a town-hall meeting, a lecture hall) where
  open discussion would only ever reach a handful of voices

## When *not* to use

- The question has one correct answer, or the decision has already been taken;
  asking for ideas you will ignore costs trust
- You need the group to converge on a choice — follow it with a prioritisation
  method such as [MoSCoW](MoSCoW_Method.md) or a dot vote
- The topic is sensitive enough that people will not speak candidly even to
  one colleague; collect input anonymously instead
- Ideas need to be examined from several angles before judging them — that is
  [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md)

## Facilitation outline (15 min)

The timings follow the Liberating Structures site. They are deliberately
short: keep the clock visible and move on when time is up, not when talk dies
down. A second round on a sharper question is better than a longer first one.

1. (1 min) State the question in one sentence and show it on a slide or
   flipchart. Make it open: "What could we do about…", not "Do you agree that…".
2. (1 min) Everyone writes their own ideas in silence. Hold the silence — this
   minute is what gives quieter and junior people something to say.
3. (2 min) Pairs: each person shares, both build on what they heard, keeping
   distinct ideas distinct rather than merging them too early.
4. (5 min) Two pairs join. The four compare, notice what is similar and what is
   different, and pick the one idea that stands out most.
5. (7 min) Whole group: invite a few fours to share their standout idea, one
   idea each, and ask "who has something nobody has mentioned yet?" rather
   than going round every table. Capture ideas where everyone can see them.

Online, regrouping pairs into fours is fiddly; going from individuals straight
to breakout groups of three, then back to the whole call, keeps most of the
benefit. In a chat, everyone can type one idea and hold it until the facilitator
says "send", so nobody's answer is shaped by the first one posted.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping or shortening the silent minute because it feels awkward
- A closed or vague question, which produces agreement or small talk, not ideas
- Letting the plenary turn into every group reporting everything; ask for one
  idea per group and stop when ideas start repeating
- The facilitator or most senior person joining a pair and steering it
- Collecting the ideas and then doing nothing visible with them
