# Mind Mapping

Write the topic in the middle of a blank sheet turned sideways. Draw a few
thick branches out from it, one for each main strand of the topic, and label
each with a single keyword. Hang thinner branches off those for the details,
and keep going outwards until what you know is on the page. Then step back:
the main branches are the parts of your text, plan or talk, and the empty or
crowded areas show where you know too little or are trying to say too much.

The name and the popular form come from the British author Tony Buzan, who
presented the technique in the BBC television series and book *Use Your Head*
(1974) and later in *The Mind Map Book* (with Barry Buzan, 1993). Buzan's
rules stress colour, images, curved branches and one word per line. Radial
diagrams of knowledge are much older, and his claim to have invented the idea
is disputed. For the purpose described here the decorative rules matter less
than the structure: one centre, a handful of main strands, details beneath.

A close relative is the **concept map**, developed by Joseph Novak's group at
Cornell in the 1970s. A concept map may have several central ideas and writes
a linking phrase on every connection ("requires", "is funded by"), so each
link reads as a statement. That makes it the better tool when the
*relationships* between ideas are what you need to get right; a mind map is
quicker and looser, and is for getting a topic out of your head and into an
order.

## When to use

- Before writing a report, concept paper, application or essay, when you know
  plenty about the topic but cannot see where to start
- When planning a project or an event, to gather every part that has to be
  thought of before you turn it into a schedule or a task list
- When preparing a talk or a lesson, to find the three or four main points and
  what belongs under each
- As a way of taking notes from a lecture, a meeting or a long document that
  keeps the connection of each point to the main subject visible
- With pupils, as a step between "collect what you know" and "write the first
  paragraph"

## When *not* to use

- When the parts of the topic influence each other, so that any tree would
  misrepresent it. A mind map puts every point under one parent; draw a
  concept map with labelled links, or a
  [Causal Loop Diagram](Causal_Loop_Diagram.md) if the effects feed back on
  each other
- When a question has to be split into parts that add up to the whole without
  overlap, so the analysis can be divided between people — that is an
  [Issue Tree](Issue_Tree.md)
- When the cause of a problem is wanted: map candidate causes with an
  [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md)
- When a group needs many new ideas rather than an orderly picture of what one
  person already knows: use [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md), or
  [SCAMPER](SCAMPER.md) to rework something that exists
- When a design depends on combining options across several dimensions: use a
  [Morphological Box](Morphological_Box.md)
- When the structure of a website or handbook should follow how its users
  think, not how its author thinks: [Card Sorting](Card_Sorting.md)
- To decide or prioritise. A finished map shows what there is, not what
  matters most or what to do first
- As a promised shortcut to better memory or learning. The evidence is modest
  and mixed: a small study with medical students (Farrand, Hussain and
  Hennessy, 2002, *Medical Education*) found a limited gain in recall of a
  short text, alongside lower motivation, and the meta-analysis most often
  quoted in this context (Nesbit and Adesope, 2006) concerns concept maps,
  found effects ranging from small to large with marked differences between
  studies, and says nothing directly about Buzan-style mind maps. Claims in
  popular books about unlocking the brain go well beyond this

## Facilitation outline (45 min, alone or with up to six people)

1. (5 min) Write the topic as a short phrase in the centre of a large sheet
   (landscape) or a whiteboard. If it is a task, phrase what has to be
   produced: "concept for the new school library".
2. (15 min) Without sorting, add a branch for every major aspect that comes to
   mind, one keyword each, and immediately hang details off them. Do not edit
   or argue yet; if a point fits nowhere, start a new branch for it. In a
   group, one person draws and the others call out, or everyone adds with
   their own pen.
3. (10 min) Look at the main branches. Merge two that say the same thing,
   split one that is carrying too much, move details to the branch where they
   belong. Aim for roughly three to seven main branches; redraw cleanly if the
   first sheet has become unreadable.
4. (5 min) Mark the gaps: a branch with nothing beneath it, or a question
   nobody can answer, goes on a list of things to find out.
5. (10 min) Number the main branches in the order you will deal with them.
   That numbering is now the outline of the text, the sections of the plan or
   the sequence of the talk.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing sentences on the branches; the map turns into a cramped text and the
  structure disappears again
- Spending the time on colours, icons and drawing instead of on sorting the
  content
- Forcing a point under one branch when it clearly belongs to two; note the
  cross-connection, or switch to a concept map if there are many
- Treating the map as the result. It is the step before the text or the plan,
  and it is finished when the order of work is clear
- Handing someone else your map without explanation and expecting them to read
  it as you do
