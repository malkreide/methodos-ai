# Theory of Change (and Logic Model)

Many programmes can list what they do and what they hope to achieve, but not
what has to happen in between. A theory of change fills that gap: starting
from the long-term outcome, the group works backwards and writes down every
intermediate result that must come first, why each step should lead to the
next, and how anyone would know. The finished picture is a hypothesis about
how change happens, and it is what monitoring and evaluation then test.

```
long-term outcome          pupils read at age-expected level by age 11
   ^  (assumption: ...)
intermediate outcomes      pupils read more fluently; parents read with them at home
   ^  (assumption: ...)
early outcomes             tutored pupils attend regularly; parents know how to help
   ^
activities                 twice-weekly tutoring; three parent evenings
   ^
inputs                     two tutors, trained volunteers, a room, CHF 40,000 a year
```

A **logic model** is the compact, tabular relative: resources (inputs),
activities, outputs, short- and long-term outcomes and impact, read left to
right as a chain of "if this, then that". It is quicker to draw and is what
many funders ask for, but it usually shows *what* is expected without saying
*why* one column should lead to the next. A theory of change spells out those
links and the assumptions behind them. In practice teams often build the
theory of change first and condense it into a logic model for reporting.

## Origin

The term was popularised by the evaluation researcher Carol Weiss. In a 1995
chapter for the Aspen Institute's Roundtable on Comprehensive Community
Initiatives ("Nothing as Practical as Good Theory", in Connell, Kubisch,
Schorr and Weiss (eds.), *New Approaches to Evaluating Community
Initiatives*, Vol. 1) she argued that such initiatives were hard to evaluate
mainly because the assumptions behind them were never stated, and that
programmes which spelled out the expected sequence of outcomes could design
better evaluations and claim credit more credibly. The Aspen Roundtable and
the New York organisation ActKnowledge then turned the idea into a workshop
method based on backwards mapping (Anderson, *The Community Builder's
Approach to Theory of Change*, 2005); ActKnowledge also runs the Center for
Theory of Change.

Logic models are older, with roots in programme evaluation of the 1970s.
The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's *Logic Model Development Guide* (updated
2004) became the standard practical handbook for grantees and is still
widely used by foundations and public funders. Funnell and Rogers'
*Purposeful Program Theory* (2011) treats both as forms of programme theory
and covers how to use them in evaluation.

## When to use

- A programme has run for a while and nobody can say what it is meant to change
- Before applying for funding, when the funder wants to see how activities
  lead to results
- When designing an evaluation, to decide which intermediate results to
  measure and when
- When several partners contribute to one goal and need a shared account of
  how their pieces fit together
- In schools and public services, when a new initiative must justify itself
  to a board, council or inspectorate beyond counting what was delivered

## When *not* to use

- To decide whether the programme is worth its cost compared with
  alternatives; that is [Cost-Benefit Analysis](Cost_Benefit_Analysis.md)
- To plan how leaders bring an organisation through a change; that is
  [Kotter's 8-Step Change Model](Kotter_Eight_Steps.md)
- For a commercial venture whose question is how it earns money; use the
  [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md)
- When the situation is so new that no plausible causal chain can be drawn
  yet. Run small experiments first (see [Cynefin](Cynefin_Framework.md)) and
  write the theory once there is something to base it on
- As a one-off document or a funding formality. Practitioners interviewed for
  a review commissioned by the UK Department for International Development
  warned that once a theory of change is prescribed as a standard deliverable
  it turns into a compliance exercise; its value lies in making assumptions
  explicit and revisiting them as monitoring data arrives (Vogel, 2012)

## Facilitation outline (two half-day workshops)

**Workshop 1: the chain (3–4 h)**

1. (20 min) Agree the long-term outcome in one sentence: who changes, in what
   way, by when. Push back on mission statements and on activities disguised
   as outcomes ("deliver 40 workshops" is not an outcome).
2. (60 min) Work backwards. Ask "what must be true just before this can
   happen?" and write each precondition on a card. Repeat for each card until
   you reach things the programme itself can directly influence.
3. (30 min) Arrange the cards into pathways and draw the arrows. Look for
   outcomes nobody's activity reaches and activities that lead nowhere.
4. (40 min) For each arrow, ask why the step should follow. Write the
   assumption next to it and mark the ones with the weakest evidence.
5. (20 min) Place the current activities against the map. Note gaps and
   activities that do not serve any pathway.

**Workshop 2: evidence and use (3 h)**

6. (45 min) For the key outcomes, define an indicator: what would be observed,
   by whom, how often, and what level counts as success.
7. (45 min) Test the riskiest assumptions: what is known from research or the
   programme's own data? Which ones need checking first?
8. (30 min) Condense the result into a one-page logic model if a funder or
   board needs one.
9. (30 min) Decide when the group will revisit the map and who keeps it up to
   date.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting from the activities and drawing arrows upward to justify them,
  instead of starting from the outcome
- Leaving out the assumptions, which turns the diagram into a list of hopes
- Jumping from activity straight to long-term impact with nothing measurable
  in between
- Writing it alone for the application and never showing it to the staff who
  run the programme
- Claiming the programme caused every change on the map, ignoring other
  influences the theory should name
- Treating the first version as final; the map should change as evidence
  comes in
