# Customer Journey Map

Follow one kind of person through a service from the moment they realise they
need it until they are done with it, and lay out on a single timeline what they
do, whom they deal with, what they think and how they feel at each step. The
organisation sees itself from the outside, across all its departments at once.

The technique grew out of service design, where Stickdorn and Schneider's
*This is Service Design Thinking* (2010) made it a standard tool, and out of
customer-experience research in marketing (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). In the
public sector it is often called a user or citizen journey map. A journey map
looks at the service from the user's side; a *service blueprint* adds the
internal back-office layer underneath it.

## What goes on the map

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Person and goal | One concrete user group and what they are trying to achieve |
| Phases | The broad stages, e.g. find out, apply, wait, receive, follow up |
| Actions | What the person actually does in each phase |
| Touchpoints | Website, form, letter, phone line, counter, office involved |
| Thoughts and feelings | Questions, doubts and the emotional high and low points |
| Pain points and drop-offs | Where people get stuck, repeat themselves or give up |
| Opportunities | Ideas for improvement, each tied to a specific moment |

## When to use

- Each department has optimised its own part, but complaints are about the whole
- People abandon a service halfway, or arrive at the counter unprepared
- Responsibility for a user's experience is split across several offices or
  organisations, e.g. school enrolment involving registry, school and childcare
- Before a redesign or digitisation project, to decide which moments matter most
- To give staff who never meet the end user a shared view of what that user goes through

## When *not* to use

- When the complaint is lead time and you need to know where work waits
  internally; that is [Value Stream Mapping](Value_Stream_Mapping.md)
- To find out *why* a single step fails; follow up with [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md)
  or an [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md)
- To understand what progress people are trying to make before they choose a
  service at all; that is [Jobs To Be Done](Jobs_To_Be_Done.md)
- As the whole design process; a journey map is one research and analysis tool
  inside a larger design cycle, not the cycle itself
- When nobody will talk to or observe real users; a map drawn from internal
  assumptions only confirms what the organisation already believes

## Facilitation outline (half day, after research)

Beforehand: interview or accompany five to ten real users of one group, and
collect complaint data, call-centre logs or drop-off figures where they exist.

1. (20 min) Agree on the person, their goal and where the journey starts and ends.
   One group per map.
2. (40 min) Lay out the phases, then the actions and touchpoints under each, using
   what users actually reported rather than the official procedure.
3. (40 min) Add the thoughts and feelings row from interview notes and quotes. Draw
   the emotional curve and mark the low points.
4. (30 min) Mark each hand-off between offices and each point where people drop
   out or must repeat information. Note which unit owns that moment, if any.
5. (40 min) For the three or four worst moments, collect improvement ideas.
6. (30 min) Pick the moments to tackle first and name an owner for each.
   Check the draft map with a few users before treating it as settled.

## Common pitfalls

- Mapping the procedure as the organisation describes it instead of as people live it
- Mixing several user groups into one average person who does not exist
- Leaving out the emotional row, which is what separates this from a process diagram
- Stopping at a well-designed poster with no owner for the pain points
- Mapping only the digital channel when people also phone, write and queue
