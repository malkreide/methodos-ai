# Benchmarking (Process and Performance Benchmarking)

Hold your own results against those of comparable organisations on a few
carefully defined indicators, find out where the gap is real, and then go and
learn how the stronger performers produce their results. The numbers only tell
you *that* someone does better; the point of the exercise is to find out *how*,
and to adapt that to your own setting.

The approach was formalised at Xerox. Facing Japanese copier makers that sold
at prices Xerox could not match, the company began around 1979 to take
competitors' products and operations apart to see how they did it. Robert C.
Camp, who brought the approach into Xerox's logistics operation in the early
1980s, described it in *Benchmarking: The Search for Industry Best Practices
That Lead to Superior Performance* (1989). In a 1993 interview he insisted on
practices before metrics, and said that most of Xerox's benchmarking by then
looked outside its own industry, because a warehouse learns more about order
picking from materials-handling specialists than from another copier maker.

Two forms are usually distinguished:

- **Performance (or results) benchmarking** compares indicators: cost per case,
  waiting time, length of stay, staff hours per unit. It shows where you stand
  and where the gaps are.
- **Process (or best-practice) benchmarking** studies how the partners work,
  through site visits, interviews and process walk-throughs, to explain a gap
  and find a practice worth adapting.

Most useful projects do both, in that order. Public bodies often run the first
part as a standing *comparison network* (inter-municipal comparison rings,
hospital benchmarking networks), in which members agree definitions once and
exchange figures every year. Research on local government separates the
common exchange of performance statistics from the rarer study of best
practice, and warns against judging one by the other (Ammons and Roenigk,
2015). A study of Norwegian municipal networks found that members do learn,
but how much depends on the network's design and on how far managers and
politicians are involved (Askim, Johnsen and Christophersen, 2008).

## When to use

- You suspect you are slower, costlier or worse than comparable organisations,
  but nobody can say by how much or why
- A council, board or funder asks how your costs or results compare with peers,
  and you want an answer that is fair rather than flattering or damning
- You have already improved what you can see from the inside and want ideas from
  organisations that solved the same problem differently
- Several similar bodies (municipalities, schools, hospitals, branches) are
  willing to share figures and learn from each other over several years

## When *not* to use

- **When the comparison would be apples to oranges.** If the partners differ in
  mandate, population served, what they count as a "case" or what is included in
  "cost", the gap you find says more about the definitions than about the work.
  Agree definitions first and adjust for differences that are out of the
  organisations' control, or do not compare at all.
- **When the figures will be published as a league table or tied to funding.**
  The OECD's review of sub-national benchmarking notes that gaming is more likely
  where comparison is competitive and rewards or sanctions are attached, and
  cites waiting-list targets met at the expense of clinical priority. Writing
  about the star ratings for English hospitals, Bevan and Hood (2006) argued that
  nobody could tell how much of the reported improvement was genuine. This is Campbell's law in
  practice. Keep the learning network separate from the accountability report.
- **When you intend to copy rather than learn.** A practice works in its home
  organisation because of its people, systems and history. Visit, ask what made
  it work, and adapt it; do not transplant it.
- **When no data-sharing agreement is in place.** Partners will not open their
  books without clear rules on what is shared, with whom, how it is anonymised
  and what may be published. Camp describes Xerox masking data through third
  parties. Personal data about staff, patients or pupils needs a legal basis.
- **When the question is your own process, not a comparison.** To see where time
  is lost inside one workflow, map it with [Value Stream Mapping](Value_Stream_Mapping.md).
  To find which of your own problem types dominate, use
  [Pareto Analysis](Pareto_Analysis.md).
- **When you need a steering system, not a one-off comparison.** A
  [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md) sets the organisation's own
  objectives and measures; benchmarking can later supply reference values for
  some of them.

## Facilitation outline (first project, about 8–12 weeks)

1. (Workshop 1, 2 h) Agree the subject. Pick one service or function and
   at most five to eight indicators that matter to the people it serves. Write a
   one-line definition for each, including what is counted and what is not.
2. (Weeks 1–3) Choose partners. Look for organisations with a similar task and
   comparable conditions, plus one or two from outside your sector for functions
   such as billing, scheduling or warehousing. Agree in writing what data is
   exchanged, how it is anonymised, who may see it and what may be published.
3. (Weeks 3–6) Collect the figures on the shared definitions. Check
   them with each partner before comparing; most surprising gaps at this stage
   are definition errors.
4. (Workshop 2, 3 h) Analyse the gaps with the partners present. For each
   gap, ask first whether conditions explain it (caseload, size, geography), then
   which partner does better under similar conditions.
5. (Weeks 7–10) Study the practice. Visit or interview the stronger performer
   with the people who do the work, not only managers. Ask what they do, what
   they stopped doing, and what had to be in place for it to work.
6. (Workshop 3, 2 h) Decide what to adapt. Pick one or two practices, set
   a realistic target and a date to measure again, and test the change on a
   small scale first, for example with a [PDCA cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md).
7. (Next cycle) Repeat the comparison after a year, refining definitions
   as you learn.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with the spreadsheet and never getting to the visit: the figures show
  a gap, only the conversation explains it
- Too many indicators, collected once at great effort and then never used
- Comparing only with organisations you expect to beat, or only with your
  direct competitors
- Treating the leader's figure as a target before you know what it costs to
  reach it
- Senior management not taking part, so findings stay with the analysts
