# Citizens' Assembly

Draw a group of residents by lot, balance it so that it roughly mirrors the
population, and give it a clear question from a public body. Over several days,
often spread across weekends, the members hear experts, affected groups and
advocates of the competing positions, question them, discuss in small
facilitated groups, and finally agree on recommendations. The commissioning
body receives a written report and answers it publicly.

The idea belongs to the family that political scientists call deliberative
mini-publics. Its modern forms appeared independently in the 1970s: Peter C.
Dienel at the University of Wuppertal developed the *Planungszelle* (planning
cell), in which about 25 randomly selected people work on a question for at
least four consecutive days and write a *Bürgergutachten* (citizens' report);
in the United States, Ned Crosby of the Jefferson Center first ran a
citizens' jury in 1974. The larger "citizens' assembly" format became widely
known with the British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform in 2004
(161 members) and in Ireland, where the Convention on the Constitution
(2013–2014) and the Citizens' Assembly (2016–2018) preceded the referendums on
marriage equality in 2015 and on abortion in 2018. Switzerland's first
national example, the Bürger:innenrat für Ernährungspolitik, was convened by
civil-society organisations in 2022: 80 randomly selected residents worked
from June to November on a future food policy and then handed over their
recommendations. The OECD's 2020 report *Innovative Citizen Participation
and New Democratic Institutions* compares a large number of such processes
and groups them into models; German-speaking practice often uses the term *Bürgerrat*.

Size and length vary by model: a citizens' jury or planning cell has roughly
12 to 25 members and meets for a few days; an assembly typically has 50 to
160 members and meets over several weekends or months.

## When to use

- A contested public question where the usual consultation is dominated by
  organised interests and the most vocal residents, and the authority wants to
  know what an informed cross-section of the population would recommend
- Long-term choices with real trade-offs between values (land use, school
  locations, transport, climate, ethical questions), where the benefits fall
  beyond the next election and elected bodies hesitate to decide alone
- A political deadlock in which a recommendation from "people like us" could
  carry more legitimacy with the public than one from officials or experts
- The commissioning body is willing, in writing and in advance, to respond to
  each recommendation and to explain any it does not adopt

## When *not* to use

- Budget and time are short. Recruitment alone takes weeks, members should be
  paid for their time and expenses, and a good process with experts,
  facilitators and documentation takes months and a substantial budget
- Nobody with decision-making power has committed to respond. An assembly
  whose report disappears into a drawer leaves participants and the public
  more cynical than before
- The question is technical and has one correct answer (the load-bearing
  capacity of a bridge, the correct dosage of a drug); ask specialists, for
  example with the [Delphi Method](Delphi_Method.md), which gathers anonymous
  expert judgement rather than lay deliberation
- You need many people to shape an agenda themselves and organise around what
  they care about: [Open Space Technology](Open_Space_Technology.md) works
  with self-selected participants and a self-organised agenda; a
  [World Café](World_Cafe.md) opens up a topic in conversation with a large
  audience but does not produce a recommendation
- The aim is a decision within an existing team or board: use
  [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md) or the
  [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md); a dispute between two
  defined parties calls for [Principled Negotiation](Principled_Negotiation.md)
- You first need to know who is affected and who has influence; a
  [Stakeholder Map](Stakeholder_Map.md) answers that and helps choose the
  witnesses for an assembly, but it does not replace hearing from the public

Be aware of three limits even when the method fits. Random invitation gives
only rough representativeness: those who accept tend to be more interested,
more educated and less busy than those who decline, which stratified
selection corrects only in part. Whoever words the question and selects the
experts shapes the result, so both choices should be made openly, ideally
with an advisory board representing the opposing positions. And the
recommendations are advisory; critics, especially in Germany, argue that a
few dozen people drawn by lot lack democratic accountability and that
facilitators can steer the outcome.

## Facilitation outline (four weekend days, about 40 members)

Larger assemblies repeat the learning and deliberation blocks over several
weekends; a planning cell compresses them into four or five consecutive days.

Before the first session (two to four months):

1. Agree the mandate with the commissioning body: one clearly worded question,
   what the body will do with the report, and by when it will respond. Publish
   both.
2. Appoint an independent organiser and a small advisory board that includes
   people from opposing sides; it checks the question, the briefing material
   and the list of witnesses for balance.
3. Send invitations by lot from the population register (often several
   thousand letters for a few dozen seats). From those who reply, draw the
   members so that the group matches the population on agreed criteria such
   as age, gender, area, education and, if relevant, attitude to the issue.
   Pay members and cover childcare, travel and accessibility needs.

Day 1, learning (about 7 hours):

4. (60 min) Welcome, purpose, mandate and ground rules; members agree how they
   will work together.
5. (4 h) Balanced inputs from experts and affected groups, each followed by
   questions from the members and small-group discussion with a facilitator.
6. (60 min) Members list what they still need to know and whom they want to
   hear.

Day 2, hearing (about 7 hours):

7. (4 h) Advocates of the competing positions and the witnesses the members
   asked for present and answer questions; written submissions from the
   public are summarised.
8. (2 h) Small groups identify the values and trade-offs at stake.

Day 3, deliberation (about 7 hours):

9. (5 h) Small groups, reshuffled during the day, draft and test options
   against the trade-offs they identified; the plenary compares the drafts.
10. (2 h) Members combine and sharpen the drafts into a set of recommendations.

Day 4, decision (about 6 hours):

11. (3 h) Final amendments in plenary; each recommendation is put to a
    secret or open vote, and the result and minority views are recorded.
12. (2 h) Members approve the report text, written with the help of the
    organisers but in their words.
13. (60 min) Formal handover to the commissioning body, ideally in public.

Afterwards:

14. The commissioning body answers each recommendation publicly within the
    agreed time, and the organiser tells members what happened.

## Common pitfalls

- Convening the assembly after the decision has effectively been taken
- A question so broad ("how should our city develop?") that members cannot
  produce concrete recommendations, or so narrow that the answer is foregone
- Witness lists chosen by one department or one side, without an independent
  check for balance
- Unpaid or inconvenient sessions, which leave mostly retirees and the
  already engaged in the room
- Facilitators who steer towards the outcome they expect instead of guarding
  the process
- No plan for communicating the process and the result to the wider public,
  who then see only a small, unknown group making demands
