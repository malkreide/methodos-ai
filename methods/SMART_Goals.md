# SMART Goals

Take one aim that is currently a good intention ("improve communication",
"strengthen cooperation with parents") and rewrite it until it says what will
be different and for whom, how anyone could tell, who answers for it, why it is
within reach, and by when. The acronym is a checklist for that rewrite: the
goal should be **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**ssignable, **R**ealistic and
**T**ime-related.

Those are the five letters as George T. Doran gave them in a two-page article
in *Management Review* in 1981 ("There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's
goals and objectives", vol. 70, no. 11, pp. 35–36), written for managers who
had to put objectives on paper. Later versions changed the letters: the A is
now usually *achievable* or *agreed*, the R *relevant*, the T *time-bound*, and
extended forms such as SMARTER add further criteria. Agree in advance which
version you are using.

The checklist has little research of its own. What stands behind it is the
much larger body of work on goal setting by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, who
summarised several decades of studies in 2002: specific, difficult goals,
combined with commitment and feedback on progress, generally lead to better
performance than vague goals or the instruction to "do your best". The same
research also marks the limits that matter below.

## When to use

- An aim exists, but nobody could say at the review whether it was met
- Responsibility for an aim is assumed rather than stated, so it falls between
  people
- A target agreement in an appraisal or a one-to-one talk has to be checkable by
  both sides a year later
- A funder, a school authority or a programme template asks for objectives with
  a target and a date
- A support plan for a pupil, a client or a patient needs goals the next
  reviewer can check
- An individual wants to turn a resolution into something they can track

## When *not* to use

- **To decide which goals are worth having.** SMART improves the wording of a
  goal you have already chosen. Choosing a few outcomes for a team and following
  them over a quarter is what [OKR](OKR.md) is for; explaining how activities
  are supposed to lead to a long-term effect is the job of a
  [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md); balancing measures across several
  perspectives is a [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md).
- **When what matters cannot honestly be counted.** Trust between a school and
  its parents, the quality of a counselling conversation or a pupil's curiosity
  can be pushed out by whatever is easy to count. Prefer an observable sign
  that people agree on (for example a short review conversation with set
  questions) over a number that measures something else, and say openly that
  part of the goal is judged rather than measured.
- **For complex or unfamiliar work where the way to succeed is not yet known.**
  Locke and Latham themselves report that a fixed performance target can then
  hinder learning, and that a learning goal ("find and try three ways to ...")
  works better until the skills are in place.
- **When targets will be tied to pay or sanctions.** Ordóñez, Schweitzer,
  Galinsky and Bazerman (2009) collect evidence that narrow, demanding goals
  can blind people to everything the goal leaves out, raise risk-taking and
  invite gaming or dishonest reporting. Their "warning label" asks whether a
  goal is too narrow, too hard, set by the right people and on the right time
  horizon. Read it before attaching consequences to a target.
- **For sorting a crowded to-do list.** That is a prioritisation question: use
  the [Eisenhower Matrix](Eisenhower_Matrix.md).
- **When the real problem is who does what across many steps.** A
  [RACI Matrix](RACI_Matrix.md) assigns every step of a process; a
  [Team Charter](Team_Charter.md) settles a new team's purpose and ways of
  working.

## Facilitation outline (60 min, for a group rewriting up to three aims)

1. (5 min) Write each aim as it currently stands, word for word. Agree which
   version of the letters you will use.
2. (10 min) **Specific.** For the first aim ask: what exactly should be
   different, for whom, and where? Replace abstract verbs ("strengthen",
   "improve", "support") with an observable change. If the aim contains two
   changes, split it.
3. (10 min) **Measurable.** Ask: how would an outsider tell, at the review,
   whether it happened? Note the current state as a baseline. If the honest
   answer is a judgement rather than a number, write down who judges and on
   what evidence.
4. (5 min) **Assignable.** Name the one person or unit that answers for the
   aim, and who contributes.
5. (10 min) **Realistic.** Check time, money and people against the target.
   Ask both "is this within reach?" and "is it demanding enough to change
   anything?" Lower or raise the target, or add the missing resources.
6. (5 min) **Time-related.** Set the date by which it should be done and one
   intermediate checkpoint.
7. (10 min) Read the finished sentence aloud and ask: could we reach this and
   still miss what we cared about? If yes, add a second measure that covers
   the gap, or turn the goal into a learning goal. Repeat steps 2–7 for the
   next aim if time allows.
8. (5 min) Put the review date and the checkpoint in the calendar.

A worked example: "strengthen cooperation with parents" might become "By the
end of the school year, every class teacher in years 1–3 has held at least one
individual conversation with the parents of each pupil, and in the June parent
survey the share who agree that they know whom to contact rises from 55% to
75%; the deputy head answers for it and reviews progress at the end of
January."

## Common pitfalls

- Writing the activity ("hold two parent evenings") as the goal, so it can be
  ticked off while nothing changes
- Choosing the measure first because the data already exists, then bending the
  goal to fit it
- Setting so many SMART goals that none receives attention
- Naming a group ("the team", "the school") instead of one person who answers
  for the goal
- Setting the target only for safety, so it is reached without any effort
- Writing the goal once and looking at it again only at the final review
- Treating a missed target as a personal failure rather than as information
  about the goal, the plan or the conditions
