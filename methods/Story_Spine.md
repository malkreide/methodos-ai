# Story Spine

Tell the reasons for a change as a story with eight fixed sentence openings:
*Once upon a time … Every day … But one day … Because of that … Because of
that … Because of that … Until finally … And ever since then …* The openings
force a beginning (the old routine), a break in that routine, a middle made of
consequences that follow from one another, a climax and a new normal. What
comes out is short enough that the people who hear it can tell it again in
their own words.

Kenn Adams, an improviser and playwright, created the Story Spine while working
with Freestyle Repertory Theatre in New York. In a 2013 guest post he dates it
to 1991; in a 2026 radio interview he places it in the late 1980s, when he also
needed an outlining tool for a middle-school creative-writing workshop. It
reached Pixar's writers through the improvisation teacher Rebecca Stockley, and
became widely known when the Pixar story artist Emma Coats tweeted a series of
storytelling tips in 2011, one of them a shortened form of the spine. Those
tweets are often reproduced as "Pixar's 22 rules of storytelling", although
Coats presented them as things she had picked up there, not as studio rules.

## When to use

- A change or project has been announced, but the people who heard the
  announcement cannot explain afterwards why it is happening
- The explanation exists only as slides, figures or a list of measures, with no
  thread connecting the old situation to the new one
- Before a briefing, to check whether the argument has a gap: a "because of
  that" that does not really follow is easy to spot once it is written down
- When many people will have to pass the explanation on, for example team
  leads briefing their own teams or staff explaining it to newcomers
- As a short warm-up for a group that has to present its work and tends to
  start with details instead of a storyline

## When *not* to use

- When the organisation has not yet decided what the change is or why; the
  spine tells a settled account, it does not settle it. Work out the logic first,
  for example with a [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md)
- When several spokespeople need to agree one core statement and its proof
  points for the press and the public — use a [Message House](Message_House.md)
- When a busy decision maker needs the recommendation in the first sentence —
  a story that starts with the old routine delays it; use the
  [Pyramid Principle](Pyramid_Principle.md)
- When you want to learn what the change has meant to the people affected —
  collect their accounts with [Most Significant Change](Most_Significant_Change.md)
  instead of telling them yours
- For painful news such as redundancies, where a storybook frame can sound
  glib; keep the structure if it helps, drop the fairy-tale wording

## Facilitation outline (45 min)

1. (5 min) Show the eight openings on a flipchart. Tell one quick spine about a
   well-known film so the group hears the rhythm; explain that the middle may
   have as many "because of that" lines as the story needs.
2. (10 min) Warm-up in a circle: each person adds the next sentence of an
   invented story, going round until it reaches "and ever since then". Run it
   two or three times. Speed matters more than quality here.
3. (15 min) In pairs or threes, write the spine of the real change. "Once upon
   a time" and "every day" describe the situation as the listeners experienced
   it; "but one day" is the trigger; each "because of that" must follow from
   the line before; "until finally" is the decision or turning point; "and
   ever since then" says what is different now or will be.
4. (10 min) Each group reads its spine aloud. The others listen for a weak
   causal link, jargon, or an ending that claims more than is known. Pick the
   strongest version or merge two.
5. (5 min) Test it: someone who did not write it retells it from memory. Where
   the retelling goes wrong marks the sentence to rewrite. Then decide how the
   spine will be used — as the opening of a briefing, the outline of a memo, or
   a script for team leads.

Working alone, the same steps take about fifteen minutes; the retelling test
needs at least one other person.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping "every day": without the old routine, listeners do not see why the
  change was needed
- Filling the "because of that" lines with a list of measures rather than
  consequences that follow from each other
- Writing the ending the project hopes for and presenting it as what has
  happened
- Keeping "once upon a time" in the final text when the audience will hear it
  as mockery; the openings are scaffolding and can be removed once the story
  holds
- Polishing a single version for an hour instead of drafting several quickly
  and keeping the best
