# Brainwriting 6-3-5

Six people sit around a table, each with a sheet of paper ruled into three
columns and six rows. In the first few minutes everyone writes three ideas on
the question into the top row. Then every sheet moves one place to the left,
and each person reads what the previous writer put down and adds three more
ideas in the next row: new ones, variations, or developments of what is
already there. After six rounds every sheet is full and each person has worked
on every sheet. Nobody has spoken, and the group holds up to 108 written ideas,
many of them built on each other.

The name is the recipe: six participants, three ideas each, five minutes per
round. The technique was devised by the German marketing consultant Bernd
Rohrbach and published in 1969 in the trade journal *Absatzwirtschaft* under
the title "Kreativ nach Regeln – Methode 635". It belongs to a wider family of
*brainwriting* techniques, in which ideas are written rather than called out.
Their rationale is supported by later research on group brainstorming: in a
series of experiments, Diehl and Stroebe (1987) found that the main reason
spoken brainstorming groups produce fewer ideas than the same number of people
working alone is *production blocking* — only one person can talk at a time,
so the others wait, forget, or drop what they had in mind. When everyone
writes at once, nobody has to wait.

## When to use

- The question is clear and specific, and you need many possible answers to
  it before choosing: uses for a space, ways to raise participation, design
  alternatives for one component
- Spoken idea rounds keep stalling after a few suggestions, or every new idea
  is debated the moment it is voiced
- You want people to build on each other's ideas, not just list their own
- You need a written record of all ideas, for example to show later where a
  proposal came from
- The group is mixed in rank or confidence and you want the ideas, not the
  speakers, to carry weight

## When *not* to use

- The question itself is still unclear or disputed. Brainwriting multiplies
  answers to whatever question is on the sheet; if that question is wrong, you
  get a hundred answers to the wrong question. Frame the problem first, for
  example with the first diamond of [Double Diamond](Double_Diamond.md)
- You need to evaluate, rank or decide. The method stops at a pile of
  options; follow it with clustering and a dot vote, a
  [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md), or examine the
  shortlisted ideas with [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md)
- You want a quick contribution from everyone in a large room and a sense of
  which ideas they find strongest — [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) does that
  in a quarter of an hour
- The aim is conversation and shared understanding rather than a list of
  ideas: [World Café](World_Cafe.md), [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md) or
  [Fishbowl](Fishbowl.md) are discussion formats
- The problem is mainly technical or factual and needs analysis, not
  imagination

The numbers six, three and five are a convention, not a rule. Groups of four
to eight work the same way with more or fewer rows; complex questions need
longer rounds than simple ones, and the later rounds usually need more time
than the first because there is more to read.

## Facilitation outline (about 40 min)

1. (5 min) Write the question on a flipchart and read it out. Make it concrete
   and open: "How could we use the free room on the second floor next year?"
   rather than "Ideas for the school". Answer questions about the question, not
   about possible solutions.
2. (2 min) Hand out the forms: three columns, six rows, the question printed
   at the top. Explain the only rules: one idea per box, write legibly, short
   phrases or quick sketches, no comments on other people's ideas.
3. (30 min) Six rounds of three to five minutes each. In the first round each
   person fills the top row with three ideas. Then all sheets move one place;
   each person reads everything on the sheet in front of them and fills the
   next row, either with fresh ideas or by extending, combining or reversing
   what is above. Call time, pass, repeat. Allow an extra minute or two in the
   later rounds.
4. (5 min) Collect the sheets and let everyone read through them, or lay them
   out on a table. Do not start evaluating in the same breath; mark only
   duplicates and ideas someone needs explained.

Afterwards, cluster the ideas and decide how to choose among them — in the
same session or a separate one. With more than about eight people, run
several circles in parallel and merge their sheets. Online, a shared
spreadsheet with one tab per person, rotated every round, replaces the paper;
run asynchronously, each person adds a row within a set day before the next
one picks it up.

## Common pitfalls

- A broad or loaded question ("How do we fix communication?"), which produces
  slogans instead of usable ideas
- Rounds that are too short for people to read what is already on the sheet,
  so later rows ignore earlier ones and simply repeat
- Allowing whispered comments or discussion during the rounds, which brings
  back the judgement the method is meant to suspend
- Treating the number of ideas as the result; the value lies in the few that
  are worth taking further
- Collecting the sheets and never coming back to them
