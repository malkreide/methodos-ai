# Issue Tree (Logic Tree)

Write the big question at the left of the page. Split it into a handful of
smaller questions that, taken together, answer it; then split each of those
again, until every question at the right-hand edge is small enough for one
piece of analysis to settle. The test at every split is the one usually
abbreviated as MECE: the branches should not overlap (*mutually exclusive*),
and together they should leave nothing out (*collectively exhaustive*).

The no-overlap, no-gap rule for grouping ideas is associated with Barbara
Minto, who developed it at McKinsey & Company in the late 1960s and made it
part of her *Pyramid Principle* (first published 1987). Consulting firms turned it
into a working tool for structuring problems, and it is now also taught to
civil servants as a way of planning policy analysis. Two variants are common:

- a **diagnostic tree** breaks down a *why* question ("why have costs risen?")
  into the possible places the explanation could lie;
- a **solution tree** breaks down a *how* question ("how could we halve early
  school leaving?") into the possible routes to the goal.

Once the team has a working answer, the same structure can carry hypotheses:
each branch states a claim that the evidence gathered for it will confirm or
reject.

## When to use

- At the start of a piece of analysis, when the question is large and vague and
  the team is jumping between favourite explanations or solutions
- When several people must split the work without duplicating or missing parts
- When a sponsor needs to see, and agree, how a question will be tackled before
  time and money go into answering it
- When a quantity can be broken into parts that add up, such as costs, demand
  or caseload, which makes the no-gap test straightforward

## When *not* to use

- For one concrete failure that keeps recurring: follow its causal chain with
  [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md), or map parallel contributing causes with an
  [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md)
- When the causes feed back into each other, as with most entrenched social
  problems (poverty, school disengagement, staff turnover). A tree places each
  cause under exactly one parent and hides the loops; draw a
  [Causal Loop Diagram](Causal_Loop_Diagram.md) instead or alongside
- When the task is to explain how a programme's activities are supposed to
  produce its intended effects — that is a [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md)
- When the group disagrees about what kind of problem it faces at all; settle
  that first with the [Cynefin framework](Cynefin_Framework.md)
- When no clean split exists and the team keeps redrawing the top level. Accept
  "mostly MECE", note the overlaps, and move on to the evidence; a perfect tree
  that is never tested is analysis paralysis

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

1. (15 min) Agree the one question the tree must answer. Write it as a full
   question, specific about scope and time ("how can the canton reduce the share
   of 18-year-olds without a qualification from 9% to 5% by 2030?"). Check it
   with whoever commissioned the work; a tree built on the wrong question is
   wasted.
2. (10 min) Decide whether it is a *why* or a *how* question, and keep every
   branch answering the same kind of question.
3. (25 min) Draft the first split, then the second. Look for a split that adds
   up — by segment, by stage, by formula (cost = volume × unit cost), or by
   internal versus external. Try two or three alternative first splits before
   choosing one.
4. (15 min) Test each split: does any branch overlap with its neighbour? Is
   anything that could answer the parent question missing? Add an "other"
   branch rather than pretend the list is complete.
5. (15 min) For each right-hand question, note what evidence would answer it
   and how hard that is to get. Mark the branches the team thinks most likely,
   so the analysis starts there.
6. (10 min) Give every end question an owner and a date. The tree is now the
   work plan.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting from a favourite solution and building the tree backwards to justify it
- Mixing levels: one branch says "staff costs", its neighbour says "the new IT
  system", which also contains staff costs
- Splitting so finely that the tree becomes a list of fifty questions nobody owns
- Treating the tree as the answer; it is a plan for finding one
- Never revisiting the structure when early evidence shows a branch was framed
  wrongly
