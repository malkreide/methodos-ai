# Johari Window

A simple model of what a group knows about each of its members, and a
facilitated exercise built on it. Picture a window with four panes, formed by
two questions: does *I* know this about myself, and do *the others* know it?

| | Known to me | Not known to me |
|---|---|---|
| **Known to others** | **Open**: what I and the others both see: my role, my visible habits, what I have said about myself | **Blind**: what others notice about me that I do not: how my tone lands, a habit I am unaware of, a strength I underrate |
| **Not known to others** | **Hidden**: what I know and have not shared: a worry, a preference, a constraint at home, how I like to receive information | **Unknown**: what neither side knows yet, for instance how I would react in a situation none of us has faced |

The panes are not fixed. Two moves change their size. When I tell others
something about myself (*disclosure*), part of the hidden pane becomes open.
When others tell me how they see me and I listen (*feedback*), part of the blind
pane becomes open. Over time, and with both moves, a group can also stumble on
things that were in nobody's view. The working assumption behind the exercise is
that a team whose members share a larger open area misread each other less
often and can rely on each other more.

The model was drawn up by the American psychologists Joseph Luft and Harrington
Ingham, who presented it in 1955 in the proceedings of the Western Training
Laboratory in Group Development at the University of California, Los Angeles;
the name joins their first names, Joe and Harry. Luft later set it out at book
length in *Of Human Interaction* (1969). It belongs to the group-dynamics and
training-group tradition of that period and is now used widely in team
development, leadership courses, nursing and medical education. A guidance
sheet from CapitalNurse, a programme sponsored by Health Education England and
NHS England, presents it as a tool for personal development and feedback and
notes that a blame culture or a closed management style makes people shrink
their open pane rather than enlarge it.

A popular variant, often called the Johari adjective exercise, gives everyone
the same list of positive or neutral adjectives (lists of fifty-odd words are in
circulation). Each person picks a handful that describe themselves, and each
colleague picks a handful for that person. Words chosen by both land in the
open pane, words chosen only by others in the blind pane, words chosen only by
the person in the hidden pane. The variant is easy to run but is a later
classroom device; the original model does not prescribe a word list, and many
facilitators prefer open questions instead (see the outline below).

The category here is *facilitation*: the window structures a conversation in
which a group learns about its members. It does not analyse a process,
evaluate a piece of work or decide anything.

### How it differs from its neighbours

- [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md) shapes *one* message from one person to
  another about one observed moment. The Johari Window is a group setting in
  which many people both disclose and receive perceptions, with no single
  incident at its centre. SBI is a good discipline for the feedback round of a
  Johari session.
- [GROW Model](GROW_Model.md) is a coaching conversation in which one person
  works out their own next step on a topic they choose. It assumes the topic is
  known; the Johari Window is for finding out what others see that you do not.
- [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) and
  [Sailboat Retrospective](Sailboat_Retrospective.md) review *how the team
  works*: routines, obstacles, goals. The Johari Window is about how members
  *perceive one another* as people and colleagues, and produces no process
  changes on its own.
- [Appreciative Inquiry](Appreciative_Inquiry.md) builds an organisation's
  future on its best experiences; it is a change process, not an exercise in
  mutual perception.
- [Bridges Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md) deals with loss and
  uncertainty after a decided change; a Johari session may help a merged team
  get to know each other, but it does not address the transition itself.

## When to use

- A team has just been formed, for example from several departments or
  organisations, and people know each other only by role and are already
  guessing at each other's motives
- A long-standing team where the same misreadings recur ("she is always
  curt", "he never commits") and nobody has ever checked them with the person
- A new manager who wants to learn how they come across and to tell the team
  how they work, early, before assumptions settle
- Leadership courses, teacher training and supervision groups that will work
  together for weeks or months and need a shared language for giving and
  asking for perceptions
- As a recurring frame rather than a one-off: groups that return to the
  window every few months can see whether their open area is actually growing

## When *not* to use

- **Without psychological safety.** The exercise asks people to make
  themselves visible. Where mistakes are punished, colleagues compete for
  promotion, or someone has been burned for speaking up, disclosure stays at
  the level of hobbies, or it becomes ammunition. Build trust in smaller ways
  first, or keep the session to self-reflection without any sharing.
- **Across steep power differences.** A manager, assessor or supervisor in the
  room changes what people are willing to say and what they can safely hear.
  Run it among peers, let the manager take part as an equal only if the team
  agrees, and never use the results in performance appraisal.
- **As therapy or conflict resolution.** It is a team-development exercise,
  not a clinical intervention. Personal histories, mental-health matters and
  open conflicts between two people need a qualified counsellor, a mediator or
  a formal process, not a group round.
- **When the real problem is structural.** If misunderstandings come from
  unclear roles, contradictory goals or overload, better mutual knowledge will
  not fix them; clarify responsibilities first.
- **When people did not choose to be there.** Compulsory disclosure produces
  polite fiction. Participation in each round has to be voluntary, and "pass"
  has to be an acceptable answer.
- **When you need evidence of effect.** The model is a heuristic. A 1983 study
  that classified 160 students as "open" or "hidden" with a Johari instrument
  found no difference in self-esteem or communication anxiety, and its author
  concluded the window was more useful as a teaching device than as a research
  tool. Present it as a way of thinking, not as a validated assessment.
- **If the adjective version would feel like a game.** Experienced or
  sceptical groups often find picking words from a list artificial, and an
  all-positive list rarely touches what actually causes friction. Use the open
  question version instead.

## Facilitation outline (team of 5–8, about 2 hours)

1. (Before) **Check the preconditions.** Talk to the team lead and, if
   possible, a few members. Is anyone in a formal assessment relationship with
   others in the group? Is there an open conflict? If so, postpone or change
   the format. Agree that nothing said is used outside the room or in
   appraisals.
2. (10 min) **Agree the ground rules.** Participation in every round is
   voluntary; anyone may pass. Perceptions are offered as "I see / I
   experience", not as facts about the person. What is said stays in the room.
3. (15 min) **Introduce the window.** Draw the four panes and explain the two
   moves, disclosure and feedback, with a neutral example. Make clear that a
   large blind or hidden pane is normal, not a failing.
4. (20 min) **Self-reflection, alone.** Each person writes down: two or three
   things about how they like to work, what helps them and what irritates them
   that colleagues may not know (candidates for disclosure); and two questions
   they would like the others to answer about how they come across.
5. (25 min) **Disclosure round.** Each person shares what they choose from
   their list. Others listen and may ask a question of understanding, but do
   not comment or reassure. The facilitator watches the time and keeps the
   level of disclosure similar across people so that nobody is exposed alone.
6. (35 min) **Feedback round.** In pairs or trios, or with each person in turn
   in the full group, colleagues answer the questions the person asked, using
   specific observations rather than character labels. The person receiving
   listens, may ask for an example, and thanks rather than defends. (In the
   adjective variant, this is where the lists are compared and sorted into the
   panes.)
7. (10 min) **Individual reflection.** Everyone notes what moved from hidden or
   blind into open for them, and one thing they want colleagues to know or to
   do from now on.
8. (5 min) **Close.** A short round: one sentence each about what they take
   away. Agree whether and when the group will come back to the window, for
   example in three months.

For remote teams, run steps 4 and 7 in a private document and steps 5 and 6
in small breakout rooms; a full-group video call makes disclosure harder.

## Common pitfalls

- Opening with the feedback round before anyone has disclosed anything, so the
  session starts with people being told how they are seen
- One person disclosing a lot while others offer safe trivia, leaving the
  first exposed; match the depth across the group
- Feedback that is really a verdict ("you're controlling") rather than an
  observation of what the person said or did
- The manager taking part as an evaluator, or quoting the session later in a
  performance review
- Treating the four panes as a personality type ("I'm a hidden-area person")
  instead of a snapshot of one group at one time
- Running it once as an away-day activity and never returning to it, so the
  open area shrinks back as soon as the team is under pressure
- Facilitating it as group therapy: probing personal histories or emotions the
  person did not offer
