# Pairwise Comparison (Paired Comparison Analysis)

Put a list in order by never asking anyone to rank the whole list. Instead,
take two items at a time and ask one question: "of these two, which matters
more?" Do this for every possible pair, count how many comparisons each item
wins, and sort by the count. People who cannot hold ten items in their heads at
once can still judge two, and the tally assembles those small judgements into a
full order.

The idea has a long history in measurement. L. L. Thurstone set out a
scientific basis for scaling things by comparing them two at a time in his
"law of comparative judgment" (*Psychological Review*, 1927), and pairwise
judgements are the core input of Thomas Saaty's Analytic Hierarchy Process,
developed in the 1970s, which uses them to derive weights for decision criteria.
The simple tally grid described here is the everyday management form of the
same idea.

## When to use

- A list of roughly six to fifteen items has to be put in order and attempts
  to rank it in one go keep ending where they started
- Decision criteria need relative weights before options are scored, and nobody
  can name the percentages but everyone can say which of two criteria counts more
- The items are of the same kind and can be judged against one shared question
  ("more important for our pupils", "more urgent for residents")
- You want to see how close items are, not only which comes first
- A single person faces a ranking they keep changing their mind about

## When *not* to use

- With more than about fifteen items: the number of pairs, n × (n − 1) / 2,
  becomes tiring. Sort coarsely first (for example with the
  [MoSCoW Method](MoSCoW_Method.md)) and compare only the top group
- When the task is to choose one option against several criteria; that is a
  [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md). Pairwise comparison
  can feed it the criteria weights
- When the ranking should rest on estimated numbers such as reach and effort;
  use [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md)
- When the group first has to generate the items and every member's voice must
  count equally; use the [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md)
- When the real question is urgent versus important in one person's workload;
  use the [Eisenhower Matrix](Eisenhower_Matrix.md)

## Facilitation outline (60 min, ten items)

1. (10 min) Agree the list and the single comparison question. Merge items that
   overlap; give each a letter. Write the question where everyone can see it.
2. (5 min) Draw a grid with the items as rows and columns. Shade the diagonal
   and the lower half, so each pair appears exactly once (45 cells for ten
   items).
3. (25 min) Everyone fills in the grid alone, writing the letter of the winner
   in each cell. Optionally add a strength from 0 (no difference) to 3 (much
   more important). No discussion yet: individual judgements keep a strong
   voice from deciding every pair.
4. (10 min) Count each item's wins (or add up its strength points) per person,
   then sum across the group. Sort the list by total and, if the result will
   serve as weights, convert each total to a share of the grand total.
5. (10 min) Look at the result together. Discuss only the pairs where the group
   split sharply and the places where neighbouring items are nearly tied.
   Note any circular results (A over B, B over C, C over A); they usually mean
   the question was read in two different ways.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting without one agreed question, so some people judge importance and
  others urgency or ease
- Letting the grid become a debate about every pair; decide alone, discuss the
  splits
- Treating small differences in the total as meaningful; neighbours a point
  apart are effectively tied
- Mixing items of very different size or kind ("hire a teacher" against
  "redesign the curriculum") and expecting a sensible order
- Adding items afterwards without redoing the comparisons they affect
- Reading the final percentages as precise measurements rather than a summary
  of judgements
