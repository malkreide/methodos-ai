# Ansoff Matrix

A two-by-two grid for sorting the ways an organisation can grow. One axis asks
whether the offering is one you already provide or a new one; the other asks
whether the people you would serve are those you already reach or new ones.
The four cells give four growth directions, and the further a direction sits
from "existing offering, existing audience", the less of what you already know
carries over and the higher the risk.

H. Igor Ansoff set out the four product-market alternatives in "Strategies for
Diversification" (*Harvard Business Review* 35(5), 1957, pp. 113–124) and
developed them in *Corporate Strategy* (1965). The grid was written for
manufacturing firms, but it reads well outside business once "product" becomes
*offering* (a course, a service, a way to give) and "market" becomes *audience*
(learners, residents, donors, funders).

## The four directions

| | Existing audience | New audience |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing offering** | **Market penetration**: more of the same for the people you already serve | **Market development**: take what you already do to new groups or places |
| **New offering** | **Product development**: something new for the people you already serve | **Diversification**: something new for people you do not yet serve |

Risk rises roughly from top-left to bottom-right. Penetration builds on
known people and proven offerings. Market development and product
development each keep one of the two familiar. Diversification keeps neither,
which is why it carries the most risk. It also has degrees: a move close to
your existing skills (related) is much safer than one with no connection at
all (unrelated).

Translations that work outside business:

- **Adult-education centre**: fill existing courses (penetration); offer them
  to retirees or newcomers (market development); new course formats for
  regular learners (product development); run a coworking space
  (diversification).
- **Library**: reactivate lapsed members; take existing services to
  under-served districts; add new services for current users; enter an
  unrelated field.
- **Charity fundraising**: ask current donors to give more; find new donor
  groups for existing appeals; new ways for current supporters to give; a
  funding source the charity has never used, such as lottery money or a gala
  for major donors (The Management Centre's adaptation).

## When to use

- Leadership agrees it must grow or reach more people but argues past each
  other about *how*, because each person is picturing a different cell
- A long list of expansion ideas needs sorting before anyone evaluates them
- A board wants to see how much of the proposed growth rests on things the
  organisation has never done
- A public or nonprofit service has a mandate to extend its reach and needs
  to tell its funders which kind of extension it is attempting

## When *not* to use

- To decide whether a path is worth pursuing. The grid only sorts options; it
  says nothing about demand, need, cost or fit. Pair each candidate with real
  evidence: a needs assessment, enrolment or usage data, donor research
- When growth is not the goal. A public body serving a defined population
  well, or a team that needs to consolidate, gains little from a framework
  that assumes expansion
- When the real question is the competitive pressure in a field. That is
  [Porter's Five Forces](Porters_Five_Forces.md)
- When you need an honest picture of your own strengths and weaknesses first.
  That is [SWOT](SWOT.md), and it is a common input to this grid
- When the future itself is uncertain and you need options that survive
  several possible outcomes. That is [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md)
- When "new" is the whole debate. A course in a slightly different format and
  an entirely new subject both count as "new offering"; if the disagreement is
  about degrees of novelty, the two-by-two hides exactly what you need to
  discuss. Some strategy textbooks redraw the axes as continuous scales for
  this reason

## Facilitation outline (2 hours)

1. (10 min) State what "growth" means here: revenue, enrolment, members,
   reach in a target group, donations. Write it down. Without this, the
   cells get filled with ideas pursuing different goals.
2. (15 min) Define "existing" precisely. Who exactly is the current audience?
   Which offerings count as current? Disagreement here is common and useful.
3. (30 min) Each person writes growth options silently on cards, one per card,
   then places each card on the grid. Leave cards that sit between cells on
   the line, rather than forcing a choice.
4. (25 min) For each cell, discuss what the organisation would need that it
   does not have today: skills, staff, partners, money, permission. Note this
   beside the cards. It is the practical meaning of "risk".
5. (25 min) Shortlist two or three options across different cells. For each,
   name the evidence you would need before committing and who will gather it.
6. (15 min) Agree how the shortlisted options will be evaluated, for example
   with a [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md) once the
   evidence is in.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the grid as a recommendation. A low-risk cell is not a good cell
  if the existing audience is shrinking or already fully served
- Calling an audience "new" when it is the same people reached through a new
  channel, or calling an offering "new" when it is a rebrand. Both understate
  the risk of genuinely new moves
- Skipping the capability question. Market development often fails because
  the new group needs a different offering after all, which quietly turns it
  into diversification
- Filling only the diversification cell because it is the most exciting, or
  only the penetration cell because it is the most comfortable
- Forgetting that growth in one cell can draw staff and money away from the
  core service

## How it differs from its neighbours

- [SWOT](SWOT.md) takes stock of where you stand; this grid lays out where you
  could grow. A SWOT often feeds it
- [Porter's Five Forces](Porters_Five_Forces.md) judges how attractive and
  contested a field is; this grid does not look at rivals at all
- [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md) describes how one venture
  creates and captures value; use it to work out a direction once the grid has
  helped choose it
- [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md) tests choices against several
  possible futures; this grid assumes today's picture of audiences and
  offerings
- [Wardley Mapping](Wardley_Mapping.md) asks which components to build, buy or
  outsource; it is about how you deliver, not where you grow
- [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md) steers and measures a strategy
  already chosen; this grid sits earlier, among the options
