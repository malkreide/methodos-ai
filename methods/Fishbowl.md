# Fishbowl

Set a small circle of chairs in the middle of the room and a larger ring of
chairs around it. A handful of people in the inner circle hold the actual
conversation; everyone else sits outside, listens and, depending on the
variant, steps in. The audience watches an argument unfold between people
who talk to one another, not to the room, and the host keeps the boundary
between the two circles open enough that the conversation does not belong to
the few who started it.

The format has no single inventor and has long been used in classrooms,
conferences and participatory meetings. Two variants are common. In the
**open** fishbowl one chair in the inner circle is left empty: anyone from the
audience may sit in it and join the discussion, and one of the current
speakers then gives up a seat so that a chair is free again. In the
**closed** fishbowl the inner circle is fixed for a round; after an agreed
time the group leaves, and another set of people takes its place. The
facilitation collection *Gamestorming*, building on Sam Kaner's
*Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making*, describes a closed
version in which half the group discusses while the other half observes and
then the roles swap. Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless include a variant
in their Liberating Structures, *User Experience Fishbowl*, in which people
who have already tackled a shared challenge talk about their experience in
the middle while the outer circle prepares questions for them.

Fishbowl is about letting many people follow a deep conversation among a few.
That is the opposite trade-off from formats that put everyone in small
groups at once.

## When to use

- A contested or sensitive question must be discussed in front of a large
  group (a staff assembly, a parents' forum, a public meeting) and both a
  podium panel and an open floor have failed before
- You want the audience to see how people with different positions reason,
  not just to hear their conclusions
- A conference panel should become a real conversation that the audience can
  join
- Colleagues who already tried something new should share their experience
  with those about to adopt it (the Liberating Structures variant)
- In a classroom, to teach discussion skills: one group talks, the other
  observes how the argument develops, then they swap

## When *not* to use

- Every person in the room should contribute an idea within a short time:
  [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) does that and Fishbowl does not
- A large group should talk among itself in many small conversations and mix
  across tables: run a [World Café](World_Cafe.md)
- A small team wants to set its own agenda for a recurring meeting:
  [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md)
- The group needs to reach a decision; follow the fishbowl with
  [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md) or another decision
  step
- The conflict is so personal or heated that putting people on show would
  harm them; private mediation comes first
- Experts should give independent judgements without influencing each other:
  that is a [Delphi](Delphi_Method.md) question, not a public conversation

## Facilitation outline (about 75 minutes, open fishbowl)

1. (Before) Frame one open question that people genuinely disagree about.
   Invite three to five people for the first inner circle who hold different
   views and who are willing to listen as well as speak. Arrange four to six
   chairs in the centre, one of them empty, with the audience around them. In a
   large hall, put a hand microphone on each inner chair.
2. (5 min) Welcome. Explain the rules: only people in the inner circle speak;
   speakers talk to each other, not to the audience; no prepared speeches;
   anyone who wants to contribute takes the empty chair, and whoever has said
   their piece then returns to the audience so that one chair stays free.
3. (30–40 min) Conversation. The host opens with the question and then mostly
   watches the chairs: invites someone waiting in the empty seat in, asks a
   long-sitting speaker to make room, and summarises briefly if the thread
   gets lost. Keep at least one seat free at all times.
4. (10 min) Audience reflection. Outer-circle participants turn to two or
   three neighbours and note what they heard, what surprised them and what
   question is still open.
5. (10–15 min) Questions back to the circle, or a last round in which the
   empty chair is open to everyone who has not yet been inside.
6. (5–10 min) Close. The host names the main points of agreement and
   disagreement and says what happens next with them.

**Closed variant (classroom or training, about 45 minutes):** split the group
in two. The first half discusses for about fifteen minutes while the second
half observes and takes notes, for instance on how arguments are built or who
is listened to; then they swap; finish with a debrief on what each side
noticed from inside and from outside.

**Online:** put the inner circle on camera and ask the audience to turn their
cameras off; audience members "take the chair" by raising a hand and being
promoted to speaker, and questions can collect in the chat.

## Common pitfalls

- An inner circle that agrees with itself, chosen to send a message; the
  audience recognises a staged debate at once
- Speakers who address the audience or deliver statements instead of
  responding to each other
- No one leaving the inner circle, so the empty chair stays taken and the
  audience gives up
- Expecting quiet or junior people to walk into the spotlight; offer
  other routes as well, such as written questions or a short neighbour talk
  before the round
- Ending without a summary, so the conversation leaves no trace and people
  leave unsure what was decided (usually: nothing yet)
