# Open Space Technology

Gather everyone who cares about a complex, pressing issue in one large circle,
name a single guiding question, and leave the programme empty. Anyone who
wants to take responsibility for a topic writes it on a sheet, announces it to
the circle and pins it to a wall, choosing a time slot and a place. Once the
wall is full, people sign up for the sessions they want to join, and the event
dissolves into many parallel working groups that run themselves. Each group
writes down what it discussed and what it proposes. By the end, the
collected reports form the record of the event, and on longer events the
group uses them to agree on which strands to pursue and who will carry them
forward.

Harrison Owen, an American organisational consultant and former Episcopal
priest, developed the format in the mid-1980s; the Open Space community dates
it to 1985. As the story is usually told, the starting point was the
feedback, after he had organised a conventional conference, that the coffee
breaks had been the most useful part. He described the method
in *Open Space Technology: A User's Guide* (third edition 2008) and its
background in *Expanding Our Now: The Story of Open Space Technology* (1997);
an earlier short version of the guide is freely available in the Harrison Owen
library on the community website openspaceworld.org. Owen died in 2024.
Practitioners adapt the format freely, and many "unconference" events borrow
its structure. This entry describes the method in its own words.

## When to use

Owen's guide and the practitioner community name the same conditions: the
issue is complex, the people involved are diverse, feelings run high enough
that conflict is possible, and the matter is urgent. In practice that means:

- A whole system has to move at once (a school community preparing a major
  change, all staff of an administration facing a new way of working, a
  network of organisations that need to coordinate) and no planning group
  could guess what people need to talk about
- Those affected hold knowledge the organisers lack, and the aim is to bring
  it out and turn it into initiatives that people will actually own
- You can give the process at least five hours, better one or two full days,
  without interruptions
- The sponsor is prepared to say in advance what support people can count on
  and to act on what comes out

## When *not* to use

- The decision has already been taken, or the person in charge believes they
  know the answer. Owen is explicit that the format is counterproductive when
  the destination and the steps to it are already clear; using it as a
  consultation ritual destroys trust
- Leadership is not willing to let go of control over the topics or accept
  results it did not foresee
- There is no real, urgent question that people care about, or attendance is
  compulsory and people would rather be elsewhere; the format runs on the
  energy participants bring
- You need a single decision, a ranked list or a costed plan from a small
  group by the end of the afternoon; use a decision or prioritisation method
  instead, or follow the event with one
- A few dozen people on two or three questions that are already known: a
  [World Café](World_Cafe.md) is simpler and shorter
- A small team's regular meeting with topics chosen on the spot: use
  [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md)
- Nobody has been made responsible for what happens afterwards. The event
  creates momentum that turns into disappointment if the reports disappear

## Facilitation outline (one day, about 7 hours)

Owen's brief guide treats about five hours as the minimum and describes
events of one to three days: a single day allows substantial exchange, a
second day firmer conclusions, and longer events suit planning and design
work. The timings below are a typical one-day shape, not a fixed script.

1. (Before) Agree the guiding question with the sponsor: short, open and
   inviting, such as "How do we make our school a good place to learn all day
   long?". Clarify what the sponsor commits to and what is out of scope.
   Invite everyone affected, on a voluntary basis. Prepare a room large enough
   for one circle of chairs, a long blank wall laid out as a grid of time
   slots and breakout locations, plenty of sheets and markers, separate
   breakout spaces, and a way to collect session reports (paper templates or
   a few computers in a corner that practitioners often call the "news
   room").
2. (45–60 min) Opening in the circle. The sponsor welcomes people and states
   the question and its commitments in a few minutes. The facilitator explains
   how the day works and introduces the ground rules (see below). Then anyone
   who wishes steps into the centre, writes a topic and their name on a sheet,
   announces it in one sentence and posts it on the wall in a free slot.
3. (15–30 min) Signing up. Everyone walks along the wall and writes their name
   on the sessions they intend to join. Conveners may merge or move sessions
   by agreement.
4. (3 × 60–90 min) Session rounds. Groups meet at the posted times and places.
   The convener opens the topic and makes sure a short report is written: who
   took part, what was discussed, what is proposed and who will follow up.
   Reports go to the news room as soon as a session ends. Meals and breaks run
   alongside rather than stopping everything.
5. (Optional, longer events) Morning announcements and an evening news circle
   for anything people want to share. Many practitioners close a multi-day
   event with a convergence step, in which participants read the collected
   reports, mark the issues they most want to pursue and form action groups
   around them.
6. (30–45 min) Closing circle. Anyone may say what they are taking away or
   what they commit to. No presentations from the groups.
7. (Afterwards) Send the full set of reports to all participants promptly,
   ideally the same day, and tell them who owns which follow-up and when they
   will hear what became of it.

### The ground rules

Owen's four principles, in his own short wording, are: *whoever comes is the
right people*; *whatever happens is the only thing that could have*; *whenever
it starts is the right time*; and *when it's over, it's over*. Many
practitioners add a fifth, *wherever it happens is the right place*. They
release people from worrying about who is missing and about the clock.

The single "law" is the law of two feet (also called the law of mobility):
anyone who is neither learning nor contributing where they are should go
somewhere they can. Hosts traditionally give playful names to those who use
it: "bumblebees", who move between groups and carry ideas with them, and
"butterflies", who join no group but often spark useful conversations
elsewhere. The law also takes the sting out of people who dominate a session,
since others can simply leave.

Practitioners also run the format online, with a shared board in place of the
wall and video breakout rooms in place of the session spaces; long days on
screen are tiring, so plan shorter blocks spread over more days.

## Common pitfalls

- A theme that is really a disguised announcement ("How do we implement the
  decision already taken?"); participants see through it at once
- A sponsor who hovers in the sessions, steers the topics or requests a
  presentation of results to management; the ownership the format creates
  depends on the sponsor staying out of the way
- Inviting only those who will agree, so that the people with the most at
  stake are missing
- Too many slots for too few people, so that sessions are empty and energy
  drops
- Sessions with no written report; whatever is not recorded is lost
- No follow-up: the reports are filed, nobody hears from the action groups,
  and the next invitation is met with cynicism
- Expecting proof of impact the method cannot give. Owen and the practitioner
  community report many successful cases, and a survey of 471 participants in
  five Italian events linked respectful dialogue and visible results to
  willingness to take part again (Mannarini & Talò, 2013), but controlled
  studies of outcomes are lacking

## See also

- [World Café](World_Cafe.md) — a large group in small, rotating table
  conversations on two or three questions set in advance; shorter and more
  tightly hosted
- [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md) — a small group choosing and timing its own
  discussion topics within a single meeting
- [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) — every voice on one question in a quarter
  of an hour; useful inside a session or for the closing
- [Fishbowl](Fishbowl.md) — a few people discuss a contested issue in front of
  a large audience who can step in
- [Appreciative Inquiry](Appreciative_Inquiry.md) — a whole-system change
  process that starts from people's best experiences; its summits sometimes
  use open, self-organised sessions
- [Design Sprint](Design_Sprint.md) — a small, hand-picked team testing one
  product idea in a week; the opposite of open participation
