# Glasl's Nine Stages of Conflict Escalation

A diagnosis aid for a conflict that has already run for some time. Before
anyone intervenes, the person responsible works out how far the dispute has
travelled, because the help that fits a sharp disagreement about the work is
useless once the parties are trying to harm each other, and heavy-handed help
applied too early can make a manageable dispute worse.

The model comes from the Austrian economist and organisation consultant
Friedrich Glasl, who first set it out in *Konfliktmanagement* (Haupt, Bern,
1980; 13th edition 2024, subtitled *Ein Handbuch für Führung, Beratung und
Mediation*). He presented it in English as "The Process of Conflict
Escalation and Roles of Third Parties", in Bomers and Peterson (eds.),
*Conflict Management and Industrial Relations* (Springer Netherlands, 1982,
pp. 119–140). For people who want to work on an early-stage conflict
themselves, Glasl wrote a companion book, *Selbsthilfe in Konflikten*
(Verlag Freies Geistesleben).

## The nine stages

Glasl describes escalation as a movement *downwards*: each stage narrows what
the parties can still see and do. The stages fall into three groups of three,
separated by thresholds that are hard to cross back over.

| Group | Stage | What you observe |
|---|---|---|
| Both can still win | 1 Hardening | Standpoints stiffen; friction, but people still talk to each other and believe it can be settled |
| | 2 Debate and polemics | Arguments become a contest of who is right; black-and-white thinking, point-scoring in meetings |
| | 3 Actions instead of words | Talking is seen as pointless; one side acts on its own and presents the other with a done deal; empathy fades |
| One must lose | 4 Images and coalitions | Each side builds a fixed picture of the other and recruits supporters; the original issue matters less than winning |
| | 5 Loss of face | Public attacks on the other's integrity; the opponent is "unmasked" as morally bad, and trust collapses |
| | 6 Threat strategies | Ultimatums and threats backed by demonstrations of power; each side tries to force the other's hand |
| Both lose | 7 Limited destructive blows | The other is no longer seen as a person; damaging them counts as a gain even at a cost to oneself |
| | 8 Fragmentation | The aim is to break the other side's power base and cohesion |
| | 9 Together into the abyss | Destroying the opponent is worth one's own ruin |

The first threshold (between 3 and 4) is where the conflict stops being about
the matter at hand and becomes about the people; the second (between 6 and 7)
is where moral restraint gives way.

## Stage and kind of help

Glasl pairs overlapping bands of stages with strategies for a third party.
The bands are guidance, not rules:

| Stages | Fitting help |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | The parties themselves, or moderation by a manager or colleague |
| 3–5 | Process consultation: a neutral helper works on perceptions and the relationship |
| 4–6 | Socio-therapeutic (systemic) process support for deeper patterns in groups |
| 5–7 | Classic mediation by a professional the parties accept |
| 6–8 | Arbitration or a court: a third party decides on the substance |
| 7–9 | Power intervention: a higher authority imposes a solution and protects those affected |

## When to use

- A dispute between people, teams or a team and its management has gone on for
  a while and someone has to decide what kind of help to bring in
- A complaint lands with HR, a school authority or a board, and they need to
  tell a heated disagreement from a feud before choosing a procedure
- A mediator or coach preparing a first contact wants a working hypothesis of
  where the conflict stands
- A leader suspects a disagreement is turning personal and wants to know what
  to watch for

## When *not* to use

- To resolve the conflict: the model only tells you where you are. For the
  talks themselves, see [Principled Negotiation](Principled_Negotiation.md)
- After a single incident in which someone was clearly hurt and needs amends —
  a [Restorative Circle](Restorative_Circle.md) is built for that
- When the question is who holds influence over a change rather than how bad a
  dispute is — use a [Stakeholder Map](Stakeholder_Map.md)
- Where there is harassment, violence or a safeguarding concern: those trigger
  legal and protective duties whatever stage you would place them at
- As a label to hand to the parties ("you are at stage 5"); it is a working
  tool for the person deciding on help, not a verdict

## Facilitation outline (60 min, for the person or small team deciding on help)

1. (10 min) Collect observations, not opinions: who talks to whom and how, what
   has been said or done in the last weeks, who has joined which side. Get
   accounts from more than one party where you can.
2. (15 min) Walk through the stages from the top. For each, ask whether its
   signs are present. The stage reached is the lowest one where several signs
   clearly apply, not the worst single incident.
3. (10 min) Check the two thresholds explicitly: is it still about the matter,
   or already about the people? Are threats being made, or has anyone tried to
   cause real damage?
4. (15 min) Read off the band of fitting help. Discuss who could provide it,
   whether both parties would accept that person, and what happens if nothing
   is done.
5. (10 min) Decide the first step and when you will re-assess. Conflicts move;
   a stage is a snapshot.

## Common pitfalls

- Taking one party's story as the diagnosis; the side that asks for help
  usually places the other further down the scale
- Rating the conflict by its loudest moment instead of by the pattern
- Sending a well-meaning colleague to moderate a conflict that is already at
  threats and ultimatums; a failed attempt drives it further down
- Treating "it is only stage 2" as a reason to wait; the early stages are where
  the parties can still help themselves
- Announcing the stage to the parties, which they will hear as a judgment
