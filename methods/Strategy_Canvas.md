# Strategy Canvas and ERRC Grid (Blue Ocean)

Draw, on one chart, the factors a field competes on and how much each provider
offers on every one of them. When the lines for you and your competitors run
almost parallel, the field has converged: everyone is offering the same thing
in slightly different amounts, and the contest is about price and extras.
The second step is to draw a different line on purpose, by deciding which
factors to **eliminate**, which to **reduce**, which to **raise** and which to
**create**.

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne of INSEAD introduced the strategy canvas in
*Harvard Business Review* (2002) and made it the central tool of *Blue Ocean
Strategy* (2005, expanded edition 2015). The canvas has two axes:

| Axis | Shows |
|---|---|
| Horizontal | The factors the field competes on and invests in: price, range, speed, service, guarantees, location, and whatever else buyers are offered |
| Vertical | The offering level buyers receive on each factor, from low to high |

Joining a provider's levels gives its *value curve* (also called its strategic
profile). The four actions are usually written up as an
Eliminate–Reduce–Raise–Create (ERRC) grid:

| Action | Question |
|---|---|
| Eliminate | Which factors that the field takes for granted could be dropped altogether? |
| Reduce | Which factors could be brought well below the usual level? |
| Raise | Which factors should go well above the usual level? |
| Create | Which factors has nobody in the field offered yet? |

The cuts pay for the additions. A grid that only raises and creates usually
produces a more expensive version of the same offer. The authors suggest
three checks on the new curve: it should have *focus* (it does not try to
excel at everything), *divergence* (its shape differs from the others') and a
*tagline* that can be stated in a sentence and is true.

The approach is not limited to firms. In Malaysia's National Blue Ocean
Strategy, public-service teams plot an as-is curve from surveys of people who
use a service and people who do not, then use the four actions to draw a
to-be curve; Xavier (2019) describes a hospital pharmacy service redesigned in
this way so that patients could receive their medicines by post.

## When to use

- Providers in a field look interchangeable to buyers and compete mainly on
  price and on adding more of the same features
- A team is split between "we are too expensive" and "we need more features"
  and has no shared picture of how its offer compares, factor by factor
- Before redesigning an offer or a service, to question which customary
  features people actually value
- When most potential users buy from nobody in the field and the question is
  what keeps them away
- A public service wants to compare itself with the alternatives people use
  instead (private agents, doing nothing, another channel) and decide what to
  stop doing as well as what to add

## When *not* to use

- To judge how attractive or profitable a whole sector is, or how much power
  suppliers and buyers hold, use [Porter's Five Forces](Porters_Five_Forces.md)
- To test which of your own resources and capabilities others would find hard
  to match, use [VRIO Analysis](VRIO_Analysis.md); the canvas looks at the
  offer as buyers see it, not at what lies behind it
- For a first broad stocktake of an organisation's position, use
  [SWOT](SWOT.md)
- To learn how others do comparable work more efficiently, use
  [Benchmarking](Benchmarking.md); the canvas compares offers, not processes
  or costs
- To choose between growing with existing or new offers in existing or new
  markets, use the [Ansoff Matrix](Ansoff_Matrix.md)
- To find out which single features buyers merely expect and which delight
  them, survey them with the [Kano Model](Kano_Model.md); its results can feed
  the offering levels on a canvas
- To lay out the whole logic of how an offer is delivered and paid for, use
  the [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md); to decide what to
  build, buy or outsource among the components behind a service, use
  [Wardley Mapping](Wardley_Mapping.md)
- Where the offer is fixed in detail by law or contract and there is no
  alternative people could turn to, the canvas has little to vary

## Facilitation outline (4 hours, plus field work)

1. (20 min) **Frame.** Agree the offer to be examined and whom it is for.
   Name the main competitors *and* the alternatives buyers use instead,
   including doing without.
2. (40 min) **List factors.** Collect the factors the field competes on, in
   the buyers' language, not in internal jargon. Keep 6–12, each one
   something a buyer could notice.
3. (40 min) **Draw the as-is canvas.** Rate your offer and two or three
   others on each factor (low to high) and join the points. Use survey or
   interview data where it exists; mark ratings that are only the group's
   opinion.
4. (Between sessions) **Check in the field.** Ask buyers, former buyers and
   people who buy from nobody what they value, what they put up with and what
   puts them off. Revise the factors and ratings.
5. (60 min) **Fill the ERRC grid.** Go through the factors one by one and put
   each in one of the four boxes or leave it unchanged. Then ask what new
   factor would matter to the non-buyers. Insist on at least one entry in
   Eliminate or Reduce.
6. (40 min) **Draw the to-be curve** on the same chart. Test it for focus,
   divergence and a one-sentence tagline. If it runs parallel to the others,
   go back to step 5.
7. (20 min) **Decide next steps.** Name what has to be true for the new
   profile to work (costs, delivery, demand) and how to test it cheaply with
   real buyers before committing. Assign owners.

## Common pitfalls

- Listing factors as the organisation sees them ("our methodology") instead
  of as buyers experience them
- Rating your own offer generously and the others harshly without data
- Only adding: a grid with nothing in Eliminate or Reduce is a price increase
- Looking only at direct competitors and existing customers, which reproduces
  the same curve
- Treating the drawn curve as a strategy; it is a hypothesis about what
  buyers will value and has to be tested
- Polishing the chart. Its value is the discussion, not the precision of the
  points

## Limits worth knowing

The strategy canvas is widely taught and easy to use, but the evidence behind
the wider Blue Ocean approach is thin. Critics note that the book's cases
were selected and interpreted after the fact, that failed attempts at the
same strategy are not counted, and that many of its ideas restate older work
on differentiation and new market space (see the criticism summarised on
Wikipedia). Burke, van Stel and Thurik (2009) note that the earlier support
was almost entirely case studies and give one of the few statistical tests,
on Dutch retail data: they find some support for creating new market space,
but also that it overlaps with competing in existing markets rather than
being an either/or choice (summarised for managers in *HBR*, 2010). Madsen and Slåtten (2019) describe Blue Ocean Strategy as a
management fashion, promoted heavily by its authors, whose actual use by
organisations is less documented than its popularity suggests. Treat the
canvas as a structured way to question an offer, not as proof that a new one
will succeed.
