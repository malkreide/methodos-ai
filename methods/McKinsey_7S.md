# McKinsey 7-S Framework

Describe an organisation through seven elements that depend on each other,
then check whether they fit together. The point is not the seven lists but the
comparison between them: a strategy that needs cooperation, paid for by a
bonus system that rewards individual results and led by managers who decide
alone, will not be carried out however good the strategy document is.

The seven elements, usually drawn as a ring around shared values:

| Element | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** | What the organisation has decided to do to reach its aims, and what it has decided not to do |
| **Structure** | How work is divided up and coordinated: units, reporting lines, who decides what |
| **Systems** | The formal and informal procedures that run the place: budgeting, planning, reporting, pay and promotion, IT, quality control |
| **Shared values** | What people in the organisation actually hold to be important, as opposed to what the mission statement says |
| **Style** | How senior people behave in practice: where they spend their time, how they decide, what they pay attention to |
| **Staff** | Who works there: numbers, profiles, how people are recruited, developed and moved |
| **Skills** | What the organisation as a whole is distinctively good at, and what it cannot yet do |

Strategy, structure and systems are often called the "hard" elements because
they are written down and easy to change on paper; the other four are the
"soft" ones, harder to see and slower to move. The framework's central claim
is that the soft elements matter at least as much, and that changing one
element without the others produces friction rather than change.

The framework was set out by Robert H. Waterman Jr., Thomas J. Peters and
Julien R. Phillips, then consultants at McKinsey & Company, in the article
"Structure is not organization" (*Business Horizons*, 1980). Peters recalls
that Richard Pascale and Anthony Athos took part in the working sessions that
produced it; Pascale and Athos used it in *The Art of Japanese Management*
(1981), and Peters and Waterman built *In Search of Excellence* (1982)
around it. The German literature knows it as the *7-S-Modell*, with the hard
elements sometimes called the "cold triangle" and the soft ones the "warm
square".

## The evidence base

Be clear about what this is. The 7-S framework is a consulting heuristic: a
checklist of what to look at and a reminder that the parts interact. It was
derived from consultants' experience with large companies, not from a tested
theory, and the core claim that fit between the elements causes better
performance has had little rigorous empirical testing. Published applications
mostly use the seven headings to sort survey or interview findings. A
cross-sectional survey of 249 physicians in Warsaw public hospitals, for
instance, grouped questionnaire items by the seven elements and found the
soft elements rated lowest (Chmielewska et al., 2022); that shows the
framework works as a sorting frame, not that it predicts outcomes. Use it to
structure a diagnosis and a conversation, and treat its conclusions as
hypotheses to check.

## When to use

- A new strategy, structure or ownership has been decided, and you want to
  find out before or soon after implementation which other parts of the
  organisation still work the old way
- Before or after a merger or takeover, to compare how two organisations are
  set up and where their ways of working will clash
- A change was implemented on paper some time ago, results have not come, and
  you suspect that only one or two elements were changed
- An organisation has grown or shrunk a lot and its procedures, leadership
  habits and people no longer match what it now does

## When *not* to use

- To decide *what* the strategy should be, or to scan the outside world —
  use [SWOT](SWOT.md) or other strategy tools first; 7-S takes the strategy as
  given and checks the organisation against it
- To plan the sequence of a large change programme and keep it going — that
  is [Kotter's Eight Steps](Kotter_Eight_Steps.md)
- When the problem is that individuals have not taken up a change they were
  trained for — [ADKAR](ADKAR_Model.md) looks at one person or group at a time
- When people are grieving what a merger or reorganisation has taken from them
  — the [Bridges Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md) deals with that
  human side, which 7-S names but does not work on
- To build a regular steering report with measures — that is the
  [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md)
- For one stalled change where you need to weigh what pushes and what blocks it
  — a [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md) is quicker

## Facilitation outline (preparation plus a 3½-hour workshop)

**Preparation (1–2 weeks).** A small team gathers evidence for each element:
organisation charts, budget and pay rules, job profiles and recruitment data,
and a handful of interviews with staff at several levels, asking for concrete
examples ("what happened the last time someone disagreed with a manager?",
"who got promoted last year, and why?"). Evidence of what people actually do
matters more than official descriptions, above all for style and shared
values.

**Workshop (3½ hours, 6–15 people, including some who are not senior).**

1. (15 min) State the trigger and the intended direction in two or three
   sentences: the new strategy, the merger, the growth. This is what fit is
   measured against.
2. (50 min) Current state. Go through the seven elements one by one, starting
   with the hard three. Write each as a few concrete statements backed by the
   preparation evidence. For style and shared values, insist on examples of
   behaviour, not adjectives.
3. (35 min) Intended state. For each element, what would it have to look like
   to support the intended direction? Mark the elements where the gap between
   current and intended state is large.
4. (50 min) Fit check. Draw a 7×7 grid (21 pairs). For each pair ask: does
   this element support, ignore or work against that one, now? Mark the
   contradictions in red. Typical finds: a structure that demands cooperation
   and a system that rewards individual targets; a strategy that needs new
   skills and a staff policy that hires the old profile; stated values that the
   leadership style contradicts daily.
5. (40 min) Pick the three to five contradictions that matter most for the
   intended direction. For each, decide which element has to move, what the
   first concrete change is, and what it depends on. Expect the soft elements
   to need the longest time.
6. (20 min) Owners, a date to repeat the fit check, and what evidence would
   show that a contradiction has been resolved.

For a merger, run steps 2 and 4 for both organisations side by side before
step 3, so that the intended state is not simply the larger partner's current
one.

## Common pitfalls

- Filling in seven separate lists and skipping the fit check, which is where
  the value lies
- Describing the official values and the leadership style as written in the
  handbook rather than as staff experience them
- Letting only the top team describe style; people rarely see their own
  leadership habits accurately
- Changing the hard elements because they are quick (a new organisation chart,
  a new reporting line) and declaring the job done
- Arguing for an hour about whether something is a skill or a staff matter;
  the label matters less than spotting the contradiction
- Treating perfect alignment as the goal: an organisation tightly aligned to
  yesterday's strategy is hard to change
- Presenting the result as proven diagnosis; it is a structured judgement and
  should be checked against what happens next
