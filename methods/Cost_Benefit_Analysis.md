# Cost-Benefit Analysis

Put every cost and every benefit in money, over a stated horizon, discounted to
present value. The discipline is in what you are forced to admit you cannot price.

## When to use

- Capital expenditure, procurement, or infrastructure choices
- Anything a budget holder or auditor will ask you to justify with a number
- Comparing options with very different timing of costs and returns

## When *not* to use

- When the dominant benefits genuinely resist pricing — the model will quietly
  weight them at zero and produce a confident wrong answer
- Early exploration, where forecasts are invented rather than estimated
- As cover for a decision already taken; the assumptions will show it
- When the benefits cannot be priced but the options deliver the same outcome:
  compare costs alone (cost-effectiveness analysis) instead of inventing prices

## Facilitation outline (1–5 days)

1. Define the option set, including a business-as-usual baseline: what happens
   if current arrangements simply continue. Carrying on usually still costs
   money (maintenance, staff, contracts), so it is not the same as spending nothing.
2. List costs across the full life: build, run, train, migrate, decommission.
3. List benefits, and mark each one priced, proxied, or unpriceable.
4. Agree the horizon and the discount rate *before* computing anything. In
   public-sector work the rate is usually prescribed by the finance ministry
   (in the UK, the Green Book's social time preference rate); use it rather than
   choosing one.
5. Strip out general inflation, then adjust cost, benefit and schedule
   estimates for optimism bias; early estimates are reliably too rosy.
6. Compute net present value per option (and, where a funder expects it, the
   benefit-cost ratio).
7. Run sensitivity analysis. Report which single assumption flips the ranking —
   that sentence is usually more decision-relevant than the NPV itself.
8. Report the unpriceable benefits alongside the number, never inside it, and
   say who bears the costs and who receives the benefits.

## Common pitfalls

- Omitting the business-as-usual baseline, which flatters every option
- Counting build cost but not five years of operations
- Choosing the discount rate after seeing which answer it produces
- Burying step 8, so unpriceable effects silently count as zero
- Treating the highest ratio as the decision; public appraisal guidance expects
  a judgement that also weighs unpriced effects, risks and distribution
