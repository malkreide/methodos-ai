# Situational Leadership

Adjust how you lead one person on one task to how ready that person is for
that task. A leader who handles everyone alike will almost always get it wrong
for someone: the newcomer is left to sink, the seasoned colleague is watched
and corrected as if they were new. Situational Leadership asks the leader to
look at each person and each important task separately, to judge how much
skill and how much confidence and motivation the person currently brings to
it, and to choose a mix of direction and support that fits.

The model describes leader behaviour along two dimensions — how much the
leader structures and directs the work, and how much the leader encourages,
listens and backs the person — and combines them into four styles:

| Style | Direction | Support | Fits a person who, on this task… |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 Directing (originally "telling") | high | low | is new to it and lacks the skill, though often keen |
| S2 Coaching (originally "selling") | high | high | has some skill but is stuck or discouraged |
| S3 Supporting (originally "participating") | low | high | is capable but unsure of themselves or lukewarm |
| S4 Delegating | low | low | is both capable and committed |

The same person can need S4 for their everyday work and S1 for an assignment
they have never done before. The diagnosis is always of a person *on a task*,
never of a person in general.

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard introduced the idea in 1969 as the "life cycle
theory of leadership", in an article in the *Training and Development Journal*
and in their textbook *Management of Organizational Behavior*; it was later
renamed Situational Leadership. In the 1980s the two authors went separate
ways; Blanchard published his revision in 1985. Hersey's Center for Leadership Studies kept the original model, built on
a person's "performance readiness" (ability and willingness). Blanchard's
company developed Situational Leadership II (SLII), which speaks of
"development level" (competence and commitment) and re-labels the styles
directing, coaching, supporting and delegating. Both versions are sold as
commercial training programmes, with their own questionnaires and
trademarks. The outline below uses neither instrument and describes only the
general idea, which is freely discussed in the leadership literature.

## How good is the evidence?

Be honest with yourself and with the people you lead: the model is very
popular and very intuitive, but research has not borne it out well.

- Graeff (1983) criticised it on conceptual grounds: the notion of a person's
  task-relevant maturity is ambiguous to apply, the prescriptive rules that
  match a style to a level have problems of their own, and the original
  assessment questionnaire has weaknesses. In a later review (1997) he argued
  that the repeated revisions had made the model harder to use and to test.
- Vecchio (1987) tested it with 303 teachers in 14 high schools and found
  support mainly for staff low in readiness — in practice, those new to the
  job — but not for the middle and higher levels. A replication with 91 nurses
  (Norris & Vecchio, 1992) found results pointing in the predicted direction
  for low and moderate readiness, but too small to be statistically
  significant.
- Thompson and Vecchio (2009) tested three versions of the model side by
  side, among them Hersey's original and Blanchard's revision. As later
  reviews summarise the study, it found no clear support for the original and
  none for the revision, whose predictions came out weaker still.
- A 2022 thesis reviewing the field (Pearson) concludes that no published study
  has fully validated either version and that the many revisions have made
  results hard to compare.

What tends to hold up is the modest part: people new to a task benefit from
more structure. Use the model as a prompt for a conversation, not as a
prescription with predictable effects.

## When to use

- A leader notices that the same way of leading works for some people and
  fails others — newcomers flounder, experienced staff feel micromanaged
- Someone competent in their usual work has taken on something unfamiliar and
  is struggling, and the leader has not changed how closely they accompany them
- Onboarding a new member of staff, deciding how quickly to step back
- A leader and an employee want to agree openly how much guidance and how much
  room the employee will get on a specific responsibility over the coming
  weeks
- A school leader or supervisor accompanies people at very different stages,
  such as a newly qualified teacher and an experienced teacher in a new role

## When *not* to use

- As a verdict on a person. "She is a D2" is exactly the misuse the model
  invites. The level describes the current grip on one task and is meant to
  change.
- When you need a validated tool, for instance to justify a performance
  rating or a personnel decision. The research base does not support that.
- When the problem lies in the team rather than in one leader's relationship
  with one person — conflict between colleagues, unclear purpose, unclear
  roles. A [Team Charter](Team_Charter.md) or a
  [Restorative Circle](Restorative_Circle.md) fits better.
- When the question is how much a whole team may decide on its own, area by
  area. That is a standing agreement between a leader and a team; use
  [Delegation Poker](Delegation_Poker.md).
- When the question is who does, approves or is informed about which tasks in
  a project — use a [RACI matrix](RACI_Matrix.md) or, for a single decision,
  [DACI](DACI_Matrix.md).
- When you already know the person needs coaching and you want a structure for
  the conversation itself; that is the [GROW model](GROW_Model.md). Situational
  Leadership only tells you that coaching is the right mode, not how to run
  the talk.
- When the person's difficulty comes from overload, illness, a conflict or a
  poorly designed job. No choice of leadership style fixes those.

## Facilitation outline (60 min, leader and one employee)

The model can be used silently by a leader, but it works better, and is less
open to labelling, when the leader and the person discuss it together.

1. (10 min, leader alone beforehand) List the three to five most important
   tasks or responsibilities the person holds now or will take on soon. Be
   specific: "draft the budget for the after-school programme", not
   "administration".
2. (10 min, together) Explain the idea in plain words: for each task, how much
   instruction and how much encouragement would help right now. Make clear
   that this is about tasks, not about the person's worth.
3. (20 min, together) Take each task in turn. Both say, independently, how
   confident and how capable the person is on it today, then compare. Where
   you disagree, talk through concrete recent examples rather than impressions.
4. (10 min, together) For each task, agree what the leader will do: show and
   check closely; explain, ask and decide together; listen, encourage and
   leave the decisions with the person; or hand it over and only hear the
   results. Write down one sentence per task.
5. (10 min, together) Agree when to look again — usually in four to eight
   weeks, or as soon as either side feels the agreed approach no longer fits.
   Note what would show that the person is ready for less guidance.

For a leader working through a whole team, repeat steps 1–5 with each person
in their regular one-to-one meetings rather than in one sitting.

## Common pitfalls

- Diagnosing the person instead of the task. An experienced colleague moving
  to an unfamiliar responsibility needs more direction there, without that
  saying anything about their competence elsewhere.
- Keeping the diagnosis secret. A leader who silently switches to close
  supervision is experienced as distrust; the same switch, explained and
  agreed, is experienced as help.
- Jumping from close direction straight to full delegation. People usually
  need a coaching and supporting phase in between, and skipping it is where
  many handovers fail.
- Never moving the person on. The point is to need less direction over time;
  a level noted once and never revisited becomes a label.
- Treating the commercial questionnaires as measurements. They are training
  aids, and their scores have not been shown to predict outcomes reliably.
- Mixing up the two versions. The original and SLII describe the levels
  differently; pick one vocabulary and stay with it.
