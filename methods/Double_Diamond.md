# Double Diamond

Spend the first half of a project on the problem and only the second half on
the answer. Each half opens wide, then closes to a decision: first to one
problem worth solving, then to one solution that has survived testing.

```
   Discover     Define        Develop     Deliver
      /\          /\             /\          /\
  <  widen  >< narrow >  |  <  widen  >< narrow >
   problem space         |    solution space
              agreed problem statement
```

## Origin

The UK Design Council drew the model in 2003–2004, when its design and
innovation team reviewed its own projects to find a common way of describing
how designers work, and began presenting it publicly in 2004. The council's
study of design practice in large companies (*Eleven Lessons*) was organised
around it. In 2019 the council placed the diamond at the centre of a wider
Framework for Innovation that adds design principles (put people first,
communicate visually, co-create, iterate), a bank of methods, and the
leadership and engagement an organisation needs around the process. The
divergence–convergence idea it draws on is older, going back to systems-design
writing such as Béla Bánáthy's.

## When to use

- A sponsor has already named the solution ("we need an app") and nobody has
  checked whether it addresses what users actually struggle with
- A previous service, form or programme was delivered on time and then barely
  used
- Public services: redesigning how residents apply for something, or how
  parents register children, before a supplier is briefed. Government digital
  services run a comparable discovery phase before they build (GOV.UK Service
  Manual)
- Education: as a scaffold for student projects, so learners research and frame
  the problem before they jump to a product idea
- As the overall plan into which more specific methods are slotted

## When *not* to use

- The problem is already well understood and evidenced, and the open question
  is only how to deliver; plan the delivery directly
- When the team is looking for the cause of a recurring defect; that is
  [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) or an [Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md)
- When the task is improving an existing process step by step through
  measured trials rather than rethinking what users need
- When there is no access at all to the people the service is for. Without
  their voice, Discover becomes the team's assumptions written down again

## Facilitation outline (a two-day sprint version)

Real projects spend weeks in each phase; this compresses the shape so a team
can feel it once. Interviews or observation should happen before day 1.

**Day 1: the problem**

1. (30 min) State the brief as given, and write down every assumption it
   contains. Agree that the brief is a hypothesis, not a decision.
2. (2 h) Discover: share what was heard from users, staff and data. One finding
   per sticky note, quoted or observed, not interpreted. Add anything surprising.
3. (1 h 30 min) Cluster the findings into themes. Name each theme from the
   user's side ("parents don't know which documents count").
4. (1 h) Define: choose one theme and write a single problem statement: who,
   what they struggle with, and why it matters. Test it against the evidence
   wall; if nothing on the wall supports it, it is not the problem yet.
5. (30 min) Close the first diamond: the sponsor confirms the problem statement.

**Day 2: the answer**

6. (1 h 30 min) Develop: generate many ideas against the statement, alone first,
   then together. Quantity matters more than polish at this point.
7. (1 h) Pick two or three ideas and build rough prototypes: paper screens, a
   mock letter, a role-played counter conversation.
8. (2 h) Deliver: put the prototypes in front of real users. Note what fails.
9. (1 h) Decide which concept goes forward, which is dropped, and what the next
   round of testing has to answer. If testing exposed a different problem, go
   back to Define rather than forcing the solution through.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping Discover because "we already know our users"; it is usually the
  phase that pays for the whole project
- Narrowing too early: one interview turned into the problem statement
- Treating the four phases as a waterfall with sign-off gates. The diamonds are
  meant to be revisited whenever new evidence arrives
- Filling each phase with workshops but no contact with the actual users
- Ending at a prototype and calling it delivered; Deliver includes testing and
  scaling up, not only building
