# Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

Large changes rarely fail because the idea was wrong. They fail because the
leadership work around the idea was skipped, rushed or stopped too soon. The
eight steps are a checklist for that work, in the order it usually has to be
done.

```
create the climate          engage the organisation       make it last
1 urgency                   4 communicate the vision      7 build on the gains
2 guiding coalition         5 remove obstacles            8 anchor in the culture
3 vision and strategy       6 short-term wins
```

## Origin

John P. Kotter, a Harvard Business School professor, drew the model from more
than a hundred corporate transformations he had observed. He first set it out
as eight errors that sink change efforts ("Leading Change: Why Transformation
Efforts Fail", *Harvard Business Review*, March–April 1995) and then as eight
steps in the book *Leading Change* (1996). His argument was that each step
builds on the one before, and that skipping ahead only creates the impression
of speed.

Kotter revised the model himself. In "Accelerate!" (*HBR*, 2012) and the book
*Accelerate* (2014) he recast the steps as eight "accelerators" that run
concurrently and continuously, carried by a broad network of volunteers
working alongside the normal hierarchy rather than by a small group at the
top. His firm's current wording of the steps follows that later version
(for example "enlist a volunteer army" and "sustain acceleration").

## The eight steps

1. **Establish a sense of urgency.** Enough of the leadership, and ideally of
   the wider organisation, must see that carrying on as before is not an
   option. Use evidence the organisation cannot dismiss, not slogans.
2. **Form a guiding coalition.** A group with enough position, credibility and
   expertise to drive the change, working as a team rather than a steering
   committee that meets monthly.
3. **Develop a vision and strategy.** A picture of the end state short enough
   to explain in a few minutes, plus the route to it.
4. **Communicate the vision.** Far more often and through more channels than
   feels necessary, and above all through what leaders visibly do.
5. **Empower broad-based action.** Remove the structures, systems, incentives
   and managers that make the new way harder than the old one.
6. **Generate short-term wins.** Plan for visible, unambiguous improvements
   within the first months, and recognise the people who produced them.
7. **Consolidate gains and produce more change.** Use the credibility from
   early wins to tackle the bigger systems and policies; do not declare
   victory yet.
8. **Anchor the new approaches in the culture.** Show how the new way caused
   the better results, and make sure succession and promotion reward it.

## When to use

- An organisation-wide change has been announced before and faded each time
- A leadership team needs a shared plan for the human side of a restructuring,
  a system rollout or a new teaching approach, beyond the project plan
- As a review lens part-way through a programme: which steps were skipped?
- In public administrations and school systems, where changes span several
  budget cycles and need a coalition that outlasts any single sponsor

## When *not* to use

- For a change that one team can make on its own; the full sequence is
  overkill, and [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md) or a
  retrospective will do
- To find out *why* a specific group is resisting. The model tells leaders
  what to do, not what is in the way. Use
  [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md) or ask the people concerned
- When nobody yet knows what the right end state is. Steps 1–3 assume the
  destination can be described; in genuinely uncertain territory, probe and
  learn first (see [Cynefin](Cynefin_Framework.md))
- As a strict waterfall. Critics point out that the model is drawn as a
  top-down, linear sequence, while in practice the steps overlap, loop back
  and need engagement at every level, not just from the top (Pollack and
  Pollack, 2015). Kotter's own later version makes the same correction
- As settled science. A literature review found support for most individual
  steps but no study testing the model as a whole, and attributed its
  popularity more to its usability than to evidence (Appelbaum et al., 2012)

## Facilitation outline (leadership workshop, 1 day)

The change itself takes months or years. This is the planning session in
which a leadership team or guiding coalition takes stock and plans the next
phase; repeat it at each major milestone.

1. (30 min) Write down the change as a concrete end state: what will people
   do differently, and how will anyone notice? No mission statements.
2. (60 min) Score the programme against each of the eight steps, 1–5, each
   person independently first. Discuss only the steps where scores diverge or
   are low.
3. (60 min) Urgency and coalition: what evidence would convince a sceptical
   middle manager? Who is missing from the group that drives this, and who in
   it carries no weight with the staff affected?
4. (45 min) Vision: draft the two-minute version. Test it on someone outside
   the room before the end of the day.
5. (60 min) Obstacles: list the policies, systems, incentives and people that
   make the old way easier. Assign an owner to removing each of the top three.
6. (45 min) Wins: pick two or three improvements that can be visible within
   three to six months, and decide how they will be made public.
7. (30 min) Anchoring: agree what would show the change has stuck, and the
   date on which the coalition will check, well after launch.
8. (30 min) Close with who does what before the next session.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating urgency as a kick-off speech; if most managers are not convinced,
  everything after step 1 stalls
- A coalition made of the people who were available instead of those with
  influence
- Communicating the vision once, in a slide deck, and calling step 4 done
- Celebrating the first win as the finish line and disbanding the team
- Running all eight steps from the top, so staff experience the change as
  something done to them
- Inventing a crisis to create urgency; people notice, and trust suffers
