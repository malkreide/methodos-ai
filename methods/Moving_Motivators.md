# Moving Motivators

Find out what drives one particular person in their work, and how a coming
change would affect it. The person lays out ten cards, each naming one
motivator, in the order in which they matter to them. Then a concrete change is
put on the table, and each card is moved up if the change would feed that
motivator or down if it would starve it. The pattern on the table is the start
of a conversation, not a result: the value lies in asking about it.

The exercise belongs to Management 3.0, the management training programme
founded by Jurgen Appelo. He published the list of ten motivators in 2013 as a
checklist under the mnemonic CHAMPFROGS, and describes the card game in
*Managing for Happiness* (Wiley, 2016, chapter 10). The ten motivators, in our
own words:

| Card | What it stands for |
|---|---|
| Curiosity | Plenty to explore, find out and think about |
| Honor | One's own values are reflected in how one works |
| Acceptance | The people around one approve of who one is and what one does |
| Mastery | Work that stretches one's ability without overwhelming it |
| Power | Enough influence over what happens around one |
| Freedom | Independence from others in one's work and responsibilities |
| Relatedness | Good relationships and contact with the people one works with |
| Order | Enough rules, structure and stability to know where one stands |
| Goal | The work serves a purpose one believes in |
| Status | A position that others recognise and respect |

The Management 3.0 material says each card can mean something slightly
different to each person, and recommends agreeing what the words mean before
playing.

### What the research behind it does and does not show

Management 3.0 says the ten motivators were drawn from the work of Steven
Reiss, Edward Deci and Daniel Pink; Appelo himself called the list "loosely
based" on Reiss. It is worth keeping three layers apart:

- **Established.** Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) is a large and
  long-standing body of research. It holds that motivation and well-being are
  supported when three psychological needs are met: autonomy, competence and
  relatedness. *Freedom*, *Mastery* and *Relatedness* correspond closely to
  these three. Pink's popular book *Drive* (2009), built around autonomy,
  mastery and purpose, draws on the same research; its purpose is close to
  the *Goal* card.
- **Researched, but mainly by its authors.** Reiss and Havercamp derived
  sixteen basic desires from factor analyses of self-reports by several
  thousand people. Several cards match desires on that list directly: power,
  independence, curiosity, acceptance, order, honor, social contact and status.
  The evidence for the sixteen desires comes largely from Reiss, his
  colleagues and the profile test they built on it; independent testing is
  much thinner than for self-determination theory.
- **Not established.** The selection of exactly these ten, the card game
  itself and the idea that moving cards predicts how someone will react to a
  change have, as far as we could find, no published independent evaluation.
  Treat the exercise as a structured conversation aid built on recognised
  ideas, not as a diagnostic test. Do not score it, compare people's results
  against each other, or use it in hiring or appraisal decisions.

## When to use

- A manager realises they know their people's tasks well but not what each
  person actually cares about in the job
- A specific change is planned (a new role, two teams combined, a new way of
  working, a move to another site) and the manager wants to hear, person by
  person, what it does to the things that keep each of them engaged
- Someone is weighing an internal move or job offer and wants to think through
  what it would give them more or less of
- A newly formed team wants to learn that its members are driven by different
  things, so people stop assuming that everyone wants what they want
- Some time after a change, to check whether it landed as people expected

## When *not* to use

- When the question is how much a team may decide by itself: that is
  [Delegation Poker](Delegation_Poker.md), the Management 3.0 practice about
  authority rather than motivation.
- When you want to know why people keep leaving the organisation and what
  would hold them, pooled across many conversations:
  [Exit and Stay Interviews](Exit_and_Stay_Interviews.md) collect that
  systematically. Moving Motivators looks at one person, often in view of one
  change.
- When people have accepted a change but are not adopting it in practice:
  [ADKAR](ADKAR_Model.md) finds which condition for adoption is missing.
- When a change is already decided and people are grieving what they lose:
  [Bridges' Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md) helps lead them
  through the ending and the unsettled interim. Asking how the change "moves
  the cards" when nothing about it is open can feel like a sham consultation.
- When the leader's question is how much instruction and support a person
  needs for a given task: see [Situational Leadership](Situational_Leadership.md).
- When the goal is to learn how one is seen by others:
  [Johari Window](Johari_Window.md) works on feedback and self-disclosure.
- When the person needs to work out their own next step on a goal:
  [GROW](GROW_Model.md) structures that conversation. A Moving Motivators round
  can be a useful start to it.
- When trust between the two people is low, or the results could be used
  against the person: they will lay out what they think is expected.

## Facilitation outline (one-to-one, 45 min)

1. (5 min) Explain the purpose: to understand what matters to the person, not
   to assess them. Say who will see the result (usually only the two of you)
   and that the person can skip any card or stop at any time.
2. (10 min) Go through the ten cards. Ask the person what each word means to
   them; let them rephrase a card if the printed word does not fit.
3. (10 min) The person lays the cards in a row, from the motivator that
   matters least on one end to the one that matters most on the other. The
   facilitator only asks questions: "What does Order look like on a good day
   for you?", "When did you last feel that Mastery was missing?"
4. (10 min) Name one concrete change, as specifically as possible ("from
   March you will work in the combined unit under a new lead"). The person
   moves each card up if the change would help that motivator and down if it
   would hurt it, and leaves the rest in place.
5. (10 min) Talk about the pattern: which important cards go down, what could
   offset that, what the person would need. Agree one or two follow-ups and
   take a photo of the layout so it can be revisited later.

**Team variant (60–90 min, 3 to 8 people).** Everyone lays out their own row
in silence, then each person explains their two most and two least important
cards. The point is to see the differences, not to agree on a shared order.
Moving the cards for a team-wide change works too, but only where people can
speak openly in front of each other; otherwise keep that part one-to-one.

The cards can be downloaded free in many languages from the Management 3.0
website for one's own use (reselling is not permitted) or bought as a printed
set; ten handwritten cards work just as well.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the row as a profile or a score. It is a snapshot of how one person
  saw things on one day and depends on their mood and the conversation.
- The manager interpreting the layout instead of asking about it.
- Testing a vague change ("the transformation"). Without a concrete change the
  moving step has nothing to act on.
- Collecting the rows and then doing nothing. If an important motivator goes
  down and nothing follows, the next round will be less honest.
- Running it right after an announcement that is final and painful. Deal with
  the loss first; see [Bridges' Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md).
- Putting results into personnel files or using them to compare people.
