# Stakeholder Mapping (Power/Interest Grid)

Most initiatives that fail on politics were not short of good arguments. They
ran into a group nobody had thought about until it was too late to bring them
in. A stakeholder map lists everyone with a stake in the outcome, places each
on two axes, and turns the picture into a deliberate plan for whom to involve
and how.

```
          high │  keep satisfied   │  manage closely
               │ (context setters) │    (players)
   power       ├───────────────────┼───────────────────
               │      monitor      │   keep informed
          low  │     (crowd)       │    (subjects)
               └───────────────────┴───────────────────
                      low      interest      high
```

## Origin

Pairing stakeholders' power with their interest is usually credited to Aubrey
Mendelow, whose 1981 paper at the International Conference on Information
Systems tied how an organisation scans its environment to the influence of its
stakeholders (textbooks often cite it with a 1991 date). Colin Eden and Fran
Ackermann developed the grid as a working tool for strategy teams in *Making
Strategy* (1998) and named the four quadrants players, subjects, context
setters and crowd. John Bryson's article "What to do when stakeholders matter"
(*Public Management Review*, 2004) set it among a family of techniques for
public and nonprofit organisations and gave a step-by-step wall-chart
procedure. The Overseas Development Institute uses the same grid to plan how
research can influence policy.

## When to use

- Before a project, policy or reform is announced, while there is still time to
  involve people rather than react to them
- When an initiative cuts across many groups — parents, staff, unions,
  councils, regulators, customers — and the team cannot keep them all in mind
- When choosing who belongs on a steering group, sounding board or
  consultation list
- Again at each major milestone, because positions move once a plan is public

## When *not* to use

- To find out *why* a change has already stalled — that is
  [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md)
- To settle who decides, who is consulted and who is informed about a single
  decision inside a team — that is [DACI](DACI_Matrix.md)
- For impersonal external trends such as legislation or demographics — use
  [PESTEL](PESTEL_Analysis.md)
- As a replacement for actually talking to the people on the map

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

1. (10 min) Write the initiative as one concrete sentence: what will change,
   for whom, by when. A vague initiative produces a vague map.
2. (20 min) Brainstorm everyone who is affected by it or can affect it, one name
   per sticky note. Name specific groups ("parents' council of school A"), not
   categories ("the public"). Ask who is missing: who could go to the press, to
   a politician, to court?
3. (25 min) Place each note on the grid. Power means the ability to stop, delay,
   fund or reshape the initiative, formally or informally. Interest means how
   much the outcome affects them or how much they care. Take notes round-robin
   so no one person places them all, and move them until the group agrees.
4. (10 min) Mark each note with its likely stance — supportive, neutral or
   opposed — with a colour or symbol. The grid alone does not show this.
5. (20 min) Agree an approach for each quadrant, and then for each name in the
   top-right: who contacts them, about what, how often. Look hard at the
   top-left; those parties are quiet now and decisive later.
6. (5 min) Decide where the map lives, who may see it, and when it will be
   reviewed.

## Common pitfalls

- Listing abstract categories instead of people and groups you could phone
- Confusing visibility with power: the loudest group is not always the one that
  can stop the plan
- Leaving the map unchanged after the announcement, when interest typically
  rises sharply among those who were in the bottom half
- Treating the bottom-right as people to placate; organised, they can form a
  coalition with real weight
- Sharing the annotated map widely; labels such as "monitor only" read badly to
  the people they describe
