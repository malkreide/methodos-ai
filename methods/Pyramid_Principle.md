# Pyramid Principle (Minto)

Put the answer first. A document meant to get something from its reader (a
decision, an approval, a change of course) opens with that one point. Below
it come the few reasons or steps that support it, and below each of those the
facts that support *them*. Read from the top down, the text answers the
reader's questions in the order they would ask them: *what do you want?*,
then *why?* or *how?*, and only then *how do you know?*

Barbara Minto developed the approach while she was a consultant at McKinsey &
Company in the 1960s and early 1970s, and later taught it in her own courses.
An early printing by her own firm is catalogued from 1981. The book appeared as
*The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* in 1987, and since 1996 in a
revised and extended edition, *The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing,
Thinking and Problem Solving*. The method is widely taught in consulting firms
and large organisations. Its central rule, to state the main point before the
details, is also a standard rule of plain-language guidance for public
administration. A German translation, *Das Prinzip der Pyramide*, appeared in
2005.

The method has critics. In German-language rhetoric training it is contrasted
with a "funnel" structure that ends on the key sentence (Stefan Wachtel's
*Zielsatz* approach), on the grounds that persuasion often depends on taking
the listener through the reasoning; others object that a conclusion-first text
states facts without engaging the reader.

## The parts

- **One governing thought at the top.** A single sentence the reader could act
  on: "We recommend moving to the new timetable model from August, at no extra
  cost to staffing." A topic ("Timetable options") is not a governing thought.
- **An introduction built from situation, complication and question (SCQ).**
  The *situation* is something the reader already knows and accepts. The
  *complication* is what has changed or gone wrong. Together they raise the
  *question* that the governing thought answers. Two or three sentences are
  usually enough, and they replace the long history many reports open with.
- **Key points beneath it, grouped.** Usually three to five. Each one directly
  answers the question the governing thought raises in the reader's mind.
  Together they should cover the case without overlapping, the test Minto
  called MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
- **A deliberate order within each group.** Minto names three kinds of order:
  by time (steps in sequence), by structure (the parts of a whole), and by
  rank (most important first). If you cannot say which order a group follows,
  the group is probably not yet thought through.
- **Two kinds of grouping.** An *inductive* group lists points of the same
  kind that together support the point above (three separate reasons). A
  *deductive* group is a chain: a statement, a comment on it, and the
  conclusion that follows. Minto advises using induction at the upper levels,
  because a chain makes the reader hold every link in mind before getting to
  the point.

## When to use

- A report, memo, briefing paper or long e-mail to a superior, a board or a
  committee that has to decide or approve something
- A status report in which one item needs action and would otherwise be lost
  among the routine updates
- When feedback on your drafts says "what is your point?" or "what do you need
  from us?"
- To check a draft that someone else wrote: write down its governing thought
  and key points; if you cannot, the reader will not manage it either
- To plan a slide deck: the governing thought is the first slide, and the
  headlines of the following slides are the key points

## When *not* to use

- While you are still working out what you think. Gather and sort what you
  know first with [Mind Mapping](Mind_Mapping.md); split an open analytical
  question into parts that can be researched with an
  [Issue Tree](Issue_Tree.md). The pyramid comes afterwards, once there is an
  answer to put at the top
- For readers who expect the reasoning before the conclusion. Erin Meyer's
  research on cultural differences in business describes cultures that are
  persuaded "principles first", by the argument before the result, and others
  that want the result straight away. The same holds within organisations
  with a strong tradition of reasoned argument. For these readers, keep the
  grouped structure but lead up to the conclusion
- For academic papers, legal opinions, judgements and other genres whose
  readers expect a fixed order of their own
- For bad news that the reader needs to be prepared for, such as a rejection
  or a serious incident affecting people. A blunt first sentence can close
  minds before the reasons are read. Prepare the ground, but do not hide the
  point
- When several groups of people have to say the same things in the same
  words over time; that is a messaging task across a campaign, not the
  structure of one document
- To give one person feedback on their behaviour: use
  [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md)
- To look at a question from several angles in a meeting before anyone
  commits to an answer: use [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md)

The evidence base is practitioner experience. The method is widely taught and
used, and its core rule matches plain-language guidance, but there are few
controlled studies of the method itself.

## Facilitation outline (60 min, alone or with one reviewer)

1. (10 min) Write down who the reader is and the question they will have in
   mind when they open the document. Then write your answer to it in one
   sentence. If you cannot, stop: the problem is your thinking, not your
   writing.
2. (10 min) Draft the introduction: one sentence of situation the reader will
   agree with, one sentence of complication, and the question that follows.
   Check that your one-sentence answer really answers that question.
3. (15 min) Ask "why?" or "how?" of the answer and list the replies. Group
   them into three to five key points. Remove overlaps, and check for a
   missing point the reader would raise. Put each group in a definite order:
   time, structure or rank.
4. (10 min) Under each key point, list the facts, figures and examples that
   support it. Move anything that supports no key point to an appendix or
   delete it.
5. (10 min) Write the document from the top down. The governing thought goes
   in the subject line or the first paragraph, and each key point becomes a
   heading or a paragraph's first sentence.
6. (5 min) Test it: give a colleague only the first paragraph and the
   headings, and ask them what you want and why. If their answer matches
   yours, send it.

## Common pitfalls

- Putting a topic or a summary of the work at the top ("This report reviews
  the timetable") instead of a point the reader can act on
- Turning the situation into a long history; it should be what the reader
  already knows, in two sentences
- Groups whose items are not of the same kind, such as a reason, a step and a
  risk under one heading
- Key points that are labels ("Costs", "Staffing") instead of statements
  ("Staffing costs stay the same")
- Long deductive chains at the top, so the reader must follow five links
  before learning why
- Treating MECE as absolute when the reasons genuinely overlap; say so, and
  group by what the reader needs to decide
