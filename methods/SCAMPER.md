# SCAMPER

Take something that already exists, such as a course, a form, an induction
day, a library service or a product, and put seven standard questions to it,
one after the other. Each question names a kind of change: **S**ubstitute,
**C**ombine, **A**dapt, **M**odify (including magnify and minify), **P**ut to
other uses, **E**liminate, and **R**everse or rearrange. Asked of each part of
the existing thing in turn, the prompts produce a long list of concrete
variants, most of them small, some of them surprising. The list is raw
material; choosing what to try comes afterwards.

The questions go back to the idea-spurring checklist in Alex F. Osborn's
*Applied Imagination* (1953), the book that also popularised brainstorming.
Osborn suggested that people looking for ideas ask systematically what could
be adapted, modified, magnified, minified, substituted, rearranged, reversed
or combined. Bob Eberle, an educator, condensed these questions into
the memorable acronym in *SCAMPER: Games for Imagination Development*, a book
of imagination exercises for children first published in 1971 and reissued by
Prufrock Press in 1996. Some secondary sources give a later date for the
acronym; the 1971 first edition is the one cited by both Wikipedia articles
listed below. Eberle also added "eliminate", which Osborn's list did not have
as a heading. From the classroom the checklist moved into product design,
service design and management training, and it is now a staple of
creativity courses and workshop handbooks.

The research base is modest. In the study most directly relevant to services,
Moreno and colleagues (2014) asked 60 experienced managers to generate ideas
for reducing overdue accounts. After a fifteen-minute introduction, those who
used SCAMPER kept up their output in a second round and produced most of the
novel ideas, while the unaided group fell back on repeating earlier ones. The
authors also found that SCAMPER users spent much of their time refining
variants of ideas they already had, which suits improvement work but not a
search for something entirely different. Moreno and colleagues cite a few
earlier design studies with broadly positive results; beyond those, the case
for SCAMPER rests on general research about checklists and prompts in idea
generation rather than on a body of controlled trials of the method itself.

## When to use

- An offer, service, document or routine works but feels tired, and you want
  ideas for improving it without throwing it away
- The group keeps producing the same two or three suggestions and needs
  prompts that push it to think in other directions
- Someone working alone wants a structured way to come up with alternatives
  to their own lesson plan, form or product
- You need variants of an existing design to test or compare, for example
  several versions of a letter, a timetable slot or a product
- As the idea-generation step inside a larger improvement or design process,
  once the problem is already clear

## When *not* to use

- When the problem itself is not yet understood. SCAMPER improves whatever is
  in front of it, so if nobody knows why the current offer is failing, first
  find out from the people who use it, for example with a
  [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md), or by working through the
  research phase of [Double Diamond](Double_Diamond.md)
- When you need a radically different answer rather than a better version of
  the current one. The prompts start from what exists and tend to stay close
  to it
- When the real need is a long list of answers to an open question, with no
  existing object to start from; a writing round such as
  [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md) suits that better
- When a design has several features that each have known alternatives and
  you want to see every workable combination; a
  [Morphological Box](Morphological_Box.md) maps that space systematically
- When the ideas already exist and the task is to judge or choose between
  them: use [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md) to examine them, or
  [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md) or a
  [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md) to choose
- When you want a whole design project from research to tested prototype;
  SCAMPER is one step that [Double Diamond](Double_Diamond.md) or a
  [Design Sprint](Design_Sprint.md) might contain, not a replacement for them
- When the question is how a team should change its own way of working
  together; [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) is built for that

## The seven prompts

| Letter | Kind of change | Example questions |
|---|---|---|
| S | Substitute | What could we use, or who could do it, instead? Which material, place, channel or time could replace the current one? |
| C | Combine | What could be merged with something else, such as two steps, two meetings, two services or two audiences? |
| A | Adapt | Where has someone solved a similar problem, and what could we borrow from them? |
| M | Modify, magnify, minify | What could be made larger, smaller, longer, shorter, more frequent or less frequent? What could look or feel different? |
| P | Put to other uses | Who else could use this, or what else could it be used for? What could we reuse from elsewhere? |
| E | Eliminate | What could be left out entirely? What would be the simplest version that still works? |
| R | Reverse, rearrange | What if the order of steps changed, or roles were swapped, or we did the opposite of what we do now? |

The questions are prompts, not a fixed script. Adapt the wording to the
object; a form needs different questions from a course or a product.

## Facilitation outline (about 75 min)

1. (10 min) Name the object in one sentence and agree on its limits: "our
   introduction day for new teachers", not "how we welcome people". Say what
   the improvement is meant to achieve, for example more involvement or less
   effort, so ideas have a direction.
2. (10 min) Break the object down into its parts on a board: the stages of a
   process, the sections of a form, the components of a product. SCAMPER works
   best when the questions are asked of specific parts, not of the whole.
3. (35 min) Go through the seven prompts in turn, about five minutes each.
   For each prompt, everyone first writes ideas silently on cards, one idea
   per card, then reads them out and posts them under the letter. No
   discussion of merit yet. Skip a prompt quickly if it plainly does not fit.
4. (10 min) Cluster similar cards and look for ideas that could be combined.
   Note which letters produced little; sometimes that shows where thinking is
   stuck.
5. (10 min) Select a handful of ideas for further work, for example with dot
   voting, and agree who will test or develop each and by when.

Alone, the same sequence takes thirty to forty minutes with a sheet of paper
divided into seven fields. Online, a shared board with one column per letter
works well, and the silent writing step can be run asynchronously over a few
days.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with an object that is too broad, so the prompts produce slogans
  instead of concrete changes
- Treating it as compulsory to find an idea for every letter, and filling the
  board with forced suggestions
- Discussing and rejecting ideas while they are still being generated
- Counting the number of ideas as success and never doing the selection step
- Expecting a breakthrough: the method is good at improvement, not at
  replacing the offer with something fundamentally new
- Letting the most senior person's ideas set the direction; the silent
  writing step is there to prevent that
