# Lesson Study

A small group of teachers studies teaching by working on one lesson together.
They agree what pupils should learn, plan a single "research lesson" in great
detail, and then one of them teaches it in a real class while the others sit
among the pupils and record how they respond: what they say, write, try and get
stuck on. Afterwards the group discusses that evidence, not the colleague who
taught, and decides what it means for the lesson and for their teaching in
general. Often the revised lesson is taught again in another class.

The practice comes from Japan, where it is called *jugyō kenkyū* (lesson
research) and has long been a routine part of teachers' professional life in
primary schools. It reached a wide audience outside Japan through James
Stigler and James Hiebert's comparison of classroom teaching in Germany, Japan
and the United States, *The Teaching Gap* (1999). Catherine Lewis and Jacqueline
Hurd set out a practical version for schools in *Lesson Study Step by Step*
(2011), and the Lesson Study Group at Mills College in California publishes a
four-phase cycle: study, plan, teach, reflect. Since then
it has been adapted for secondary schools, for university teaching (Cerbin &
Kopp, 2006) and for vocational education and training, for example in the
Erasmus+ project LS4VET with partners in Austria, Hungary, Malta and the
Netherlands (Mewald et al., 2021).

Three features set it apart from an ordinary classroom visit:

- **The lesson is the group's, not the teacher's.** Because everyone planned
  it, the person who teaches it is carrying out a shared design. If something
  goes wrong, it is the plan that failed, which keeps the debrief about
  teaching rather than about a person.
- **Observers watch the pupils.** Each observer follows particular pupils or a
  particular table and notes evidence of their thinking. Comments on the
  teacher's manner or classroom management are out of scope unless they bear
  on what pupils learned.
- **Predictions come first.** During planning, the group writes down how it
  expects pupils to respond to each task, including wrong answers. The debrief
  then compares what happened with what was predicted, which is where most of
  the learning lies.

In Japan, school-based lesson study usually runs for a year or more around a
shared school-wide research theme, and what is learned feeds into the next
research lesson rather than into reteaching the same one; the
"revise-and-reteach" loop is more typical of adaptations elsewhere. Groups may
also invite an experienced outside commentator who speaks at the end of the
debrief.

## When to use

- Teachers work in isolation, rarely see each other's classes, and good
  practice does not spread beyond one classroom
- A topic goes wrong every year and the group wants to see, in an actual
  lesson, where pupils' understanding breaks down
- A school is introducing a new curriculum or teaching approach and wants
  teachers to try it out together and learn from pupils' responses instead of
  relying on a single training day
- Lecturers or vocational instructors who teach the same course want to
  improve one critical session together
- A school or network wants a lasting, teacher-led form of professional
  learning rather than a sequence of one-off courses

## When *not* to use

- **Without protected time.** A cycle needs several planning meetings, a
  timetabled lesson that colleagues are released to attend, and a debrief soon
  afterwards, spread over weeks. Squeezed into spare minutes, it shrinks into
  an ordinary lesson observation
- **Where trust is low or the observation is tied to appraisal.** If teachers
  fear that what is seen will feed into performance reviews, the research
  lesson turns into a show lesson and the debrief into an evaluation of the
  teacher. Keep line managers out of the observer role, or start somewhere
  else
- **When you need a quick fix for an urgent problem with one class or pupil.**
  A difficult situation with one pupil or parent is better served by
  [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md)
- **As a promise of better test results.** The evidence outside Japan is
  mixed. A large randomised trial in England with 181 primary schools found
  no evidence that the version it tested improved pupils' maths and reading
  results at the end of primary school, although teachers valued it and
  reported changes to their practice. The evaluators noted that some
  comparison schools already used similar practices such as peer observation
  (Murphy et al., 2017). A US trial with 39 educator teams found significant
  gains in teachers' and pupils' knowledge of fractions when teams ran lesson
  study with a resource kit of mathematical tasks, research and lesson
  materials (Lewis & Perry, 2017). One reading of the two results is that the
  process alone is not enough: the group needs good subject material to work
  with
- **When there is no subject question to investigate.** If the group cannot
  name what pupils should understand better, the planning drifts into
  generic activity design

## Facilitation outline (one cycle over 4 to 8 weeks, 3 to 6 teachers)

Agree ground rules before the first meeting: the lesson belongs to the group,
observers record what pupils do rather than judging the teacher, and what is
said about individual pupils stays in the group. Inform pupils, and where
required parents, that colleagues will be in the room and why. Name one person
to convene the meetings and keep time.

1. (60–90 min, week 1) **Goal.** Agree a long-term aim for pupils (for
   example, explaining their reasoning) and a topic where pupils regularly
   struggle. Look at curriculum documents, textbooks, pupils' work and any
   research on how pupils learn the topic.
2. (2–4 meetings of 60–90 min, weeks 2–5) **Plan the research lesson.** Choose
   one lesson within the unit. Each member works through the main task
   themselves. Write a lesson plan that states the goal, the sequence of tasks,
   the reasoning behind each decision, and the responses you expect from pupils,
   including likely mistakes. Decide what evidence to collect (pupils' talk,
   written work, solution methods) and who observes which pupils. Prepare an
   observation sheet that follows the plan.
3. (one lesson, week 5 or 6) **Teach and observe.** One member teaches the
   lesson as planned. Observers stand or sit where they can see and hear their
   assigned pupils, do not help or interrupt, and record what pupils say and
   do at each stage, with times. Collect pupils' written work at the end. Some
   groups also interview two or three pupils briefly afterwards.
4. (60–90 min, the same day or the next) **Debrief.** The teacher speaks first
   about what they noticed and any departures from the plan. Each observer then
   reports evidence from their pupils, phase by phase, compared with the
   predictions. Keep to evidence: "Two of my three pupils drew the fraction as
   separate pieces, not on a line" rather than "the explanation was unclear".
   If an outside commentator is present, they speak last.
5. (60 min, within two weeks) **Revise and record.** Decide what the evidence
   means for the lesson and for teaching the topic. Revise the plan, and
   either reteach it in another class with roles swapped or carry the findings
   into the next research lesson. Write up the lesson plan, the evidence and
   the conclusions so that colleagues who teach the same topic can use them.

In the University of Wisconsin's college lesson study project, teams typically
spread 15 to 20 hours of work over an academic year and ran two iterations of
one lesson (Cerbin & Kopp, 2006).

## Common pitfalls

- Observers turning into evaluators and commenting on the teacher's voice,
  pacing or classroom management instead of on pupils' learning
- Skipping the predictions, which leaves the debrief with nothing to test
  observations against
- A lesson plan that only lists activities and does not say why each one is
  there or what pupils are expected to think
- Choosing a showpiece lesson rather than a topic where pupils genuinely
  struggle
- Observers who help pupils during the lesson and so change what they were
  meant to observe
- Debriefs held days later, when observers' notes have gone cold
- Running one cycle as a project and never repeating it, so it never becomes
  a way of working
- Treating the finished lesson plan as the main product and losing what the
  group learned about pupils' thinking

## How it differs from its neighbours

- [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md) — peers advise
  one colleague on a difficult situation that colleague brings and describes;
  nobody observes a lesson, and the case owner stays responsible for what
  happens next. In lesson study the whole group designs and watches a lesson
  and shares responsibility for it
- [Gemba Walk](Gemba_Walk.md) — leaders go to where work is done to see its
  obstacles for themselves; here peers observe pupils' learning in a lesson
  they have planned together, and no manager needs to be involved
- [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) — a team looks back on an event
  it has just been through; lesson study designs the event in advance as a
  test, with predictions and planned observation, and then reviews it
- [PDCA Cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md) — the general improvement loop; lesson study is
  a specific form of it for teaching, with a fixed way of planning, observing
  and discussing one lesson
- [Jigsaw Method](Jigsaw_Method.md) — a way of organising pupils' learning in
  class; lesson study is for the teachers, and could be used to study a lesson
  that uses the Jigsaw Method
- [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md) — tells one person how their behaviour came
  across; lesson study deliberately avoids giving the teacher personal feedback
  and focuses on pupils
