# GROW Model

A four-stage outline for a conversation in which one person helps another think
a problem through, instead of solving it for them. The helper mostly asks and
listens; the other person does the thinking and owns what comes out of it.

- **Goal** — what does the person want to get out of this topic, and out of
  this conversation in particular? What would be different if it worked?
- **Reality** — where do things actually stand? What has been tried, what
  helped, what got in the way, what is within their control?
- **Options** — what could they do? Collect several routes before judging any
  of them, including ones that look unlikely at first.
- **Will** (also "Way forward") — which option will they act on, what exactly
  is the first step, by when, who supports it, and how will they know it
  happened?

The model grew out of British business coaching in the late 1980s. John
Whitmore set it out in *Coaching for Performance* (Nicholas Brealey, first
edition 1992), and Graham Alexander and Alan Fine are both credited with
shaping it; accounts of who coined the acronym differ. Whitmore described the
purpose of coaching as raising the other person's awareness and sense of
responsibility, and GROW is the scaffolding for that, not the purpose itself.
Many trainers, including NHS training material, put a **T** for *Topic* in
front (T-GROW) so that the person first names what they want to talk about.

The category here is *facilitation*: GROW does not decide anything or analyse a
system; it gives shape to a conversation so that it reaches a usable outcome.

## When to use

- A leader notices that staff bring every difficulty to them and leave with the
  leader's answer, and wants people to build their own judgement
- One-to-one or development talks keep ending without a clear agreement about
  what happens next
- A supervisor, mentor or peer wants to help someone who is stuck on a specific
  issue they are able to work on themselves
- Short regular check-ins, where a simple shared structure saves time
- Thinking through a personal decision on your own, by writing answers to the
  four stages

## When *not* to use

- **Performance, conduct or disciplinary issues** where the expected standard is
  not up for discussion. Asking "what options do you see?" when the answer is
  already fixed is confusing at best and manipulative at worst; say plainly
  what is required, then use questions only for *how* the person will meet it
- **Crises and safety-critical moments** that need a fast, directive decision.
  Coach afterwards, if at all
- **When the person lacks the knowledge or skill** the situation demands. A new
  colleague who has never done a task needs to be shown, not asked what they
  think the procedure might be; teach first and coach once they have something
  to draw on
- **When the person does not want to be coached**, or the relationship carries
  too much power or mistrust for open answers. The CIPD notes that whether line
  managers can coach their own staff, given the power relationship, is a
  recurring concern
- **As a script.** Whitmore's own warning, quoted by his firm, was that "any
  dictator can use GROW": the four letters in order, without real listening and
  curiosity, produce an interrogation, not a coaching conversation
- **For deep personal or existential questions** (identity, values, meaning)
  where a goal-first structure narrows the conversation too early

## Facilitation outline (one-to-one, about 40 minutes)

1. (3 min) **Topic and contract.** Ask what the person wants to use the time
   for. Agree that you will mostly ask questions and that the decisions are
   theirs. If you are their line manager, say openly which parts are
   negotiable and which are not.
2. (7 min) **Goal.** Clarify two goals: the longer-term one for the topic and
   the one for this conversation ("by the end of these 40 minutes I'd like
   to..."). Ask how they would recognise success.
3. (12 min) **Reality.** Ask for facts and examples, not judgements: what is
   happening, what they have already tried, what worked and what did not, who
   else is involved, what they can influence. Resist offering solutions here;
   people often see new options simply by describing the situation fully.
4. (10 min) **Options.** Ask for several possibilities and keep asking "what
   else?" after the first two or three. Only add your own idea at the end, if
   at all, and offer it as one option among theirs, not the answer.
5. (6 min) **Will.** Ask which option they choose and why, the very first step,
   the date, what might stop them and who could help. A rating ("how likely,
   from 1 to 10, are you to do this?") often exposes a commitment that is
   still too vague.
6. (2 min) **Close.** Ask them to state the agreed step in their own words, and
   agree when you will check back.

In practice the conversation moves back and forth, especially between Reality
and Options, and sometimes Reality shows that the original goal was the wrong
one. That is expected: return to the stage that needs work rather than forcing
the order. A 20-minute check-in compresses each stage; a small group can use
the same stages with each member answering in turn.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping Reality and jumping from the goal straight to solutions, which
  produces options that do not fit the actual situation
- Asking leading questions ("have you thought about simply...?") that are
  advice in disguise
- Setting a goal that is too vague to recognise when it has been reached
- Ending without a dated first step, so the conversation feels productive and
  changes nothing
- Never following up; the next conversation should start by asking what
  happened with the agreed step
- Using the model to steer someone towards an outcome the leader decided in
  advance; people notice and stop answering honestly
