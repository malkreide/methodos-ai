# Team Charter (Working Agreement, Team Canvas)

At the start of a team's life, sit down together and write a short agreement on
how you will work as a group: why the team exists, who brings what to it, how
decisions are taken, how members keep each other informed and how they handle
disagreement. The point is not the document but the conversation that produces
it. Every team develops rules anyway; a charter makes them explicit and shared
before unspoken, conflicting assumptions harden into friction.

The idea has several names. In organisational research it is a *team charter*;
agile teams speak of *working agreements*; the *Team Canvas* by Alex Ivanov and
Mitya Voloshchuk (2015, published under CC BY-SA 4.0) is a free
one-page template for the same conversation, inspired by the Business Model
Canvas. Its basic version walks a group through goals, roles and skills,
purpose, values, and finally rules and activities: how the team will
communicate, decide, carry out and review its work.

The research behind it is encouraging but modest. In J. Richard Hackman's
account of what makes teams effective (*Leading Teams*, 2002), an enabling
team structure is one of five conditions for success, next to a real, stable
team and a compelling direction; later summaries of his work with Ruth Wageman
spell out that this structure includes a handful of explicit norms about what
members should and should not do when they work together. Mathieu and Rapp (2009)
followed 32 teams of MBA students through a business simulation: the teams that
sustained the highest performance had both a high-quality charter and a
high-quality plan for the task itself, so the charter paid off in combination
with sound task planning rather than as a substitute for it. The teams were
students in a simulation, so how far the result carries over to workplace teams
remains open.

## When to use

- A new project group, task force or committee is formed, especially when its
  members come from departments or organisations with different habits
- Two teams have been merged, or a team has had a large change of membership,
  and old ways of working now clash
- A new leadership team (a school head and deputies, a newly appointed board)
  needs to agree how it will work before the first difficult decision
- Student or trainee groups start a long assignment and the lecturer wants
  them to settle in advance how they will share work and deal with absentees
- A group working mostly online has no shared rules on channels, response times
  or when something counts as decided

## When *not* to use

- When there is already a serious conflict or a breach of trust. Writing
  pleasant rules on top of it will not resolve it and may hide it; deal with
  the conflict first, if necessary with outside help.
- When the leader has already written the rules and only wants signatures.
  An imposed charter carries none of the commitment that makes it work; call it
  what it is, a set of instructions.
- When nobody intends to revisit it. A charter that is filed and forgotten is
  paper; if you cannot commit to a first review, keep the session short and
  focus on two or three rules that matter.
- When the real question is which unit performs which step of a process and who
  answers for it — use a [RACI Matrix](RACI_Matrix.md).
- When the question is how much a team may decide without its leader — use
  [Delegation Poker](Delegation_Poker.md). A charter can record the outcome,
  but it is not the instrument for negotiating authority.
- When the group needs measurable results for the coming period — that is
  [OKR](OKR.md). A charter's purpose statement says why the team exists, not
  what it must achieve this quarter.
- When people misread each other and trust stays shallow — the
  [Johari Window](Johari_Window.md) works on how members see one another; a
  charter works on the rules they share.
- When the team is grieving a reorganisation it did not choose — start with the
  [Bridges Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md); rules about meetings
  will not reach people who are still mourning the old arrangement.

## Facilitation outline (120 min)

1. (10 min) Explain why you are doing this: everyone will bring assumptions
   from earlier teams, and it is cheaper to compare them now than to discover
   them in a dispute. Agree that the result belongs to the whole group and can
   be changed later.
2. (15 min) **Purpose.** Each person writes in one sentence why this team
   exists and what would be lost if it did not. Compare, then draft one shared
   sentence. Keep it to the reason for the team; detailed targets belong in a
   separate plan.
3. (15 min) **People and roles.** Each member notes their name, their formal
   role, the skills they bring and what they need from the others. Read them
   out. Note gaps and overlaps, but do not try to assign every task here.
4. (20 min) **Decisions.** Agree how the group will decide in normal cases (the
   leader after consulting, majority, consent, the person responsible for the
   topic) and what happens when it cannot agree. Record any decisions that are
   fixed by law, statute or the wider organisation so nobody expects to vote on
   them.
5. (25 min) **Communication and meetings.** Which channel is used for what;
   how quickly people are expected to answer; when the group meets and how
   meetings are prepared and minuted; what is confidential; how absent members
   are kept up to date.
6. (15 min) **Disagreement and commitments.** How members raise a problem with
   a colleague; what happens when someone misses an agreed deadline or stops
   contributing; who may call for the charter to be reopened.
7. (10 min) **Check and close.** Read the whole draft aloud. Ask each person
   whether they can work by it; change anything someone cannot accept. Set the
   date of the first review, usually four to eight weeks later, and name who
   will call it.

A short version of 45 minutes, in the manner of the basic Team Canvas, covers
purpose, roles and a handful of ground rules, and suits short projects. Keep the
result visible: on the wall of the team room, at the top of the shared
workspace, or as the first page of the meeting minutes.

## Common pitfalls

- Stopping at values. "Respect" and "openness" are easy to agree on and hard to
  apply; each value needs at least one concrete rule that shows what it means
  in practice.
- Writing too much. A charter of ten pages will not be read; one or two pages
  with rules people can remember are enough.
- Letting the most senior or most talkative members draft everything. Give
  everyone time to write individually before discussing.
- Agreeing rules nobody enforces. When the first breach passes without
  comment, the charter loses its force; agree beforehand how breaches are
  raised.
- Never updating it. New members, a new phase of work or a first crisis are
  all reasons to reopen the agreement, and newcomers should be walked through
  it rather than handed a copy.
