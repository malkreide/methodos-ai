# Objectives and Key Results (OKR)

Pick a few things that must be different by the end of a fixed period, usually
a quarter. Write each as an **objective**: a short, qualitative statement of
the outcome you want. Under each, write two to five **key results**: measurable
statements that would show, without argument, that the objective was reached.
Then look at the numbers together every week or two, and change course when
they are not moving.

Andy Grove built the approach at Intel in the 1970s as an adaptation of
management by objectives and described it in *High Output Management* (1983).
John Doerr, who learned it at Intel, brought it to Google in 1999 and set out
the practice for a wider audience in *Measure What Matters* (2018). The
underlying idea, that specific and demanding goals with feedback outperform
"do your best", rests on decades of goal-setting research (Locke and Latham,
2002); so do its best-documented side effects (Ordóñez et al., 2009).

## When to use

- Teams report being fully occupied, but nobody can name the few results that
  work is supposed to produce this quarter
- Goals exist only as slogans ("be customer-centric") or as activity lists
  ("launch the portal", "run three workshops")
- Several units work towards the same strategy without seeing each other's
  priorities, so they duplicate or contradict each other
- Progress is only discussed in an annual review, long after a course
  correction would have helped

## When *not* to use

- **When scores feed pay, bonuses or performance appraisal.** People then set
  targets they know they can hit and report numbers in the most favourable
  light. Doerr's own guidance keeps OKRs separate from compensation, and
  Google's states that they are not a performance evaluation. If your organisation cannot keep them apart, use a
  different planning tool.
- **When you cannot keep the list short.** Ten objectives with five key results
  each is the old wish list with new headings. If leadership will not drop
  anything, the method will not create focus; settle priorities first, for
  example with [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md) or [MoSCoW](MoSCoW_Method.md).
- **When the "key results" are really tasks.** "Hold four stakeholder
  workshops" can be completed while nothing changes. If a team can only name
  activities, the outcome has not been understood yet, and that conversation
  has to happen before OKRs are useful.
- **With caution in public services and education.** Many outcomes there (a
  pupil's understanding, a citizen's trust, the quality of a social-work
  decision) resist honest measurement. Forcing them into numbers invites
  Goodhart's law: once a proxy becomes the target, people optimise the proxy,
  for instance by closing cases quickly rather than well, or teaching to a
  test. Use fewer, carefully chosen measures, pair any count with a quality
  check, and accept that some objectives are better judged by evidence and
  discussion than by a score.
- **For a one-off crisis or a single project** with a fixed scope and
  deadline: a project plan fits better than a recurring goal cycle.
- **When the organisation has no strategy yet.** OKRs translate a direction into
  quarterly commitments; they do not choose the direction. Start with
  [SWOT](SWOT.md), [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md) or a
  [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md).

## Facilitation outline (one quarter)

**Setting workshop (about 3 hours, per team, before the quarter starts)**

1. (20 min) Recap the organisation's priorities and last quarter's results.
   Note what was learned, not who missed what.
2. (40 min) Each person writes candidate objectives on their own, then the
   group clusters them. Test each: would the team be proud if this were true in
   three months? Is it an outcome, not a project?
3. (20 min) Choose at most three objectives. Explicitly list what is *not*
   being pursued this quarter, so the choice is visible.
4. (60 min) For each objective, draft two to five key results. Each must be a
   number or a verifiable state with a baseline and a target ("repeat
   enquiries fall from 30% to 15%"), and must describe an effect, not an
   activity. Ask of each one: could we hit this and still fail the objective?
   If yes, rewrite it or add a counterbalancing measure.
5. (20 min) Agree an owner for each key result, how and how often it will be
   measured, and which targets are stretch goals rather than commitments.
6. (20 min) Share the draft with neighbouring teams and leadership; adjust
   where objectives collide or depend on each other.

**Check-ins (15–30 min, every one or two weeks)**

7. Each owner reports the current value, a confidence level for reaching the
   target, and what is blocking it. Discuss only the key results that are off
   track; decide what to change.

**Review and retrospective (about 90 min, at the end of the quarter)**

8. (45 min) Score each key result (for example 0.0–1.0). For stretch goals,
   reaching around 0.6–0.7 is normally a good result; consistently scoring 1.0
   means the targets were too safe.
9. (45 min) Discuss what the scores taught you about the work and about the
   measures themselves. Carry lessons, not unfinished objectives by default,
   into the next setting workshop. An
   [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) works well here.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing key results as a project plan ("launch", "deliver", "hold")
- Setting objectives for every individual; most practitioners now recommend
  team- and organisation-level OKRs only, because individual ones turn into
  task lists and appraisal targets
- Cascading every objective mechanically down the hierarchy, which makes
  setting slow and leaves no room for teams to propose their own
- Writing the OKRs at the start of the quarter and next reading them at the end
- Measuring only what is easy to count, then treating the count as the goal
- Rolling over every unfinished objective, so the list grows instead of focusing
- Punishing low scores on deliberately ambitious targets, which guarantees the
  next round of targets will be timid
