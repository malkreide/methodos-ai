# Fist to Five

A one-minute reading of how much support a concrete plan has in the room. The
facilitator states the plan, counts to three, and everyone raises one hand at
the same moment: a closed fist means "I cannot go along with this", five
fingers means "fully behind it, and I will help carry it out", and the numbers
in between grade the space from serious concerns to "I can live with it". The
hands are looked at together, the people with low readings are asked what
would have to change, and the plan is amended and checked again if needed.

The point is to make the difference between agreement and silence visible.
Nods and an absence of protest are easy to mistake for support; a plan that
only a few people really back tends to stall once the meeting is over. A
graded, simultaneous show of hands brings those hesitations out while the plan
can still be adjusted.

The gesture is common in agile software teams, where it is also called "fist
of five" (Mike Cohn credits Jean Tabaka with showing it to him), in workplace
facilitation guides, and in schools, where teachers use the same hand scale to
let pupils show how well they have understood something. Sources do not agree
on the exact labels or on the threshold for going ahead, so each group sets
them for itself.

Fist to Five is a temperature check, not a decision rule. It tells the group
how strongly people back a plan; it does not say who decides or what happens
after a fist. Pair it with whatever decision rule the group already uses.

## When to use

- Before a plan, rule or working agreement is recorded as agreed, when you
  suspect that quiet people are going along rather than agreeing
- When earlier decisions in this group "passed" in the meeting and then were
  not carried out
- After a plan has been amended, to see whether the change actually moved
  people
- In a classroom or student council, to let every pupil show a view at once
  instead of the class following a few confident voices

## When *not* to use

- To decide between several options; it reads support for one plan at a time.
  To narrow a list, use the [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md)
- When the group needs a proper procedure for adopting a rule and handling
  reasoned objections — that is [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md);
  Fist to Five can serve as a quick first sounding within it, not as a
  replacement for its objection round
- When it is unclear who decides at all, or how much a team may decide on its
  own — clarify that first with a [DACI Matrix](DACI_Matrix.md) or
  [Delegation Poker](Delegation_Poker.md)
- On sensitive or contested matters, or where people fear consequences for
  their view: a public show of hands invites conformity. Use an anonymous poll
  instead
- When the plan itself is still vague; people cannot rate what they have not
  understood

## Facilitation outline (10 min)

1. (2 min) Agree the scale before the first round, and write it up where
   everyone can see it. A common version: fist = I cannot go along with this
   and we need to talk; 1 = major concerns; 2 = some concerns I want to raise;
   3 = I can live with it; 4 = I support it; 5 = fully behind it and willing
   to help lead it. Also agree the threshold, for example "we go ahead when
   nobody shows fewer than three".
2. (1 min) State the plan in one or two sentences, exactly as it would be
   recorded. Check that nobody has a question of understanding.
3. (1 min) Count to three; everyone shows a hand at the same moment and keeps
   it up until the facilitator has looked round. Remote groups type the number
   in the chat at a signal, or use a poll with results hidden until all have
   answered.
4. (5 min) Invite those at two or below to say what would have to change for
   them to move to three. Listen; do not argue them up. Note the concerns.
5. (1 min) If the plan is amended, check again. If the threshold is met,
   record the result and, where the group keeps one, add it to the
   [Decision Log](Decision_Log.md). If it is still not met after a second
   round, hand the matter back to the group's decision rule rather than
   repeating the count until people give in.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting without an agreed scale, so a "three" means acceptance to one person
  and doubt to another
- Letting people raise their hands one after another; the later ones follow
  the first
- Treating the count as a vote that settles the matter, when it was meant to
  reveal concerns
- Pressing the people who showed a low number to justify themselves in front
  of everyone, which teaches the group to show higher numbers next time
- Reading a room full of threes as strong backing; it means the plan is
  tolerated, which may not be enough for something people must actively carry
