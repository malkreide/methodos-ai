# Tuckman's Stages of Team Development

A lens for reading where a group stands in its own development and what it
needs from its lead at that point. The usual labels are **forming** (members
find their feet, are cautious and look to the lead for orientation),
**storming** (they jostle over roles, influence and how the work should be
done, and test the lead), **norming** (they agree, mostly unspoken, how they
work together and start to trust each other), **performing** (the group
organises much of its own work and handles disagreement without derailing)
and **adjourning** (the work ends and the group disbands).

Bruce Tuckman proposed the first four stages in 1965, while working at the
Naval Medical Research Institute. He did not observe teams himself: he
organised a collection of about fifty published articles on group
development, many of them on therapy groups and training groups, into one
sequence covering both how members relate to each other and how they handle
the task. In 1977 he and Mary Ann Jensen reviewed twenty-two later studies
and added the fifth stage. In his own retrospective account Tuckman recalls
that the journal editor first rejected the article because the studies it
drew on were not of good enough quality, and that he won acceptance by
arguing that he was drawing inferences from them rather than vouching for
them.

That history matters for how you use it. The model is a summary of what a
mixed body of studies seemed to have in common, not a tested prescription.
Treat the stages as a vocabulary for a conversation, not a diagnosis or a
timetable.

## When to use

- A team is a few weeks or months old and the early courtesy has given way to
  friction over roles, influence or the lead's decisions, and people wonder
  whether the team is failing
- A settled team has taken on new members or a new lead and old patterns have
  come apart
- A group is pleasant but nothing gets decided or done, and the lead wonders
  whether people are avoiding disagreement to keep the peace
- A lead wants to adjust how much direction they give the group as a whole,
  stepping back as it learns to run itself
- A project team is about to be disbanded and the lead wants to close its work
  deliberately

## When *not* to use

- To decide how closely to guide one particular person on one particular task;
  that is an individual question for [Situational Leadership](Situational_Leadership.md)
- When the friction is an open, hardening dispute between named people or
  factions; a stage label will not settle it, and it needs to be dealt with as
  a conflict in its own right
- When the cause is plainly outside the group: an impossible deadline, missing
  resources, a remit nobody can fulfil. Calling that "storming" hides it
- To excuse behaviour that is unacceptable at any stage, such as bullying
- As a prediction of how long a phase will last; the model gives no timings

## Facilitation outline (60 min)

Run it with the whole team, not about them. The lead can prepare alone with
steps 1 and 2.

1. (5 min) Explain the five stages in plain words, one sentence each, and say
   openly that groups move back and forth between them and that no stage is a
   failure.
2. (10 min) Each person privately notes which stage they think the team is in
   now and one concrete observation from the last few weeks that makes them
   think so, for example "we reopen decisions after the meeting".
3. (15 min) Collect the placements on a line from forming to adjourning. Talk
   about the spread before the average: where people disagree, ask what each
   of them is seeing.
4. (15 min) For the stage most people recognise, ask two questions: what does
   the group need now to work well (clearer goals and roles, room to argue
   about how to work, agreed ways of working, more autonomy, a proper ending),
   and what does it need from the lead? Write the answers down.
5. (10 min) Agree two or three changes: something the lead will do more or
   less of, and something the team will try. If the team needs explicit
   ground rules, this is the point to draft them.
6. (5 min) Agree when to look at it again, especially after the next change in
   membership.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the stages as a fixed sequence and waiting for "norming" to arrive
  on its own
- The lead labelling the team without asking it; members often place the
  group differently from the lead, and that difference is the most useful
  finding
- Suppressing every disagreement because it looks like storming; arguing about
  how to work is often how a group finds its way
- Reading a calm, polite group as performing when it is still avoiding
  difficult questions
- Forgetting that a change of members or of lead usually sends the group back
  to an earlier stage
- Skipping the ending: a team that simply stops meeting loses the chance to
  take stock and part well

## About the evidence

- The 1965 article is a narrative review of about fifty studies, many from
  therapy and training groups, rather than an empirical test; the 1977 update
  reviewed twenty-two later studies, which broadly supported the stages and
  suggested the fifth.
- Not every study in the original review found a distinct storming stage, and
  later researchers describe groups that cycle back or follow other patterns.
  Gersick (1988), observing task groups that worked to a fixed deadline,
  described a long stable first half and a marked change of approach around
  the midpoint of the group's time, rather than gradual stages.
- Bonebright (2010) is a historical review of how the model developed and
  spread into management and training practice.
- Use it to start a conversation about the group, not as evidence about it.
