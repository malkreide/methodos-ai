# Delphi Method

Put a hard question to a panel of experts in writing, show them an anonymous
summary of what the panel as a whole answered, and let each of them revise
their answer in the light of it. Repeat for a few rounds. Because nobody knows
who said what, the arguments carry the weight, not the names attached to them.

The method was developed at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s, initially to
estimate military questions on which no data existed, and was first described
in the open literature by Norman Dalkey and Olaf Helmer (1963). Harold Linstone
and Murray Turoff's edited volume (1975) carried it into policy, health and
education, where it is now one of the standard ways to build expert consensus,
for example on quality indicators, curriculum content or future skill needs.

Three features define it: **anonymity** of the panelists towards each other,
**iteration** over several rounds, and **controlled feedback**, meaning the
organiser, not the loudest panelist, decides what the panel sees between
rounds. Most applied studies add a fourth: a statistical summary of the group
answer, such as the median and the spread.

## When to use

- The question needs judgement because data does not exist yet: forecasts,
  priorities, indicators, definitions, "what will matter in ten years"
- The relevant expertise is spread across institutions, disciplines or regions
  and a joint meeting is impractical or too expensive
- Status differences are large, and in a meeting the professor, the chief
  physician or the director would set the answer before anyone else spoke
- You need a defensible, documented basis for a decision, such as a set of
  standards a ministry or professional body will publish

## When *not* to use

- When the answer can be measured or looked up; a panel is a poor substitute
  for evidence that already exists
- When the decision is needed this week: a Delphi takes weeks at the least
- When the people affected must own the decision, not just inform it; use a
  group decision procedure such as [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md)
- When the aim is to explore several possible futures rather than estimate one;
  [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md) is built for that
- When you cannot keep a panel engaged for several rounds; heavy drop-out
  undermines the result more than a smaller, committed panel would

## Facilitation outline (organiser's view, 6 to 10 weeks)

The panelist spends perhaps 30 to 60 minutes per round. The organiser's work
is where the time goes: roughly one to ten working days in total, spread over
the calendar weeks below.

1. (1–2 weeks) Frame the question and recruit the panel. Write down what
   counts as expertise and why each panelist qualifies. Aim for diversity of
   perspective, not just seniority. Decide in advance what "consensus" will
   mean (for example, 70 % rating an item 7 or higher on a 9-point scale) and
   how many rounds you will run at most.
2. (1–2 weeks) Round 1. Either open questions ("which competences will…?")
   that you then condense into statements, or a first list of statements to
   rate. Ask for a short reason with every rating.
3. (1 week) Analyse. For each item, compute median and spread, and collect
   the reasons, especially those given for outlying answers. Remove names.
4. (1–2 weeks) Round 2. Each panelist sees the group summary, their own
   previous answer and the anonymised reasons, and either confirms or revises.
   Anyone who stays far from the group is asked to explain why.
5. (1–2 weeks) Further rounds only for items still unresolved. Stop when the
   consensus rule is met, when answers stop moving, or at the round limit.
6. (1 week) Report: which items reached agreement, which did not, and the
   strongest reasons on each side of every unresolved item. Name the panel's
   composition and the response rate per round.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating convergence as truth: a panel can agree and still be wrong
- Deciding the consensus threshold after seeing the results
- Feedback that shows only numbers; without the reasons, panelists drift
  towards the median rather than reconsidering
- Panels made of one profession or one institution, which then agree quickly
  because they already shared the same view
- Losing a third of the panel by round three and not reporting it
- Dropping dissenting items from the report instead of presenting the dissent
