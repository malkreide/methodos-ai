# Timeboxing

Decide in advance how much time a piece of work gets and when, guard that slot,
work on nothing else until it ends, then look at what came out of it. The box is
fixed; what fits into it is not. When time runs out you stop, take stock and book
the next box, rather than letting the work run on into the rest of the day.

The idea has two roots. In software projects of the 1980s and 1990s, a timebox
was a fixed delivery period whose end date did not move: if not everything fit,
scope was cut. The DSDM Agile Project Framework still defines a timebox as a fixed
period at the end of which an objective has been met, and Scrum sprints work the
same way. As a personal technique the best-known form is the Pomodoro Technique,
which Francesco Cirillo began developing as a university student in the late
1980s with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer: 25 minutes of focused work on a single
task, a short break, and a longer break after four rounds. Cal Newport later
popularised time blocking, where every hour of the working day is given a job
the evening before, as the way to make room for concentrated "deep work".

What these forms share is the target problem: not deciding *what* matters, but
getting an unbroken stretch of attention for it in a day that is otherwise
chopped up by messages, calls and drop-in questions.

## When to use

- You know which task needs doing, but your day is fragmented and it never gets
  a long enough stretch of attention
- A daunting or open-ended task (writing, analysis, lesson preparation) keeps
  being postponed, or swells to fill all the time available once begun
- Studying or revising, when long sessions decay into phone-checking and
  half-attention
- A team that is interrupted constantly agrees on shared quiet hours, with no
  meetings or calls, for work that needs concentration

## When *not* to use

- In roles whose job is to respond at once: a school office, a reception desk,
  a help line, a duty or on-call shift. Blocking time there moves the cost onto
  the people waiting. At most, agree a short covered slot with a colleague.
- When the real problem is that there is more work than time. Booking boxes for
  everything only makes the overload look orderly; the conversation needed is
  about dropping or deferring work — use the [Eisenhower Matrix](Eisenhower_Matrix.md)
  for personal tasks or [MoSCoW](MoSCoW_Method.md) when a team must cut scope
  before a deadline.
- When you do not yet know which task deserves the box. Timeboxing protects a
  choice; it does not make it.
- When you are in a productive stretch of deep concentration and a fixed
  interval would break it. Treat the bell as a prompt to check in, not an order
  to stop mid-sentence.
- As a performance measure imposed on others: counting someone's completed
  intervals turns a focus aid into surveillance.

## How strong is the evidence?

Modest. Timeboxing in delivery projects rests mainly on practitioner experience.
For fixed work-and-break intervals, the most cited controlled study is small:
Biwer and colleagues (2023) compared 87 university students over one day of
self-study. Students on fixed breaks (24 minutes of work, 6 of rest, or 12 and 3)
reported less fatigue and distraction and more concentration and motivation than
students who chose their own breaks, and finished the same amount of work in less
time; mental effort and task completion did not differ. That is encouraging for
students, but it says little about office work, long-term effects or the
interruptions other people impose. Claims such as "time blockers get twice as
much done" are authors' estimates, not findings.

## Facilitation outline (personal, one working day)

1. (10 min, the evening before or first thing) Pick the one task that needs
   concentration tomorrow. Write down what "done for today" looks like in one
   sentence. Choose box lengths you can sustain: 25 minutes to begin with, up to
   90 for experienced writers.
2. (5 min) Put the boxes in the calendar as appointments, at the time of day you
   concentrate best. Book a separate box for email and messages so they have a
   place to go.
3. (5 min) Guard the box: notifications off, door or status set to busy, phone
   out of reach. Tell colleagues when you are reachable again.
4. (each box) Work on that task only. When something else comes to mind, note it
   on a slip of paper and return to the task. If someone interrupts, offer a time
   to come back to them; abandon the box only for something that cannot wait.
5. (2–5 min after each box) Stop, take a short break away from the screen, and
   note what got done. After three or four boxes take a longer break.
6. (10 min, end of day) Compare what you planned with what happened. Move
   unfinished parts into a new box deliberately; do not let them spill over
   silently. Adjust box length or timing for tomorrow.

For a team agreeing on shared quiet hours, run steps 1–3 together in a
30-minute meeting (which hours, what counts as an emergency, how urgent requests
reach the person on cover) and step 6 as a ten-minute review after two weeks.

## Common pitfalls

- Booking boxes back to back with no slack, so the first overrun wrecks the day
- Letting the box quietly extend instead of stopping and deciding consciously
- Using the box for whatever feels pressing at the time instead of the task it
  was booked for
- Treating email as something to squeeze into every gap rather than giving it
  its own box
- A manager praising quiet hours while still calling meetings into them
- Judging the method after one disrupted day; the review step is where it improves
