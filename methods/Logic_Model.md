# Logic Model

A logic model is a short chart that describes a programme in columns, read
from left to right as a chain of "if this, then that": the resources it
draws on, the activities it carries out, the outputs those activities
produce, the outcomes expected for the people or organisations it serves in
the short and the longer term, and the wider impact it hopes to contribute
to. Most versions add a row for the assumptions the plan relies on and the
external factors the programme cannot control. Its everyday job is to make
plain the difference between what the programme *does* and what is supposed
to *change*, and to give each column something that can be counted or
observed.

```
RESOURCES        ACTIVITIES          OUTPUTS               SHORT-TERM OUTCOMES       LONGER-TERM OUTCOMES     IMPACT
2 advisers,      weekly drop-in      300 consultations     young adults know their   fewer enforcement        fewer young adults
CHF 90,000,      hours; workshops    a year; 12 workshops  options and open their    orders; repayment        start working life
room at the      at vocational       with 180 apprentices  letters; first payment    plans kept after         with unmanageable
youth centre     schools                                   plans agreed              12 months                debt
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Assumptions: apprentices come forward before debts reach enforcement; creditors accept payment plans
External factors: rent levels, changes in debt-collection practice, local job market
Indicators: one per output and outcome cell, with source and date (e.g. share of plans kept at 12 months, from case files)
```

## Origin

Logic models grew out of programme evaluation in the 1970s. The
University of Wisconsin-Extension training guide (Taylor-Powell and Henert,
2008) traces the ideas to Suchman (1967) and Weiss (1972), names Bennett's
hierarchy of evidence for Extension programmes (1976) and the USAID logical
framework of the 1970s as forerunners, and cites Joseph Wholey's *Evaluation:
Promise and Performance* (1979) as the publication usually credited with the
term. Evaluators had found that the programmes they were asked to assess
often had vague goals or were not run in a way that could produce the
results claimed; a model of the intended chain showed what could sensibly
be evaluated.

In the 1990s the United States Government Performance and Results Act
(1993) and the outcome-measurement work of United Way pushed public agencies
and nonprofits to report results rather than activities, and the format
spread; the UW-Extension guide lists McLaughlin and Jordan's article "Logic
models: a tool for telling your program's performance story" (*Evaluation
and Program Planning* 22, 1999) among its sources. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's *Logic
Model Development Guide* (updated January 2004) became the standard handbook
for grantees, with its five columns from resources to impact and its advice
to "do the outcomes first". UW-Extension's own template adds the situation
the programme responds to, splits outputs into activities and participation,
and places assumptions and external factors under the chart. The
evaluation framework of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
asks for a logic model, with a short narrative, as the description of a
programme before it is evaluated.

## Logic model or theory of change?

The two are closely related, and the Kellogg guide itself treats a "theory
approach" logic model as one variant. The working difference in this
catalog:

- A [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md) starts from the long-term goal
  and works backwards to map every precondition, the causal links between
  them and the assumptions and evidence behind each link. It answers *why*
  the programme should work, and it may have several pathways and loops.
- A logic model is the compact, column-by-column summary of one specific
  programme: what goes in, what is done and delivered, what results are
  expected when. It answers *what* will be measured and reported, and is
  the document funders and boards usually ask for.

Teams that cannot explain why their activities should lead to the intended
result should build the theory of change first and condense it into a logic
model afterwards. Teams that broadly agree on the approach but report only
activity counts can start with the logic model.

## When to use

- A funder, board, council or education authority asks for a summary of
  the programme from resources to expected results, often on its own
  template
- Reports describe courses held and people reached, and someone asks what
  changed for those people
- Before a new service or project starts, to agree what to measure at each
  stage and to set up data collection from the first day
- When a funder wants many grantees to describe their projects in the same
  format so that reports can be compared
- When an evaluator joins and needs a quick shared picture of what the
  programme does and claims to achieve
- To introduce new staff, volunteers or partners to what the programme is
  for and where their part fits

## When *not* to use

- When nobody can yet explain how the activities are supposed to produce
  the long-term effect; map that with a [Theory of
  Change](Theory_of_Change.md) first
- To set a few ambitious quarterly results for teams and track them every
  few weeks; that is [OKR](OKR.md)
- To decide which product features or deliverables actually serve a business
  goal, by way of the people whose behaviour must change; that is [Impact
  Mapping](Impact_Mapping.md)
- To steer a whole organisation's strategy through a stable set of measures
  across several perspectives; that is the [Balanced
  Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md)
- For a school team working from its own assessment results to a change in
  teaching; that is the [Data Wise Improvement
  Process](Data_Wise_Improvement_Process.md)
- To judge whether the programme is worth what it costs; use [Cost-Benefit
  Analysis](Cost_Benefit_Analysis.md)

## Facilitation outline (one workshop of about 3 hours)

Invite the people who run the programme, someone who handles its data or
reporting and, where possible, one or two participants or partners. Bring
the funder's template if there is one, and a large blank chart with the
column headings.

1. **(15 min) Situation and purpose.** Write in two or three sentences what
   problem the programme responds to and for whom. Everything in the chart
   must relate to it.
2. **(40 min) Results first.** Following the Kellogg advice, start on the
   right: what should be different for participants within a year or so
   (knowledge, skills, attitudes, first actions), what later (behaviour,
   situation), and what wider change the programme contributes to. Reject
   any card that describes something the programme does.
3. **(25 min) Outputs.** What will the activities produce, and whom will
   they reach: numbers of sessions, consultations, materials, participants
   of which groups.
4. **(30 min) Activities and resources.** List what the programme actually
   does and what it needs to do so: staff, money, rooms, partners,
   equipment, existing evidence. Check that every activity leads to an
   output and that every outcome is served by at least one activity.
5. **(20 min) Read it as "if … then".** Read the chart aloud from left to
   right. Where a step sounds like a leap, either add the missing
   intermediate outcome or write down the assumption it depends on.
6. **(20 min) Assumptions and external factors.** Name the beliefs about
   participants, partners and resources that must hold, and the outside
   conditions that could help or hinder. Mark the ones to watch.
7. **(30 min) Indicators.** For each output and outcome, agree one or two
   indicators, the source of the data, who collects it and when. Strike out
   outcomes nobody could ever observe, or reword them.
8. **(Afterwards)** One person redraws the chart cleanly. Review it when
   data comes in, at least once a year, and change it when the programme
   changes.

## Common pitfalls

- Filling the outcome columns with activities ("hold 12 workshops") or
  outputs ("180 apprentices reached")
- Writing the chart alone for the application deadline, so the staff who run
  the programme never recognise it
- Leaving out the assumptions and external factors, then reading every
  change in the figures as the programme's doing
- Claiming impacts that lie far outside the programme's reach or time frame
- Choosing indicators only because the data already exists
- Treating the chart as fixed; it should change when evidence or the
  programme changes
