# Restorative Circle

A facilitated meeting after someone has been harmed, in which the person who
caused the harm, the people affected and those who support them sit together
and each answers the same few questions in turn: what happened, who has been
affected and in what way, and what needs to happen now to make things right.
The meeting ends with an agreement the participants work out themselves. The
aim is not to decide on a penalty but to repair what the incident damaged
between people who will keep sharing a classroom, an office or a club.

The approach comes from restorative justice, a way of thinking about
wrongdoing that asks what harm was done and who has to repair it rather than
only which rule was broken and what punishment fits; Howard Zehr's *Changing
Lenses* (1990) is its best-known statement. The scripted form most schools
and organisations meet today traces back to family group conferences
introduced in New Zealand in 1989, which an Australian police officer, Terry
O'Connell, adapted in 1991 to divert young offenders from court. The
International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP) in Pennsylvania
spread it as the *restorative conference* and describes a less formal
*restorative circle* that uses some of the same questions without a strict
script. Both are covered here; the preparation and the questions are the same,
and the formal version suits more serious incidents.

The name is also used for a distinct process that Dominic Barter developed
with residents of Rio de Janeiro's favelas from the mid-1990s
([restorativecircles.org](https://www.restorativecircles.org/)); this entry describes the conference-and-circle tradition set out by
the IIRP and the UK Restorative Justice Council.

## Core elements

- **Voluntary participation.** Nobody is ordered to attend. The person who
  caused the harm must at least broadly accept that they did it; the meeting
  is not the place to establish facts.
- **Separate preparation.** The facilitator meets each participant, or each
  family, beforehand: explains the process, hears their account, checks that
  meeting is safe and wanted, and agrees who they will bring for support.
- **A fixed order of speaking.** The facilitator asks the person responsible
  first, then those harmed, then their supporters, then the supporters of the
  person responsible. Nobody interrupts. Only after everyone has spoken does
  the conversation open up.
- **Restorative questions.** For the person responsible: what happened, what
  were you thinking at the time, what have you thought since, who has been
  affected and how, what do you need to do to make it right. For those
  harmed: what did you think when you realised what had happened, what impact
  has it had on you and others, what has been hardest, what needs to happen
  now.
- **A written agreement.** What will be done, by whom and by when, and how
  everyone will know it has happened. The facilitator does not propose the
  content.

## When to use

- Someone has been hurt by a specific, acknowledged act (bullying, a
  humiliating remark, damage to a shared space, a fight) and the people
  involved will keep living, learning or working together
- A sanction has been imposed, or would be, but on its own it will not repair
  the relationship or the trust of the wider group
- Those affected want to be heard by the person responsible and to have a say
  in what happens next
- A school, youth service or workplace wants to bring someone back into the
  group after an incident instead of only excluding them

## When *not* to use

- **Where there is ongoing danger, threats or abuse.** If someone is still at
  risk, protection comes first: follow your safeguarding, child-protection or
  workplace-safety procedures and involve the designated staff. A meeting can
  expose the harmed person to more pressure
- **Where the power imbalance is severe**, such as a manager and an
  apprentice, an adult and a child, or a pattern of coercive behaviour,
  unless experienced facilitators judge in preparation that it can be
  balanced with supporters and safeguards
- **For criminal matters and serious misconduct** that need a police report,
  a disciplinary or employment procedure, or legal advice. Restorative work
  can accompany or follow those processes, never replace them, and should run
  through services trained for that setting
- **When anyone is pushed to take part.** A harmed person who agrees to
  please the school, or a culprit who attends only to avoid a sanction, turns
  the meeting into a performance or a second injury
- **When the facts are disputed** and the person responsible denies the act.
  Clarify first through the appropriate process
- **Without trained facilitation and time to prepare.** Running it on the
  spot with the whole class, without preparation talks, is a common way to
  make things worse
- **As a promise of better outcomes across a whole school.** In a randomised
  trial in Pittsburgh's public schools (Augustine et al., RAND, 2018), a
  whole-school restorative programme reduced suspensions and improved the
  school climate as teachers rated it, but academic results did not improve
  and fell in grades 6 to 8, and arrests did not decrease. An English trial
  of a whole-school approach that included restorative practice (Bonell et
  al., *The Lancet*, 2018) found a small reduction in bullying and no effect
  on aggression. Use the method to repair specific harm, and expect a single
  meeting to change relationships, not school-wide statistics

## Facilitation outline (meeting about 90 minutes, preparation beforehand)

**Preparation (days before, 30 to 60 minutes per party).** Meet the person
responsible and their supporters, then those harmed and theirs, separately.
Check that the act is acknowledged, that everyone attends voluntarily and
safely, who will come, and where everyone will sit. Agree how the facilitator
will stop the meeting if it becomes unsafe. Arrange a quiet room with chairs
in a circle and no table between people.

1. (5 min) **Opening.** Welcome everyone, name who is present and why. State
   the purpose: to understand who was affected by what happened and to agree
   how to put it right, not to judge anyone as a person. Remind everyone that
   participation is voluntary and set the rule of speaking one at a time.
2. (15 min) **The person responsible speaks.** Ask the questions for the
   person responsible in order. Let them answer in their own words; do not
   prompt an apology.
3. (25 min) **Those harmed speak.** Ask each harmed person the questions for
   those harmed, then their supporters how it has affected them. Allow
   silence and emotion; do not hurry to a solution.
4. (10 min) **Supporters of the person responsible speak.** How did they
   learn of it, how has it affected them, what has been hardest.
5. (5 min) **Response.** Give the person responsible the chance to say
   anything now that they have heard everyone. An apology offered here
   carries weight; one demanded by the facilitator does not.
6. (20 min) **Agreement.** Ask those harmed what they would like to come out
   of the meeting, then discuss it with everyone. Write down concrete,
   achievable steps, who does what by when, and who checks. Everyone who
   agrees signs or confirms it.
7. (10 min) **Closing.** Thank everyone, invite a short round on how they feel
   now, and offer informal time with refreshments; much of the repair happens
   in those minutes.

**Follow-up (two to six weeks later).** Check with each party whether the
agreement has been kept. If not, meet again or hand the matter back to the
formal process.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping the preparation talks because "everyone already knows what
  happened", then discovering in the meeting that the harmed person did not
  want to come or that the account is disputed
- A facilitator who is also the head teacher, line manager or judge of the
  case and cannot appear neutral; bring in someone from outside the line
  of authority
- Using the meeting to extract a scripted apology, which harmed people
  recognise immediately
- Letting the supporters turn it into a trial of the person responsible's
  character
- Writing an agreement full of vague promises ("will be nicer") with no date
  or check
- Treating the circle as a replacement for the school's or employer's duty to
  protect, report or discipline

## How it differs from its neighbours

- [Principled Negotiation](Principled_Negotiation.md) — two sides with
  competing interests bargain towards a deal on an equal footing; here one
  side has harmed the other, the harm is acknowledged, and the meeting is
  about consequences and amends, not a trade
- [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md) — one person tells another how a specific
  behaviour came across, in a short conversation; the circle brings in
  everyone affected and supporters for both sides and needs preparation and
  a facilitator
- [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md) — peers advise
  a colleague about a difficult case while the people in it are absent; in
  the circle the people involved are in the room and speak for themselves
- [Fishbowl](Fishbowl.md) — a few people discuss an issue while an audience
  watches; the circle is closed and confidential, and everyone in it has a
  stake in the incident
- [Johari Window](Johari_Window.md) — a group gets to know how its members
  see each other; it assumes no specific harm and no one is responsible for
  repairing anything
- [Team Charter](Team_Charter.md) — a group agrees rules of collaboration
  before trouble starts; the circle responds after a particular incident has
  already hurt someone
