# Morphological Box (Zwicky Box)

Break a design problem into the few features any solution has to settle, list
the realistic alternatives for each feature in a grid, and treat every pick of
one alternative per feature as a candidate design. Then check the alternatives
against each other in pairs and strike out every combination that contains a
pair which cannot coexist. What is left is a map of the consistent designs,
including ones nobody in the room had thought of.

The approach was developed by Fritz Zwicky, a Swiss astrophysicist at the
California Institute of Technology, who used it from the 1940s onwards for
problems as different as classifying astronomical objects and designing jet
and rocket engines, and set it out in *Discovery, Invention, Research through
the Morphological Approach* (1969). From the 1990s Tom Ritchey and colleagues
in Sweden extended it, as "general morphological analysis", to policy,
planning and scenario work, and named the pairwise step "cross-consistency
assessment". In German-speaking workshop practice it is widely known simply as
the *morphologischer Kasten*.

## When to use

- A new offer, service, schedule or product has several features that can each
  be decided in more than one way, and the discussion keeps returning to the
  same two or three variants
- You suspect the obvious variants are obvious only because they are familiar,
  and want evidence that nothing workable has been overlooked
- The features are qualitative (who delivers, where, how often, for whom), so
  modelling or scoring them with numbers is not meaningful
- A group needs a shared, written picture of the design space before deciding
  which configurations deserve a closer look

## When *not* to use

- When you need ideas for a single open question rather than a structure for a
  many-sided one; a free idea round such as
  [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md) is faster and less constrained
- When the alternatives are already known and the task is to pick one; that is
  a job for a [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md) or
  another evaluation method. The box produces options, not a decision
- When the features are not really independent, for example when choosing the
  location already fixes the group size. Merge such features first, or the
  grid fills with combinations that only look different
- With more than about six or seven features, or many alternatives per feature:
  the number of combinations multiplies with every column (five features with
  four alternatives each already give 1,024), and working through them by hand
  stops being realistic. Narrow the scope or use dedicated software
- When the real need is to understand a problem or its causes rather than to
  design a solution; break the question down with an [Issue Tree](Issue_Tree.md)
  instead
- For radical departures: the box can only recombine what was written into it,
  so a genuinely new kind of answer must come from elsewhere

## Facilitation outline (about 3 hours)

1. (15 min) State the design task in one sentence and agree its boundary: what
   the result must achieve and which questions are already settled and stay
   out of the grid.
2. (35 min) Collect the features any solution must decide, for a timetable for
   instance lesson length, group size, room and subject mix. Merge overlapping
   features and drop those that do not change the result. Aim for four to six.
   Write each as a row heading.
3. (35 min) For each feature, list three to five distinct, concrete
   alternatives, including at least one that nobody currently favours. Check
   that each feature's alternatives exclude one another and together cover the
   realistic range.
4. (45 min) Assess consistency in pairs. Take each pair of alternatives from
   different features and ask only whether they can coexist, not whether they
   are desirable. Mark the pairs that are logically impossible or clearly
   impractical, and note the reason for each. Keep preferences out of this
   step; Ritchey recommends deciding what is possible before what is wanted.
5. (30 min) Strike out every combination containing a marked pair. Draw paths
   through the grid for the remaining configurations, starting with the
   familiar variants and then deliberately looking for unfamiliar ones. Give
   the promising ones a short name.
6. (20 min) Choose a handful of configurations for further work and agree how
   they will be evaluated, tested or costed, and by whom.

For small grids, paper or a whiteboard is enough: features as rows,
alternatives as cells, and coloured lines for each configuration. Larger grids
need a spreadsheet or specialist software for the consistency step. Ritchey's
facilitated projects worked with groups of six or seven subject specialists
and took between two and fifteen workshop days, so the outline above suits a
modest design question, not a full policy model.

## Common pitfalls

- Mixing features of different levels, such as "location" next to "cost",
  where cost is a consequence of the other choices rather than a choice
- Alternatives that overlap ("small group", "fewer than ten") so that the same
  configuration appears twice under different names
- Letting preferences into the consistency check, which quietly removes the
  unfamiliar configurations the method is meant to surface
- Stopping at the familiar variants once they reappear in the grid instead of
  examining the unexpected ones
- Treating the surviving configurations as a recommendation; they are
  consistent, not necessarily good
