# Data Wise Improvement Process

A school team works through eight steps that lead from assessment results to
a change in teaching, and then starts again. The steps fall into three
phases. In **Prepare**, the team sets up the conditions for working together
and learns what its assessments can and cannot tell it. In **Inquire**, it
moves from a broad overview of results to students' actual work, names one
problem of learning that many students share, and then looks at its own
teaching to find the problem of practice behind it. In **Act**, it plans an
instructional change, decides in advance how it will see whether students
learn more, carries the plan out and checks. The next cycle returns to the
inquiry phase rather than to the beginning.

The process grew out of a partnership between the Harvard Graduate School of
Education and Boston Public Schools. From 2001 the economist Richard Murnane
worked with the district on making assessment results accessible to teachers
and principals; he then brought together school administrators, faculty and
graduate students, among them Kathryn Parker Boudett and Elizabeth City, who
over about two years developed the eight steps. Boudett, City and Murnane
edited the first book, *Data Wise* (Harvard Education Press, 2005), followed
by a revised and expanded edition in 2013. A third edition by Boudett and
Candice Bocala (2025) adds a set of equity questions to every step. The
project describes Data Wise as a way of working rather than a programme to
implement, and Boudett contrasts it with what she calls the "low road" of
high-stakes testing: raising scores instead of examining learning and
teaching.

What sets it apart from a generic improvement loop is the order in which a
team narrows its focus:

- **From results to students' work.** A test score shows *that* students
  struggle, not *how*. The team does not plan anything on the basis of the
  overview; it goes on to look at samples of student work, classroom tasks and
  what students say, until it can state a *learner-centred problem* in terms of
  a specific skill or understanding.
- **From students to teaching.** Before choosing a remedy, teachers examine
  how the skill is currently taught, often by observing each other's lessons,
  and restate the learner-centred problem as a *problem of practice*: something
  about instruction that the team itself can change. Explanations that point
  only at students or their families do not count as an answer.
- **Measures before action.** The action plan is written together with
  short-, medium- and long-term evidence of student learning, so the team knows
  before it starts what it will look at to decide whether the change worked.

## When to use

- Test results, assessment reports and student records arrive regularly, but
  staff only glance at them and nothing changes in classrooms
- Meetings about results consist of a leader presenting charts by class or
  subject, and teachers leave without a shared question
- A department or year-group team has noticed a weak area in assessment
  results and wants to understand what students actually misunderstand before
  deciding what to change
- A district or education authority wants its schools' teams to share one
  vocabulary and sequence of steps for examining evidence of learning
- A school wants teachers to take collective responsibility for a learning
  problem rather than explaining weak results by students' backgrounds

## When *not* to use

- **When data is already overwhelming everyone.** If a school has more
  dashboards, item analyses and reports than it can read, adding a data
  process can deepen the overload. The method only helps if the team is
  willing to put most of the data aside, start from a deliberately small
  overview and spend most of its time on students' work and on teaching. If
  that is not possible, cut the reporting first
- **When scores are used to judge, rank or pay.** Where test results drive
  league tables, performance pay or sanctions, teams tend to aim at the score
  rather than at the learning it is meant to represent, and observation of
  teaching is experienced as inspection. The process assumes that observation
  is non-evaluative and that assessment results are a starting point for
  questions, not a verdict on teachers. If the system cannot offer that, be
  open about the risk or choose a lower-stakes starting point
- **As a proven route to higher achievement.** The evidence is thin. The US
  What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on using student achievement data
  (Hamilton et al., 2009) rated the research behind making data part of an
  ongoing cycle of instructional improvement as *minimal*. An IES-funded
  randomised trial of Data Wise, planned by SRI International from 2020
  for about fifty schools, could not recruit schools as planned during the
  pandemic and was redesigned as a study of a toolkit for measuring team
  inquiry, so it reports no achievement effect. The project's own accounts rest
  on case studies, testimonials and reach figures. Use it because the steps
  make sense for your team, not because a score gain has been demonstrated
- **Without protected time and leadership backing.** A cycle needs regular
  team meetings over months, and someone with authority to open classrooms and
  free teachers to observe. Fitted into the last minutes of a staff meeting, it
  shrinks to looking at charts
- **For a single, urgent problem with one class or one student.** That is
  better handled by [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md)
  or by the teacher concerned
- **Where the system has no assessments worth reading.** The method needs some
  evidence of learning across classes. A team can use student work and teacher
  assessments instead of standardised tests, but then has to create a common
  task first

## Facilitation outline (one cycle over a school year, team of 4 to 12)

The team usually includes several teachers, a school leader and, where
available, an instructional coach or subject lead. Name one facilitator for the
whole cycle. Meetings are 60–90 minutes unless stated.

**Prepare**

1. (2–3 meetings, weeks 1–4) **Organise for collaborative work.** Agree who is
   on the team, when it meets and for how long, and write meeting norms, for
   example: assume good intentions, back statements with evidence, ask before
   concluding. Make an inventory of the assessments and other data the school
   already has and who holds them.
2. (1–2 meetings, weeks 3–6) **Build assessment literacy.** For each source
   the team is likely to use, work out what it measures, what it does not, and
   how far small differences between classes can be trusted. Practise reading
   one report together.

**Inquire**

3. (1 meeting, about week 6) **Create a data overview.** A small group prepares
   two or three simple displays from one assessment that show a pattern worth
   discussing, not a complete analysis. The whole staff or team looks at them
   in two rounds, first stating only what they notice, then what they wonder,
   and agrees one priority question about student learning.
4. (2–3 meetings, weeks 7–10) **Dig into student data.** Bring several kinds
   of evidence on the priority question: student work samples, classroom tasks,
   item-level results, brief conversations with students. Look for a problem
   many students share and state it precisely as a learner-centred problem, for
   example "students identify characters and events but cannot state a text's
   central idea in their own words".
5. (2–3 meetings plus observations, weeks 10–14) **Examine instruction.**
   Agree what effective teaching of this skill looks like, then observe each
   other's lessons or watch recorded ones with that focus, without evaluating
   the teacher. Compare what was seen with the shared picture and restate the
   learner-centred problem as a problem of practice the team can work on.

**Act**

6. (1–2 meetings, weeks 14–16) **Develop an action plan.** Choose one
   instructional strategy that addresses the problem of practice. Write who
   does what by when, what support teachers get (coaching, shared planning,
   materials) and how the team will know the strategy is being used.
7. (1 meeting, about week 16) **Plan to assess progress.** Set short-term
   measures (classroom tasks over weeks), medium-term ones (common assessments
   over a term) and long-term ones (the next annual results), with explicit
   goals for student learning.
8. (rest of the year, a meeting every 3–4 weeks) **Act and assess.** Carry out
   the plan, look at the short-term evidence at each meeting, adjust, and record
   what happened. At the end, decide whether to deepen the same problem or
   return to step 3 with a new priority question.

Steps 1 and 2 take longest the first time; in later cycles the team returns to
them only when its membership or its assessments change.

## Common pitfalls

- Spending the whole cycle on data displays and never reaching students' work
  or classrooms
- Presenting every chart the school has at step 3; a large overview produces
  discussion but no shared question
- Stating the learner-centred problem as a topic ("reading", "algebra")
  rather than as a specific thing students cannot yet do
- Skipping step 5 and jumping from a student problem straight to buying a
  programme or adding lessons, so the team's own teaching is never examined
- Letting observation in step 5 turn into appraisal, which ends honest
  discussion of practice quickly
- Choosing several strategies at once, so that the team cannot tell which one
  made a difference
- Measuring progress only with the next annual test, which arrives too late to
  adjust anything
- Treating the cycle as a one-year project rather than a way of working that
  continues when staff change

## How it differs from its neighbours

- [PDCA Cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md) — the general loop of planning, trying, checking
  and adjusting a change, usable for any process. Data Wise is a specific
  sequence for schools that spends most of its effort *before* the plan: on
  reading assessments, studying student work and examining teaching. Its act
  phase can be run as a series of small PDCA trials
- [Lesson Study](Lesson_Study.md) — teachers jointly design one lesson, watch
  how pupils respond and refine it. Data Wise starts from school-wide
  assessment results and works towards a problem of practice; a team could use
  lesson study inside steps 5 and 8
- [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) and [Ishikawa Diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md) —
  tools for tracing one problem to its causes in a single session. Data Wise is
  a year-long team process; a team might use them while digging into student
  data, but it deliberately looks at evidence of learning and teaching rather
  than collecting possible causes
- [Pareto Analysis](Pareto_Analysis.md) — counts which few categories account
  for most occurrences; it can help to build the data overview but says nothing
  about why students struggle or what to change in teaching
- [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md) and [OKR](OKR.md) — ways for
  leadership to steer an organisation with objectives and measures. Data Wise
  does not set organisational targets; it is a teaching team's inquiry into one
  learning problem
- [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) — looks back on one event the
  team has just been through. Data Wise looks at patterns of learning across
  many students and plans forward
