# World Café

Seat a large group at small tables of four or five, give everyone a question
worth talking about, and after a round of conversation send people off to new
tables while one person stays behind to brief the newcomers. Over three or
more rounds, each person meets several different small groups, and the ideas
from one table travel with them to the next. A closing conversation with the
whole room then gathers the themes that kept turning up.

The format was first improvised in 1995 at the home of Juanita Brown and David
Isaacs in Mill Valley, California, when rain moved a planned dialogue among
about two dozen business and academic leaders indoors onto small tables
covered in paper. Brown and Isaacs later set it out, with the practitioners
around them, in *The World Café: Shaping Our Futures Through Conversations
That Matter* (2005). "World Café" is the name under which the World Café
Community Foundation, a non-profit based in California, maintains and teaches
the method; its website publishes the design principles and a free hosting
guide under a Creative Commons Attribution licence. This entry describes the
method in its own words and points to the Foundation for the original
material. Many organisers run events under other names ("knowledge café",
"conversation café") with a similar structure; those variants differ in detail
and are not covered here.

## When to use

- A large gathering (a school community evening, a citizens' participation
  event, a staff conference) where people should talk with each other rather
  than listen to presentations
- Two or three important, open questions that nobody can answer alone and that
  benefit from many different perspectives meeting
- The aim is to explore and understand a topic together, and to surface shared
  themes, before any plan or decision is drafted
- Mixed groups (parents and teachers, residents and officials, managers and
  front-line staff) who rarely sit at the same table

## When *not* to use

- You need a decision or a ranked list at the end; follow the café with a
  decision method such as [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md)
  or a prioritisation step
- You only have fifteen minutes and one question, and the goal is simply to
  hear from every person in the room: [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) does
  that faster
- A small team's regular meeting with topics proposed on the spot by the
  participants is better served by Lean Coffee
- The questions have already been answered by management and the event would
  only be for show; participants notice quickly and trust suffers
- You need to know who has influence over a change and how to involve them;
  map that first with a [Stakeholder Map](Stakeholder_Map.md)

## Facilitation outline (about 2 hours)

Timings follow the hosting guidance on the World Café Community Foundation's
website: rounds of about twenty minutes, at least three of them. Plan the
questions in advance; they matter more than any other preparation.

1. (Before) Settle the purpose and write two or three open questions, ideally
   building on each other ("What do we value most about…", "What could…",
   "What would it take to…"). Set up tables for four or five with paper on
   each and pens; something to make the room feel less like a lecture hall
   helps.
2. (10 min) Welcome. Explain why people are here, how the rounds work and a
   few table habits: speak from your own experience, listen for what is new,
   jot or doodle ideas on the paper, link what you hear to what you heard
   before.
3. (20 min) Round 1 on the first question. Each table chooses a host who will
   stay put.
4. (20 min) Round 2. Everyone except the host moves to a different table,
   ideally away from the people they came with. The host summarises the
   previous conversation in two minutes; newcomers add what they brought from
   their tables. Use the same question or the next one.
5. (20 min) Round 3, the same way. Optionally, people return to their first
   table to combine what they each heard.
6. (30 min) Harvest with the whole room: ask what themes, surprises or
   questions kept coming up across tables, rather than having each table
   report. Capture them visibly, on a wall, a graphic recording or a shared
   document.
7. (Afterwards) Collect the table papers, write up the patterns and tell
   participants what will happen next.

Online, breakout rooms stand in for tables and a shared board for the paper;
a host per room stays while the others are reshuffled. The movement between
rounds loses some of its energy, so keep rounds shorter and the groups small.

## Common pitfalls

- Questions that are closed, too abstract or already answered, which produce
  polite agreement instead of real conversation
- Opening with a long keynote so that the conversations start late and get cut
- Table hosts who lecture the newcomers instead of briefing them in two minutes
- People moving as a group from table to table, so no new perspectives meet
- A harvest in which every table reads out its notes; this takes too long and
  hides the patterns
- No follow-up: participants who never hear what became of their ideas will
  not come to the next event
