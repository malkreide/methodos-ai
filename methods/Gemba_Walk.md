# Gemba Walk

Leave the meeting room, go to the place where the work is done, and look at it
before you decide anything about it. Watch the work as it happens, ask the
people doing it what makes it hard, and keep your hands in your pockets: the
point is to understand, not to inspect and not to fix things on the spot.

*Gemba* is Japanese for "the actual place". In lean management it means the
place where value is created: the shop floor, but just as well a service
counter, a hospital ward, a school secretariat, a canteen kitchen or a
warehouse. The practice comes from the Toyota Production System. Taiichi Ohno
is said to have drawn a chalk circle on the factory floor, put an engineer
inside it and told him to watch until he could say what he saw, sometimes for
hours. Toyota's former chairman Fujio Cho summed up the attitude as "go see,
ask why, show respect". Jim Womack's essays, collected as *Gemba Walks*, helped
carry the practice into offices, hospitals and government.

## When to use

- Decisions about how work should be done are made from reports, dashboards and
  meetings, and the people making them have not seen that work for a long time
- Front-line staff say new rules or procedures do not fit their reality, and
  management cannot tell whether that is resistance or a real problem
- Before redesigning a process, so that the redesign starts from what actually
  happens rather than from the documented procedure
- A new manager takes over sites or teams and knows them only from figures
- Public administration and health care: managers of a service centre, an
  emergency department or a ward who need to see whether a plan made centrally
  can be carried out as intended at the counter or the bedside

## When *not* to use

- **When trust is low**, for example during job cuts or after a conflict. A
  manager who turns up to watch will be taken for an inspector. Say beforehand
  why you are coming, that you are looking at the system and not at people, and
  stay away from performance judgements; if even that will not be believed,
  repair the relationship first.
- **When you intend to fix things while you are there.** Handing out
  instructions on the floor goes over the head of the local supervisor and
  teaches staff to wait for the boss. Note what you see and follow it up with
  the team afterwards. Something unsafe is the obvious exception.
- **As a one-off.** A single visit changes nothing and, if nothing follows,
  leaves people more cynical than before. The value lies in a regular rhythm and
  in visibly acting on what you were told.
- **As a substitute for data.** People work differently while a manager is
  watching, and an hour shows you what happens, not how often. Use what you see
  to ask better questions of the figures, for example with a
  [Pareto Analysis](Pareto_Analysis.md).
- **When most of the work happens on screens or in people's heads.** A short
  walk-through of an office shows little. Sit with one person for several hours
  and ask them to explain as they go, or let them teach you to do the task
  yourself (Roser, 2016).
- **When you need proof that it will pay off.** The published evidence is
  mostly case reports and practitioner accounts. A five-year study of
  gemba-inspired safety rounds in a Spanish public health area found gains in
  staff ratings of management support and feedback, alongside a decline in some
  other dimensions such as perceived safety (Lopez-Picazo et al., 2025).

**Neighbouring methods.** If the problem is that work takes far longer end to
end than the hands-on effort, map the flow with
[Value Stream Mapping](Value_Stream_Mapping.md). If it is what the service is
like for the person receiving it, use a
[Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md). To find the cause of a
specific, known failure, use [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md) or an
[Ishikawa diagram](Ishikawa_Diagram.md). To learn from an event that has just
ended, run an [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md); to compare your
practice with other organisations, use [Benchmarking](Benchmarking.md). A gemba
walk often comes before these: it tells you which of them is worth doing.

## Facilitation outline (about 90 min including preparation and follow-up)

1. **Prepare (10 min).** Choose one place and one question, such as "what
   happens when a citizen arrives at the counter without the right documents?"
   Tell the local supervisor and team when you will come and why, and ask the
   supervisor to accompany you. Go alone or with one or two people, never with
   an entourage.
2. **Arrive (5 min).** Greet people, repeat the purpose, and make clear that you
   are there to learn how the work goes, not to check anyone.
3. **Observe (20–40 min).** Stand where you can see without getting in the way
   and follow several rounds of the real work. Note what you see, not what you
   think of it: waiting, searching, re-entering data, workarounds, handwritten
   notes taped to screens, things fetched from elsewhere.
4. **Ask (15 min).** When people have a moment, ask open questions: "What are
   you doing right now?", "What usually gets in the way?", "What did you have to
   work around today?", "What would make this easier?" Ask what happens before
   asking why. Do not correct, judge or promise solutions.
5. **Close (5 min).** Thank people, say back what you heard, and tell them what
   will happen next and when they will hear from you.
6. **Follow up (15 min, same day).** Write down your observations separately from
   your interpretations. Agree with the supervisor on one or two points to
   pursue with the team, and report back to the staff what became of them.
   Repeat the walk on a regular schedule.

## Common pitfalls

- Turning the walk into a tour, where the host shows the best corner and the
  visitor leaves after ten minutes believing they understand the work
- Carrying an audit checklist, which turns observing into inspecting
- Solving problems on the spot over the local supervisor's head
- Blaming individuals for what is a problem in how the work is set up
- Walking only when something has gone wrong, so that a visit signals trouble
- Collecting suggestions and never reporting back what happened to them
