# Ecocycle Planning

Most organisations are good at starting things and poor at ending them. Each
year brings a new programme, a new offer, a new pilot; very few are ever
formally closed. After a while people are spread across far more activities
than anyone chose deliberately, long-standing ones continue because nobody put
their end on the agenda, and recent ones never receive enough attention to
take root. Ecocycle Planning gives a group a way to look at all of its
activities together, as things that are born, mature, decline and make room
for something new, and to agree what to let go of, what to sustain and what to
back.

```
            ┌──────────────── [rigidity trap] ────────────────┐
            │                                                 ▼
  birth ──► maturity                          creative destruction
    ▲                                                         │
    │                                                         ▼
    └──────── [scarcity / poverty trap] ◄──────────── renewal ┘
```

*Drawn in practice as a figure-eight or infinity loop. Activities move from
birth into maturity, through creative destruction into renewal, and from
renewal back into birth. The two traps sit at the hand-overs where activities
most often get stuck.*

## Origin

The structure was adapted by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless as one of
their Liberating Structures, a collection of facilitation formats described on
liberatingstructures.com and in their book *The Surprising Power of Liberating
Structures* (Liberating Structures Press, 2014). The Liberating Structures
website points to the complexity work of Brenda Zimmerman; the International
Training Centre of the ILO credits Zimmerman and the management writer David
Hurst.

Behind the figure-eight is the ecologist C. S. Holling's **adaptive cycle**.
Holling and colleagues described ecosystems and social-ecological systems as
passing repeatedly through four phases: exploitation, a phase of rapid
growth; conservation, a long phase in which resources accumulate and the
system becomes tightly connected; a sudden release; and a reorganisation that
opens space for something new. Nested cycles of different speeds form what they called a
**panarchy** (Holling, 2001; Gunderson and Holling, 2002). The resilience
literature also describes two ways in which such a system can get stuck: a
**rigidity trap**, where strong, self-reinforcing control prevents the
flexibility to adapt, and a **poverty trap**, where connections are so loose
that ideas and resources cannot be mobilised (Carpenter and Brock, 2008).

Ecocycle Planning borrows these ideas as an analogy. Its four phase names,
**birth**, **maturity**, **creative destruction** and **renewal**, map onto
Holling's exploitation, conservation, release and reorganisation. The current
Liberating Structures description calls the second trap the **scarcity trap**;
older material and the ecological literature call it the poverty trap.

## The phases and the traps

- **Birth.** Activities that are being tried out or have just started. They
  need protection, experimentation and some early resources.
- **Maturity.** Established activities that run reliably and that others
  depend on. The work here is to keep them effective and efficient.
- **Creative destruction.** Activities that are winding down or should be.
  Ending them releases people, money and attention.
- **Renewal.** Ideas, conversations and relationships that are not yet
  activities. The work here is to connect people and prepare the ground.
- **Rigidity trap** (between maturity and creative destruction). Activities
  that are kept going after they stopped being useful, because of habit,
  loyalty, sunk cost or because nobody has the mandate to stop them.
- **Scarcity or poverty trap** (between renewal and birth). Ideas that people
  agree are promising but that never get the time, people or money to start.

A healthy portfolio has something in every phase. One crowded with mature
activities and empty elsewhere is likely heading for a rigidity trap; one full
of ideas and short of anything established may be in a scarcity trap.

## When to use

- A school, public body, nonprofit or company has accumulated many programmes
  and offers over the years and has never systematically decided what to end
- A new strategy or a leadership change is the occasion to look at everything
  the organisation runs before adding more
- Staff are spread thin and suspect that some activities persist out of habit
  while worthwhile newer ones are starved
- A board or funder wants to see its grants or projects as a whole rather than
  one at a time
- An individual wants to review their own commitments; the figure-eight works
  for one person with a sheet of paper

## When *not* to use

- To compare a list of candidate items and put them in order. The ecocycle
  shows where activities stand in their life, not which one matters most; use
  [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md) or the [MoSCoW Method](MoSCoW_Method.md) for that
- To collect quick feedback on how a team works together. That is what
  [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) is for; the ecocycle is about
  the activities an organisation runs, not about the team's own behaviour
- To choose a growth direction for products or services in markets.
  [Ansoff Matrix](Ansoff_Matrix.md) compares those paths by risk
- To assess the organisation's position against its environment. Use
  [SWOT](SWOT.md) for strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats
- To decide whether to build, buy or outsource components of a system.
  [Wardley Mapping](Wardley_Mapping.md) tracks how technical components evolve
  toward commodities; the ecocycle does not look at markets or supply
- To help people through the loss once something has been ended. The ecocycle
  can decide that a programme closes; [Bridges' Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md)
  is about the people who go through that ending
- When nobody in the room can actually end anything. If the decisions sit with
  someone who is absent and has not agreed to act on the result, the session
  produces a list of wishes. Agree beforehand who decides and how
- When the group will not engage with the metaphor. Some groups find the
  ecological language unfamiliar or odd; explain it plainly or use simpler
  labels such as "starting", "running well", "should end" and "ideas waiting"
- As a scientific diagnosis. The adaptive cycle is a theory about ecosystems,
  and its use for organisations is an analogy. The evidence for Ecocycle
  Planning itself is practitioners' experience and facilitation guides, not
  controlled studies

## Facilitation outline (100 min)

Prepare a large drawing of the figure-eight on a wall or an online whiteboard,
a small copy on paper for each participant, and sticky notes. Decide in
advance what counts as one activity (a programme, a service, a project, a
recurring event) and who will take the decisions afterwards. The groups follow
the [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) pattern.

1. (10 min) Introduce the phases and the traps with two or three examples the
   group knows, including at least one thing that was ended well in the past.
   Make clear that an activity's position says nothing about the quality of the
   people who run it.
2. (10 min) Alone. Each person writes the activities they know of and places
   them on their own copy of the figure-eight.
3. (10 min) In pairs, compare placements and agree on a shared version. Note
   where you disagree and why.
4. (15 min) In groups of four, combine the pair versions and write one sticky
   note per activity for the large figure-eight.
5. (15 min) All. Groups place their notes on the wall. Where the same activity
   lands in different phases, leave both notes and ask the groups to explain
   their reasons. These disagreements are often the most informative part.
6. (15 min) Look at the pattern. Which phases are crowded, which are empty?
   Which activities sit in the rigidity trap and which in the scarcity trap?
7. (15 min) In small groups, take one activity from each trap. For the
   rigidity trap: what would it take to end or radically renew it, and what
   would be freed? For the scarcity trap: what is the smallest step that would
   give it a real start?
8. (10 min) All. Record the proposed endings and starts, who will take each
   to the person who can decide, and when the group will look at the
   figure-eight again.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the session as a sorting exercise and stopping once every note has
  a place, without the conversation about what to end and what to start
- Letting the most senior person place the notes, so the picture shows the
  official view instead of what staff see
- Naming candidates for ending and then never deciding on them; trust in the
  next round falls sharply
- Putting a colleague's programme in the creative-destruction area without
  acknowledging the work that went into it
- Listing too many activities at too many levels at once; agree the level
  of detail beforehand
- Treating renewal as empty: it is where conversations and relationships grow
  that later become new activities
