# Impact/Effort Matrix

Draw a square with two axes: the benefit an action is expected to bring, from
low to high, and the effort it will take, from little to a lot. The group
places each candidate action on it by shared, rough judgement, then reads the
four quadrants:

- **Quick wins** (high benefit, little effort): start these now.
- **Major projects** (high benefit, a lot of effort): worth doing, but only
  with a plan, an owner and resources; take on few at a time.
- **Fill-ins** (low benefit, little effort): do them when capacity is spare,
  and drop them when something better turns up.
- **Thankless tasks** (low benefit, a lot of effort): set them aside, however
  attached someone is to them.

The grid's origin is not documented. The quadrant names above are the ones
Mind Tools uses for its "Action Priority Matrix". Quality-improvement teams in
health care use it as a "prioritisation matrix" to choose which change ideas to
test first (NHS Lothian), and Lean Six Sigma has a close relative, the PICK
chart (Possible, Implement, Challenge, Kill), which is credited to Lockheed
Martin. The workshop collection *Gamestorming* (Gray, Brown and Macanufo,
O'Reilly, 2010) includes it as the "Impact & Effort Matrix".

Its value lies in being rough. Nobody computes anything: the group only has to
agree whether an item sits higher or lower, further left or right, than the
ones already on the board. That makes it quick, and it makes disagreements
visible the moment someone wants to move a card.

## When to use

- Once the candidate actions already exist, from an earlier workshop, a review,
  a survey or a team's own list, and the group must decide what to act on
- When there is no time, data or need for a scoring model and a rough sort is
  good enough to get started
- To pick a few early, visible results that build momentum and trust before
  larger work is funded
- To give a group explicit grounds for dropping costly items that would bring
  little

## When *not* to use

- To generate the actions in the first place: collect them first, for example
  with [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md) or
  [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md)
- When the order of the work has to be defended with explicit estimates of
  reach, impact and confidence: use [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md)
- When the value of the items drains away at different speeds while they wait:
  use [Cost of Delay / WSJF](Cost_of_Delay_WSJF.md)
- To choose one option among alternatives against several weighted criteria:
  use a [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md)
- When a group cannot decide which of a dozen items matters most and needs a
  full ranking: use [Pairwise Comparison](Pairwise_Comparison.md)
- To agree what a fixed-date release can do without: use
  [MoSCoW](MoSCoW_Method.md)
- For a personal task list sorted by urgency and importance: use the
  [Eisenhower Matrix](Eisenhower_Matrix.md)
- When you first need to know which problem is actually the biggest: count
  occurrences with [Pareto Analysis](Pareto_Analysis.md)
- For one large investment whose payback must be shown: run a
  [Cost-Benefit Analysis](Cost_Benefit_Analysis.md)

## Facilitation outline (60 min)

Prepare a large square on a wall or an online board. Label the vertical axis
"benefit" and the horizontal axis "effort", and leave the quadrant lines faint.
Write each action on its own card in a few words, starting with a verb.

1. (10 min) **Agree the yardsticks.** Name the one goal that benefit refers to
   ("shorter waiting times", "more members by spring") and what counts as
   effort: staff time, money, approvals, disruption. Without this, people
   place the same card in different places for different reasons.
2. (5 min) **Anchor the board.** Place two or three cards everyone knows well,
   one clearly cheap and one clearly expensive, so later cards are judged
   relative to them.
3. (25 min) **Place the rest.** The person who brings a card reads it out and
   puts it where they think it belongs; anyone may move it with a reason in one
   sentence. Cards that are moved back and forth more than twice go to a
   "needs clarifying" strip beside the board rather than holding up the round.
4. (10 min) **Check the corners.** Look hard at the quick-wins corner: is each
   item really that cheap, and who would do it? Then look at the thankless
   tasks and agree openly which of them the group will stop pursuing.
5. (10 min) **Decide.** Pick a handful of quick wins with an owner and a date,
   at most one or two major projects to plan properly, and note the
   clarification items with the person who will find out more.

## Common pitfalls

- Optimism about effort: almost every card drifts towards the cheap side;
  ask who exactly would do the work and when
- Placing items in silence by the most senior person; whoever owns a card
  places it, and anyone may challenge it
- Treating the board as a final verdict; repeat it once the first quick wins
  are done or new information comes in, because placements change
- Doing only the quick wins, so the one large undertaking that would really
  change things never starts
- Lumping many small actions into one card, or splitting one action into
  many, which distorts both axes
- Arguing about exact positions near the quadrant lines; a card slightly left
  or right of the line is a judgement call, not a different category
