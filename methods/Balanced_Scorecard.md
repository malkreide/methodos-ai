# Balanced Scorecard (and Strategy Map)

Describe the strategy as a handful of objectives in several **perspectives**,
draw how they are expected to drive one another, and only then pick one or two
measures for each objective. Leadership then reviews that one compact set on a
fixed cycle, instead of a budget report on one side and a scattering of
operational statistics on the other.

The classic perspectives are:

- **Financial** (in public and nonprofit bodies: **resources** or stewardship):
  what the organisation must achieve with, or within, its money
- **Customer** (in public bodies: **citizens, service users, the mission**):
  what the people the organisation exists for should experience
- **Internal processes**: which processes must work especially well to deliver
  that
- **Learning and growth**: which skills, information and culture the staff
  need to run those processes

Robert Kaplan and David Norton introduced the scorecard in the *Harvard
Business Review* in 1992. It grew out of a 1990 multi-company research project
in which Arthur Schneiderman of Analog Devices presented the corporate
scorecard he had built there; Kaplan later recounted that Analog's quality
measures had all improved while its share price fell by nearly 70%, because
nothing linked the operational gains to customers or finances. Kaplan and
Norton soon found that the measures should not be chosen first: the
**strategy map**, set out in an HBR article in 2000 and the book *Strategy
Maps* (2004), puts linked strategic objectives on one page before any
measure is selected (Kaplan, 2010).

For nonprofit and public-sector organisations Kaplan (2001, 2010) moves an
objective for the mission or social impact to the top of the map, in place of
financial results, and widens the customer perspective to cover both those who
pay (taxpayers, donors) and those who receive the service. The budget becomes
a constraint and an enabler rather than the goal.

## When to use

- Leadership talks about budget, spending and output counts, while quality,
  the people served and staff development only surface as crises
- A strategy has been agreed, but nothing in the regular reports shows whether
  it is being carried out
- Operational indicators keep improving while the results that matter do not,
  and nobody can say which improvements were supposed to lead where
- A public body, school or charity needs to show its board, council or funders
  whether the mission is being achieved, not just whether the money was spent
  as planned
- A large organisation wants its units' reporting to share one structure, so
  the top can compare them and see how each contributes

## When *not* to use

- **When there is no strategy yet.** The scorecard translates a strategy into
  objectives and measures; it does not choose one. Without a direction it
  degenerates into sorting existing indicators into four boxes, which Kaplan
  himself warns is unlikely to capture what drives success. Settle the direction
  first, for example with [SWOT](SWOT.md),
  [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md) or a
  [Business Model Canvas](Business_Model_Canvas.md).
- **When you want a few ambitious targets for the next quarter.** That is
  [OKR](OKR.md): short cycles, a handful of outcomes per team, deliberately
  stretching targets. The scorecard is the slower frame for the whole
  organisation; the two can coexist, with OKRs as the quarterly commitments
  toward scorecard objectives.
- **When you need to explain how one programme produces its effect.** A
  [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md) works out the causal logic of a
  single intervention in depth; a strategy map is a deliberately simplified
  picture of a whole organisation.
- **When the measures will feed pay or punishment directly.** Every measure is
  a proxy. Once people are rewarded or blamed for the number, they learn to
  move the number, which is Goodhart's law. Public-sector research describes
  staff hitting targets at the expense of the service itself (Moullin, 2017).
  Review the figures in a conversation about causes, not as a verdict.
- **When the organisation already drowns in indicators.** If nobody is willing
  to drop measures, a scorecard adds another report on top of the existing
  ones. A set that grows past about two dozen measures has stopped being a
  scorecard.
- **With care in public administration.** A scorecard imposed from above as a
  reporting template tends to be filled in for the ministry and never used to
  decide anything. A New Zealand study of local government found it used
  mainly for measurement and reporting, with real difficulties in naming the
  "customer", capturing qualitative outcomes and mapping causal links
  (Northcott and Taulapapa, 2012). Build it with the people who will steer
  with it, and expect to rename and reorder the perspectives.

## Facilitation outline (build: three workshops over about two months; then monthly or quarterly reviews)

**Workshop 1: from strategy to objectives (about 4 hours, leadership team)**

1. (30 min) Restate the mission, the strategy and the planning horizon in a few
   sentences. If the room cannot agree on them, stop here; that is the work
   to do first.
2. (30 min) Agree the perspectives and their order. A company keeps financial
   results at the top; a public or nonprofit body puts the mission and the
   people served at the top and treats the budget as a constraint.
3. (90 min) Per perspective, collect candidate objectives as short verb
   phrases ("cut waiting times for building permits", "keep experienced nurses").
   Many teams write customer objectives in the voice of the person served.
   Aim for three to five per perspective, no more.
4. (60 min) Draw the strategy map: arrows from learning and growth to processes,
   from processes to the people served, from there to finances or the mission.
   Any objective with no arrow in or out is either missing a link or does not
   belong on the map.
5. (30 min) Name an owner per objective and list the open questions for the
   next workshop.

**Workshop 2: measures and targets (about 4 hours, leadership plus owners)**

6. (120 min) For each objective choose one or two measures. Mix early-warning
   indicators (training completed, process cycle time) with lagging results
   (satisfaction, outcomes, cost). For each measure ask: could we improve this
   number while the objective gets worse? If yes, add a counterweight or pick
   another measure.
7. (60 min) Record the current value, a data source, how often it is measured
   and a target where a target is genuinely useful. Some measures are better
   watched for trend than tied to a number.
8. (60 min) Cut. Remove duplicates and anything nobody would act on. Check the
   total stays within roughly 15 to 25 measures.

**Workshop 3: initiatives and the review rhythm (about 3 hours)**

9. (90 min) List the projects and programmes already running and map each to
   the objective it serves. Projects that serve none are candidates to stop;
   objectives that no project serves need one or a decision to accept the gap.
10. (90 min) Fix the review cycle (monthly or quarterly), who prepares the data,
    and how decisions taken in the review will be recorded. Decide how units
    below the top will derive their own scorecards, if at all.

**Regular strategy review (60 to 120 min, monthly or quarterly)**

11. Walk the map from the bottom up. Discuss only objectives that are off
    track or where the expected links are not showing. Ask what is causing it
    and what will change, then record the decision.
12. Once a year, test the map itself: did the measures that were supposed to
    lead actually move the results? Rewrite links and measures that did not
    hold.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with the measures that are already available instead of the
  objectives the strategy requires
- Letting every department add "its" indicator until the scorecard has 80
  lines and nobody reads it
- Handing the build to a staff unit or consultants; Kaplan traces most
  failures to the absence of senior leadership, not to the design
- Treating the arrows on the map as proven when they are hypotheses to be
  checked against the data
- Keeping the financial perspective on top in a public body, so the mission
  becomes a footnote to the budget
- Using it only as a report and never as the agenda of a decision-making
  meeting
- Cascading a scorecard to every individual, which turns it into an appraisal
  form
