# Metaplan (Moderationsmethode)

A way of running a group discussion in which every contribution is written on
a card and pinned to a large board where everyone can see it. A facilitator
(in German, the *Moderator*) asks the group a carefully worded question, each
participant answers on cards before hearing the others, and the cards are read
out and grouped on the wall with the group's help. Objections are marked on the
card they concern rather than argued away. The group then picks which themes to
take further, works on them, and closes with a written list of next steps. At
the end the boards themselves are the record of the meeting.

The technique grew out of the office-planning consultancy of the brothers
Eberhard and Wolfgang Schnelle, which moved to Quickborn near Hamburg in 1961
and was renamed the Quickborner Team in 1965. Their planning work brought
clients' staff into the process, and pinning small cards to a large wall was
part of how they worked from the start. From about 1968 Eberhard Schnelle ran
"decision-maker training" built on cards and wrapping paper, and the term
*Moderationstraining* appears soon afterwards. In 1972 the brothers left the
Quickborner Team and founded Metaplan GmbH to concentrate on training and
facilitation. Former colleagues spread the approach through handbooks such as
Klebert, Schrader and Straub's *ModerationsMethode* (1980), and it became the
basis of what German-speaking organisations, schools and adult education simply
call "Moderation". The VIPP approach (Visualisation in Participatory
Programmes) later adapted its visual techniques for development work.
"Metaplan" is a trademark of the firm; the cards and boards are sold under many
names.

## When to use

- Workshops and long meetings that go round in circles and end without anyone
  being able to say what was agreed or who does what next
- Sessions where a few people dominate and the rest stay silent, and the group
  also needs to work the input through to a result (for a quick round of input
  alone, [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) is lighter)
- Workshops with a mixed or hierarchical group, where people hold back in
  front of a superior but will write a card
- Half-day or full-day sessions that must move from collecting views to
  selecting themes to an action plan, with one facilitator running the whole
  arc
- Groups that want a visible, photographable record instead of minutes that
  each participant later reads differently

## When *not* to use

- The decision has already been taken. Running a card session to stage
  participation ("Scheinbeteiligung") damages trust in the convenor and in the
  method; use the session to plan implementation instead, and say so openly
- Nobody available has facilitation training. Without a neutral facilitator
  who handles the question wording, clustering and objections well, the
  session turns into a ritual of coloured cards that changes nothing
- The aim is a long list of ideas on one narrow question; silent rounds such
  as [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md) produce more ideas with less
  set-up
- The group must reach a binding, countable order of options; sticking dots
  shows where interest lies but is not a decision rule. Use
  [Nominal Group Technique](Nominal_Group_Technique.md) for a ranked list or
  [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md) for a decision
- You need a quick round of input from everyone in a short meeting or a large
  hall; [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) takes fifteen minutes and no
  materials
- You want to learn how users would group content for a website or handbook;
  that is [Card Sorting](Card_Sorting.md), a research method with users, not a
  meeting format
- Hundreds of people should set their own agenda; use
  [Open Space Technology](Open_Space_Technology.md)
- The session has to be fully remote. Digital whiteboards can imitate the
  cards, but much of what makes the method work (everyone standing at the same
  wall, the facilitator reading and moving cards in view) is lost

## Facilitation outline (half day, about 3.5 hours, 8 to 15 people)

Timings for the single techniques follow Metaplan's own primer (a card
question about 30 to 45 minutes, a dot round about 5 minutes); the six-phase
arc follows German adult-education guides such as methoden-kartothek.de. Prepare the questions in
writing beforehand and keep to them.

1. (15 min) **Open.** State the purpose, the time frame and what will happen
   with the results. Put up the discussion rules: speak for no more than about
   half a minute, everything gets written on a card, objections are marked with
   a lightning symbol on the card in question, nobody is forced to agree. An
   opening statement that participants mark on a scale helps them arrive.
2. (45 min) **Collect.** Pose one open question, written on the board, for
   example "What gets in the way of our project work?". Everyone writes two or
   three cards, one thought per card, a short sentence rather than a single
   word, in large letters. The facilitator collects the cards, shuffles them,
   reads each aloud and pins it, suggesting where it belongs; the group
   corrects the placement. Keep clusters small and let single cards stand
   alone. Give each cluster a heading that says something ("Handover between
   teams is unclear"), not a label ("Communication").
3. (15 min) **Work the objections.** Go through the clusters again. Wherever
   a card carries a lightning mark, ask for the counter-argument and write it
   on a different-shaped card next to the original.
4. (10 min) **Choose.** Ask a specific question such as "Which of these should
   we work on today?" Each participant gets a few dots, writes down their
   choices first and then everyone places dots at the same time. Note the
   themes with the most dots on a topic store (*Themenspeicher*): a poster
   listing the themes, with the rest kept visible for later.
5. (75 min) **Work on themes.** Small groups of three to five each take one
   theme with a prepared set of questions: what exactly is the problem, what
   could be done, what stands in the way, what is the first step. Each group
   records its work on its own board and presents it in a few minutes; the
   plenary adds comments on cards.
6. (30 min) **Plan action.** Turn the proposals into an action plan poster
   with columns for what, who, with whom and by when. Only people present can
   take on a task; anything without an owner goes back to the topic store.
7. (10 min) **Close.** Read out the action plan, agree when it will be
   reviewed, and ask for brief feedback on the session. Photograph every board
   and circulate the photographs as the record.

## Common pitfalls

- Questions that are too vague ("What do you think about communication?"),
  which produce cards nobody can act on
- The facilitator taking sides on content, re-wording cards or quietly
  dropping awkward ones
- Clusters so large that real differences disappear under one heading; a
  cluster that hides a dispute should be split or carry a lightning mark
- Treating the dot count as a vote with authority; it shows where the group's
  attention lies, and the convenor still has to say what will be decided
  where
- A wall full of cards and no action plan, so the session felt productive but
  nothing changes
- Using the same card routine for every meeting until it becomes a ritual
  people go through without engagement
- Assuming the method is backed by controlled studies. It rests on decades of
  practitioner experience and training literature, not on comparative
  research
