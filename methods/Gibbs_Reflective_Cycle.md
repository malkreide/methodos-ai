# Gibbs' Reflective Cycle

A sequence of six questions that one person works through, in order, about one
experience of their own: a conversation, a lesson, a shift, a meeting. The
order is the point. Left to itself, thinking about something that went badly
tends to loop between the scene and the feeling it left, or to jump straight
to a verdict ("I'm no good at this", "they were impossible"). The cycle slows
that down and makes the person finish with a plan for the next time.

The six stages are:

1. **Description**: what happened, where, who was involved, what you were
   trying to achieve. Facts only; no judgement yet.
2. **Feelings**: what you felt and thought before, during and after, and how
   the others involved may have felt.
3. **Evaluation**: what went well and what did not. Both lists, not just one.
4. **Analysis**: why it went the way it did. This is where you bring in what
   you know: experience, training, theory, the context you were working in.
5. **Conclusion**: what you have learned, and what else you could have done
   at the time.
6. **Action plan**: what you will do if a similar situation comes up, and
   what you need (a skill, information, a conversation) to be able to do it.

The model goes back to Graham Gibbs' *Learning by Doing: A guide to teaching
and learning methods* (Further Education Unit, Oxford Polytechnic, 1988). Gibbs
set it out as a structured debriefing to support the reflective step of David
Kolb's experiential learning cycle (experience, reflection, conceptualisation,
experimentation). It is now one of the most widely taught reflection models in
nursing and other health professions, teacher education and social work, and
many universities use it as the template for reflective writing. The
University of Edinburgh's Reflection Toolkit notes that Gibbs had repeated
situations in mind; applied to a one-off experience, the action plan simply
becomes more general.

The category here is *retrospective*: the cycle looks back at something that
has already happened in order to change what happens next. It differs from the
catalog's other retrospectives in being the individual one: one person, one
experience of their own, usually without a facilitator.

### How it differs from its neighbours

- [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) is a *team* debrief of a shared
  event, comparing what was planned with what happened. Gibbs is one person
  thinking about their own part and their own reactions; feelings are an
  explicit stage, which they are not in an AAR.
- [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) and
  [Sailboat Retrospective](Sailboat_Retrospective.md) review how a *team*
  works over a sprint or a term. Gibbs takes one episode and one person.
- [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md) brings a
  stuck, ongoing case to a group of peers who offer perspectives. Gibbs is
  done alone (or with one mentor) on an experience that is over. A Gibbs
  reflection is a good way to prepare a case for consultation.
- [GROW Model](GROW_Model.md) is a forward-looking coaching conversation about
  a goal; Gibbs starts from what already happened.
- [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md) shapes a message *to someone else* about one
  moment. [Johari Window](Johari_Window.md) is a group exercise about how
  others see you. Gibbs uses neither other people's views nor a group.

## When to use

- Someone keeps replaying an encounter that went badly and wants to get from
  rumination to a usable lesson
- Trainees on placement (teaching, nursing, social work) who must reflect in
  writing and need a structure that goes beyond narrating events
- A new manager or professional building a habit of learning from individual
  conversations, meetings or decisions
- Preparing for supervision or a mentor meeting: the six answers make a
  focused starting point
- A situation that recurs (a type of parent conversation, a type of patient,
  a type of meeting), where the action plan can be tried and checked

## When *not* to use

- For a traumatic or deeply distressing event. The cycle asks you to go back
  into the experience and its feelings on your own; that is not a substitute
  for supervision, debriefing by trained staff or therapy.
- For problems in how a team works or in the organisation. Reflecting
  individually on a structural problem tends to end in self-blame; use a team
  format such as an [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) or
  [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md).
- As a compulsory, graded writing exercise where the six headings are filled
  in to satisfy a marker. Reflection written for an assessor tends to be
  performed rather than honest.
- When you have nothing new to learn from: routine events that went fine do
  not need six stages.
- As proof that practice improved. Reviews of reflective practice in teacher
  and health professions education (see the Wikipedia overview below) find the
  evidence largely theoretical or descriptive; treat the cycle as a useful
  thinking discipline, not an intervention with demonstrated effects.

## Outline (about 30 minutes, alone or with a mentor)

1. (5 min) **Description.** Write down what happened in a few sentences, as a
   neutral observer would. Who, where, what you intended. Strike out any
   adjectives that judge.
2. (5 min) **Feelings.** Note what you felt before, during and afterwards.
   Then one line on how the other person may have felt.
3. (5 min) **Evaluation.** Two short lists: what went well, what did not. If
   one list is empty, look again.
4. (8 min) **Analysis.** Take the most important item from each list and ask
   why. What did you know, assume or overlook? What about the setting, the
   timing, the other person's situation? What does your training or reading
   say about situations like this?
5. (4 min) **Conclusion.** Finish the sentence "Looking back, I could also
   have ..." with at least two alternatives.
6. (3 min) **Action plan.** One or two specific things you will do the next
   time a similar situation arises, and what you need in order to do them.
   Put a reminder where you will see it before that situation.

With a mentor, the reflecting person speaks and the mentor only asks the next
stage's question and keeps the order; the mentor does not evaluate.

## Common pitfalls

- Spending most of the time on description and stopping before analysis,
  which is where the learning actually happens
- Skipping feelings because they seem unprofessional, so they leak into the
  evaluation instead
- An evaluation that is only self-criticism, or only about what others did
- An action plan such as "communicate better" that names no situation and no
  behaviour
- Never returning to the action plan, so the cycle never actually closes
