# ADKAR Model

A change only works when the people affected actually work differently. When
they do not, the usual reflex is to repeat what was already done: another
announcement, another training day. ADKAR asks a narrower question first. For
this person or this group, which of five conditions is not yet in place? The
answer decides what kind of help makes sense.

```
Awareness ──> Desire ──> Knowledge ──> Ability ──> Reinforcement
 why is it     do I want    do I know     can I do it    does it stick,
 needed?       to take      how?          in my real     or do I slip
               part?                      work?          back?

find the FIRST condition that is missing and work on that one
```

## Origin

The model comes from Jeff Hiatt, founder of the change-management firm Prosci.
Prosci's own account says it grew out of Hiatt asking why change-management
activities such as communication and training work at all; the company then
refined the elements through its own research. Hiatt set it out in book form in
*ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and Our Community* (Prosci,
2006). Prosci describes ADKAR as a model of *individual* change, not a complete
change-management methodology, and calls the first missing element the
"barrier point". ADKAR is a registered trademark of Prosci, which also sells a
formal assessment and certification training. This guide describes the idea in
its own words and does not reproduce Prosci's questionnaire or templates.

## The five conditions

1. **Awareness.** The person understands why the change is happening: what
   problem it solves, what happens if nothing changes. Missing awareness
   sounds like "nobody ever explained why we are doing this". The response is
   a clear reason, given by someone the person trusts, more than once.
2. **Desire.** The person chooses to take part. Understanding the reason is
   not the same as wanting it: someone may see the point and still lose
   status, time or a familiar way of working. The response is to find out
   what the person stands to lose or gain, and to deal with that honestly.
   Pressure rarely helps.
3. **Knowledge.** The person knows how to work the new way: the steps, the
   tool, what good looks like. The response is training, documentation and
   examples, aimed at the tasks this person actually does.
4. **Ability.** The person can do it in real work, at real speed, with real
   interruptions. Knowing and being able are different; the gap between them
   is where much adoption stalls. The response is practice time, coaching on
   the job, a colleague to ask, and the equipment and workload that make it
   possible.
5. **Reinforcement.** The new way is kept up. The response is recognition,
   follow-up, feedback on results, and removing the routes back to the old
   way.

The order matters as a diagnostic rule: there is little point in teaching
someone who does not yet want to take part, and no training reaches someone
who does not see why. In practice the conditions overlap and loop back.

## When to use

- A tool, routine or policy has been introduced and trained, but uptake is
  low or uneven, and nobody knows why
- Before ordering another round of training, to check whether training is
  what is missing
- When different teams, schools or wards are at very different points and a
  single response for everyone is wasting effort
- In a one-to-one conversation with someone who is struggling with a change,
  as a structure that avoids blame
- In schools, administrations and hospitals introducing new systems or
  procedures, where staff are trained once and then left alone

## When *not* to use

- To plan and lead an organisation-wide change programme. ADKAR looks at
  individual adoption and has nothing to say about sponsorship, coalitions
  or sequencing; use [Kotter's 8-Step Change Model](Kotter_Eight_Steps.md)
- When people are grieving a loss that has been decided, such as a merger or
  closure. That is a transition to accompany, not a gap to diagnose; see
  [Bridges' Transition Model](Bridges_Transition_Model.md)
- When the obstacle is probably structural: missing equipment, impossible
  workload, a process that does not work, a decision that was wrong. ADKAR
  locates problems in individuals and can turn a systemic fault into a
  verdict on people's attitude. Map the forces in the situation first with
  [Force Field Analysis](Force_Field_Analysis.md)
- To trace a single failure back to its cause; use [Five Whys](Five_Whys.md)
- When you need a validated instrument. Prosci's assessment is proprietary
  and sold commercially; do not copy it. A home-made checklist is fine for a
  conversation but is not a measurement
- As proven science. Most of what is published about ADKAR comes from Prosci
  itself or from practitioners. Independent studies are mostly
  cross-sectional readiness surveys, for example of primary healthcare
  managers (Arbaein et al., 2024) or university web editors (Rodriguez
  Vazquez, 2022); they describe where people stand, but none tests whether
  working with the model improves adoption compared with doing without it
- When the strict sequence would mislead: someone may already know the new
  system well and still not want it. Treat the order as a guide to where to
  look first, not as a law

## Facilitation outline (team diagnosis, 90 min)

For a leadership or project team looking at adoption in several groups. With a
single person, the same questions make a 30-minute conversation.

1. (10 min) Name the change as concrete behaviour: who should be doing what
   differently, from when. "Use the platform" is too vague; "post each week's
   assignments on the platform" is not.
2. (15 min) Gather evidence before opinions: usage figures, short anonymous
   questions to the groups concerned (one or two per condition, in your own
   words), what people say in corridors and meetings.
3. (25 min) For each group, rate the five conditions as present, partly
   present or missing, and mark the first one that is missing. Ask each time:
   could this be a structural obstacle that only looks like a personal one?
4. (20 min) Choose one response per group, aimed at its first missing
   condition, and set aside responses aimed at later ones for now.
5. (10 min) Decide what you would expect to see in four to six weeks if the
   response worked.
6. (10 min) Owners and dates; agree when to look again.

## Common pitfalls

- Answering every gap with more training, the one response that only fixes
  knowledge
- Treating low desire as a character flaw rather than a reason to listen
- Ignoring structural causes and blaming individuals for them
- Running the diagnosis as a test of staff, so people give the answers they
  think are expected
- Diagnosing once and never checking again; groups move, and the barrier
  moves with them
- Copying Prosci's assessment or materials instead of writing your own
  questions
