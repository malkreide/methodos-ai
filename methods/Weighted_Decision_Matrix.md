# Weighted Decision Matrix

Lay the options out as columns and the criteria as rows, agree how much each
criterion counts, then rate every option on every criterion. Multiplying each
rating by its weight and adding up gives each option a total. The total is not
the decision; it is a structured starting point for one, and a record of the
reasons behind it.

The best-known engineering form is the concept-selection matrix Stuart Pugh
presented in 1981 and set out in *Total Design* (1991): each design concept is
compared with a reference design and marked better, worse or the same on each
criterion. The weighted-sum version used here belongs to the wider family of
multi-criteria decision analysis, which governments use for appraisal and which
public procurement law builds on: Swiss procurement law (BöB, Art. 29, for the
Confederation; the identical Art. 29 of the intercantonal IVöB for cantons and
municipalities) and the EU
procurement directive (2014/24/EU, Art. 67) both require contracting
authorities to announce their award criteria and, as a rule, how they are
weighted before any bid is assessed.

## When to use

- Choosing one of several real alternatives — suppliers, sites, tools,
  applicants — where several factors matter and they do not matter equally
- When meetings keep circling between favourites, or the decision drifts
  towards gut feeling or the most senior or vocal person
- When the choice must be explained afterwards: to unsuccessful bidders, a
  school board, an auditor or a court
- As the evaluation step of a tender, where the criteria and weights must be
  fixed and published in advance anyway

## When *not* to use

- When one option is obviously better or the choice is cheap to reverse; the
  ceremony costs more than a wrong pick
- When the real question is whether benefits justify the money spent — use a
  [Cost-Benefit Analysis](Cost_Benefit_Analysis.md)
- When you are ordering a long list of work items rather than picking one
  option — use [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md) or the
  [MoSCoW Method](MoSCoW_Method.md)
- When the criteria cannot be separated: if "price" and "value for money" or
  "ease of use" and "training effort" both appear, the same advantage is
  counted twice. Merge them or drop one
- When the decision has already been made and the matrix is wanted to justify
  it. Weights tuned after seeing the options produce the answer they were
  tuned for, and the numbers lend that answer an authority it has not earned
- When the inputs are soft guesses and someone will read a difference of
  0.2 points as meaningful — the matrix gives false precision

## Facilitation outline (2 hours)

1. (10 min) State the decision in one sentence and list the options. Agree who
   decides at the end; the matrix informs that person or body, it does not
   replace them.
2. (15 min) Set knock-out conditions first: requirements an option must meet to
   be considered at all (legal compliance, a budget ceiling, data protection).
   Options that fail one leave the table now, whatever they would have scored.
3. (25 min) Collect criteria, then remove overlaps until each one measures
   something the others do not. Five to eight is usually enough.
4. (20 min) Agree weights *before* looking at how options perform — for example
   by distributing 100 points across the criteria. Write down what a 1, 3 and 5
   mean on each criterion so everyone rates on the same scale.
5. (30 min) Rate each option on each criterion. Have people rate individually
   first, then discuss large disagreements instead of averaging them away.
6. (10 min) Multiply, add up, and look at the totals. Then shift the two
   heaviest weights by a reasonable amount and see whether the winner changes.
7. (10 min) If the result feels wrong, name the criterion the matrix is missing
   rather than adjusting numbers until it feels right. Record the decision and
   the reasoning.

## Common pitfalls

- Choosing weights after the ratings are visible, so they bend towards a
  preferred option
- Letting many minor criteria together outweigh one essential requirement —
  essentials belong in the knock-out step, not in the sum
- Treating a narrow lead in the total as decisive when the sensitivity check
  shows it flips under small changes
- Rating scales nobody defined, so one person's 4 is another person's 2
- In procurement, changing criteria or weights after bids are opened; this is
  usually unlawful as well as unfair
