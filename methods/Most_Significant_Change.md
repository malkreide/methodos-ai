# Most Significant Change (MSC)

Ask the people a programme is meant to reach a single open question: over
the last period, what was the most significant change for you, and why does
it matter? Collect the answers as short accounts, then have groups at
successive levels of the organisation read them, choose the one they find
most significant and write down their reasons. Pass the choices and the
reasons back to everyone who contributed, and repeat the cycle.

The technique does without predefined indicators on purpose. It answers a
different question from a dashboard: not "did we hit the target?" but "what
is actually different for people, and which of those differences do we, as
an organisation, care about?" Because nobody tells the storytellers what to
look for, it is good at turning up effects that nobody planned, welcome or
not. And because each panel must justify its choice, the selection rounds
reveal what managers, staff and funders really value, which is often not
what the strategy paper says.

## Origin

Rick Davies developed the technique in the mid-1990s, as part of his PhD
fieldwork with the Christian Commission for Development in Bangladesh
(CCDB), to monitor a large rural development programme whose activities and
results varied too much for a conventional indicator set. Jess Dart later
applied it in Target 10, a dairy-industry extension programme in Victoria,
and helped spread it in Australia. Their joint guide, *The 'Most Significant Change' (MSC)
Technique: A Guide to Its Use* (Davies and Dart, 2005), describes ten steps
and remains the standard reference; Dart and Davies (2003) present it as a
dialogical, story-based evaluation tool in the *American Journal of
Evaluation*. By the time of the guide it had been used by NGOs and
government bodies on several continents, in sectors from agriculture to
education and health.

## The ten steps in brief

Davies and Dart treat steps 4 to 6 as the core; the others are optional and
depend on the setting.

1. Raise interest and find people who will champion the process
2. Agree a few loose *domains* of change (for example "changes in people's
   lives", "changes in how we work with partners", "anything else"), or
   start with none
3. Set the reporting period, such as monthly or quarterly
4. **Collect accounts of change** from participants and frontline staff:
   what happened, to whom, when, and why the teller thinks it matters
5. **Select** the most significant account per domain at each level, in a
   group, and record the reasons in full sentences
6. **Feed back** which accounts were chosen and why to everyone involved
7. Verify selected accounts where needed, for example by a follow-up visit
8. Quantify where useful (how often has a similar change been reported?)
9. Analyse the whole collection and the process itself: who is telling
   stories, which kinds are selected, which never are
10. Revise the design in light of what you have learned

## When to use

- A programme whose outcomes differ from person to person, such as
  counselling, family support or individual coaching, where no single
  measure of progress fits everyone
- Complex or open-ended programmes, where the most important effects may be
  ones nobody wrote into the plan
- When you want people who are served by a programme, not only its
  managers, to say what counts as a result
- When leadership, staff and funders need an explicit conversation about
  which outcomes they actually value
- In schools and public services, alongside the usual statistics, to show
  what an initiative has meant for pupils, parents or residents

## When *not* to use

- **As a measure of how widespread an effect is.** MSC samples on purpose
  for the striking case, not the typical one. It cannot tell you what
  happened to the average participant or how many benefited; for that you
  need a representative survey or another impact design, and Davies and
  Dart recommend combining the two
- **For reassurance or publicity.** Most accounts collected are positive,
  in the authors' experience around nine in ten, unless the process
  explicitly asks for change for the worse. Used to produce good-news stories
  it becomes self-confirming
- **For accountability alone or a quick, cheap verdict**, or for a one-off
  look at a programme that has already ended. Its value comes from repeated
  cycles and feedback; a single round costs much of the effort and delivers
  little of the learning
- **Without real participants.** If there is nobody with first-hand
  experience of the programme to ask, there is nothing to collect
- **For a simple programme with clearly countable outcomes** (a vaccination
  campaign, say), where indicators answer the question faster
- To set out *in advance* how a programme is supposed to work, use a
  [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md); MSC then shows what actually
  happened, including what the theory did not foresee
- To learn from one specific event right after it ends, use an
  [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md)
- To build a future on an organisation's existing strengths, use
  [Appreciative Inquiry](Appreciative_Inquiry.md): it is a change process
  with a vision and a design phase, whereas MSC is a monitoring and
  evaluation practice with structured selection
- To steer against agreed targets and indicators, use a
  [Balanced Scorecard](Balanced_Scorecard.md) or [OKR](OKR.md); MSC
  complements them with what the numbers do not show
- To pool the anonymous judgement of distant experts on a question, use the
  [Delphi Method](Delphi_Method.md); MSC asks those directly affected

## Facilitation outline: one selection meeting (2 h)

The collection of accounts happens beforehand, in interviews, group
conversations or written forms over the reporting period. This outline
covers the meeting at one level where a panel chooses among them.

1. (10 min) Remind the panel of the domains, the reporting period and
   what happened to the choices from the last round. Make clear that
   accounts of change for the worse are as welcome as good news.
2. (30 min) Read the accounts aloud, one domain at a time, around eight to
   ten per domain at most. No discussion yet beyond clarifying questions.
3. (40 min) For each domain, each person names the account they find most
   significant and why. Discuss the differences; use an anonymous vote
   first if the group has a strong hierarchy.
4. (25 min) Agree one account per domain and write down the reasons in
   full sentences: what makes this change matter more than the others? A
   few keywords such as "sustainability" are not enough.
5. (15 min) Decide who sends the chosen accounts and reasons up to the
   next level and who reports back to the storytellers, and by when.

## Common pitfalls

- Recording the reasons in a rush at the end, so the most valuable output
  of the meeting is lost
- Never feeding back: storytellers who hear nothing stop telling
- Treating selected accounts as representative results in a report to
  funders
- Letting authors judge their own accounts, which turns selection into a
  contest between colleagues
- Asking only for good news, or punishing those who report problems
- Rewriting participants' words into staff language until the account no
  longer belongs to the person who told it
