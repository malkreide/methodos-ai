# Lean Coffee

A meeting that arrives without an agenda and builds one in its first few
minutes. Everyone writes the topics they would like to discuss, the group
spends a small number of votes to put them in order, and then works down the
list one topic at a time, each in a short, fixed slot. When a slot runs out the
group decides on the spot whether to keep going or move on. The result is a
meeting with firm structure but no predetermined content: the people in the
room choose what it is about, and no single topic can quietly take over.

The format began in 2009, when Jim Benson and Jeremy Lightsmith started a
weekly morning meetup in a Seattle coffee shop and wanted it to need nothing
but people who turned up wanting to learn something. They borrowed the board
from Personal Kanban, the visual work-management approach Benson co-wrote
about, so topics move visibly from "to discuss" through "discussing" to
"discussed". The name reflects that origin (a lean format, run over coffee);
the method has since spread to open Lean Coffee groups in many cities and to
in-company team meetings, communities of practice and classrooms.

## When to use

- A recurring meeting whose agenda is set by one person, so the topics others
  care about rarely get airtime
- Meetings where one item regularly overruns and everything after it is
  postponed
- Communities of practice, peer-learning circles and open meetups with no
  owner preparing content
- Staff or team meetings where people want time to compare notes and learn
  from each other, not only to receive updates
- Sessions where the set of interesting questions is only known once the
  participants are in the room

## When *not* to use

- The meeting exists to take or record a formal decision; use a decision
  procedure such as [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md), and
  park the item on a Lean Coffee board only if it first needs open discussion
- One specific question needs input from every person present; that is
  [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md), which works through a single question
  rather than choosing among many
- Looking back on how a sprint or project went; use a retrospective format such
  as [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md) or the
  [Sailboat Retrospective](Sailboat_Retrospective.md)
- There are fixed items that must be covered regardless of interest, such as
  statutory approvals; give them their own agenda slot and run Lean Coffee
  only for the open part of the meeting
- The group is too large to see one board; split into tables of four to eight
  with a board each

## Facilitation outline (60 min)

Timings follow the descriptions by the method's originators and the Open
Practice Library. Keep the timer visible and treat it as the group's tool, not
the facilitator's.

1. (3 min) Draw three columns on a wall or online board: *To discuss*,
   *Discussing*, *Discussed*. Explain the rules: everyone proposes topics,
   everyone votes, each topic gets a short timebox, the group decides on any
   extension.
2. (5 min) Everyone writes topics on sticky notes, one topic per note, a few
   words each. Any number per person is fine.
3. (5 min) Each author reads their notes aloud in one or two sentences, so
   everyone knows what each note means. Merge obvious duplicates.
4. (2 min) Everyone gets two or three dot votes to spread across the notes as
   they like, including several on one note. Sort the *To discuss* column by
   number of dots.
5. (about 40 min) Move the top note to *Discussing* and start a five-minute
   timer. When it rings, the group shows thumbs: up to continue, sideways for
   indifferent, down to move on. If most thumbs are up, add a shorter slot,
   such as three minutes, and check again when it ends. Otherwise move the
   note to *Discussed*, optionally note a takeaway or action next to it, and
   pull the next note.
6. (5 min) Close by reviewing the *Discussed* column and any actions, and take
   a photo of the board as the record. Unreached notes can seed the next
   session.

Online, a shared whiteboard with three columns and its built-in dot voting
works well; thumbs can be shown on camera or as reactions. For a group spread
across time zones, the same steps can run asynchronously: topics are collected
on a shared board over a week, voted on the following week, and then discussed
in threads one at a time.

## Common pitfalls

- Letting the timer slide because a discussion feels productive; the group
  should choose to extend, not drift past the slot
- Extending every topic, so that only one or two are ever covered
- Skipping the one-sentence explanation of each note, so votes go to the most
  intriguing wording rather than the most useful topic
- The manager or most senior person treating the board as a new agenda they
  control, re-ordering notes or vetoing topics
- Recording nothing, so a good discussion leaves no trace and recurring topics
  are rediscussed from scratch
- Using it for decisions it was not designed to take, then finding nothing
  binding was agreed
