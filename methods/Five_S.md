# 5S Workplace Organisation

A space that several people share stays usable only if they agree what belongs
in it, where each thing lives and who looks after it. 5S is a five-step routine
for reaching that agreement and keeping it: take out what is not needed, give
what remains a marked place, clean the area and look for faults while doing so,
write down the standard the team has just created, and hold it with short,
regular check-ups.

The five steps are named after five Japanese words that each begin with an S
when romanised: *seiri* (sort), *seiton* (set in order), *seiso* (shine),
*seiketsu* (standardise) and *shitsuke* (sustain). The practice grew out of
Japanese factories, where an orderly, self-explaining workplace was the basis
for visual management: when every tool has an outlined place, an empty outline
shows at once that something is missing. Hiroyuki Hirano's sourcebook, first
published in Japanese around 1990 and in English as *5 Pillars of the Visual
Workplace* (1995), made the five-step version widely known outside Japan.
Toyota itself has traditionally counted only four S, treating discipline as
part of the regular audits of standard work. After reaching Western
manufacturing in the 1980s, the same steps were taken up in offices,
hospitals, clinics and laboratories, and later for shared digital folders.
The governments of India, Senegal, Sri Lanka and Tanzania have made it part
of their quality-improvement strategies for health care (Kanamori et al.,
2016).

## When to use

- People lose time searching for things in a shared room, store, cupboard,
  workbench or vehicle, and supplies are reordered because nobody can see what
  is already there
- A big clear-out has been done before and the space was back to its old state
  within weeks, because nothing was agreed about where things go or who keeps
  them there
- A shared drive or team folder has grown into a maze of duplicates and old
  versions, so people keep private copies rather than trust the shared one
- Safety or quality depends on the right item being in the right place, such as
  an emergency trolley, a first-aid cabinet, a laboratory bench or a tool board
- Schools and public services: a staff room, a stock of teaching materials, a
  municipal workshop or a records room that belongs to everyone and therefore
  to no one

## When *not* to use

- **As an order from above.** 5S imposed by management, with photo
  inspections and rankings, feels like policing. People keep the space tidy
  while someone is watching and stop when the audits stop. The people who use
  the space must decide what stays and where it goes; the manager's job is to
  give them the time and to take part in the check-ups.
- **For creative or highly variable work.** A studio, a design team's wall or
  a researcher's desk often needs things to lie around in changing
  arrangements. Fixed, outlined places hinder that. Apply 5S to the shared
  stock and the common tools, not to someone's work in progress.
- **As a one-off clean-up.** Sorting, labelling and cleaning without the last
  two steps produces good photos and nothing lasting. If the team is not
  willing to agree a standard and to check it for a few minutes every week or
  month, save the effort.
- **When the problem is the flow of work, not the place.** If cases or orders
  take weeks although the hands-on work takes hours, the delay sits between
  steps; map it with [Value Stream Mapping](Value_Stream_Mapping.md).
- **When you need proof of large gains.** Most published evidence consists of
  before-and-after case studies from manufacturing and health care, often
  combined with other lean tools, so the effect of 5S alone is hard to isolate
  (Kanamori et al., 2016). A survey of Spanish factories found 5S use associated
  with better quality and productivity, but that is a correlation, not proof
  of cause (Bayo-Moriones et al., 2010).

**Neighbouring methods.** A [Gemba Walk](Gemba_Walk.md) is leaders going to
watch the work and learn what gets in the way; 5S is the team reorganising its
own space, and a walk is often where the need for it is first noticed. To try
out a wider change in small steps and check its effect, use the
[PDCA Cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md); its "act" step is where a 5S standard fits. If a
specific, repeated failure needs explaining, look for its cause with
[Kepner-Tregoe Problem Analysis](Kepner_Tregoe_Problem_Analysis.md) or
[Five Whys](Five_Whys.md). If what the organisation must let go of is
programmes and projects rather than objects and files, use
[Ecocycle Planning](Ecocycle_Planning.md). If the overload is in your own task
list rather than in a shared space, the
[Eisenhower Matrix](Eisenhower_Matrix.md) or [Timeboxing](Timeboxing.md) fit
better.

## Facilitation outline (first round about 4 hours, then 15 minutes a month)

1. **Agree the area and take a "before" picture (15 min).** Choose one
   clearly bounded space, such as the material room, one cupboard wall or one
   shared folder. Everyone who uses it should be represented. Photograph it, or
   for a drive record the folder count and the oldest files.
2. **Sort (60 min).** Go through every item and ask whether it is needed here,
   how often, and in what quantity. Mark doubtful items with a red tag or
   sticky note and put them in a holding area for a fixed time, for example two
   weeks; what nobody claims is disposed of, given away or archived. Check with
   the people responsible for waste, data protection and records before
   throwing out chemicals, devices or official files.
3. **Set in order (60 min).** Give every remaining item one place, close to
   where it is used and in proportion to how often. Label shelves and boxes,
   outline places for tools, mark minimum stock levels, and for a drive agree
   a short folder structure (for example working, reference, archive) and a
   naming rule.
4. **Shine (45 min).** Clean the area thoroughly and use the cleaning as an
   inspection: note what is broken, worn, leaking, expired or out of date, and
   decide who repairs or replaces it.
5. **Standardise (30 min).** Write the result down on one page: a photo of
   how the area should look, who looks after which part, and what is done
   daily, weekly and monthly. Hang it where the work happens or pin it at the
   top of the folder.
6. **Sustain (15 min a month, ongoing).** Walk through the area against the
   one-page standard with a short checklist, take turns doing it, fix
   deviations on the spot and adjust the standard when the work has changed.
   Report the check, not a ranking of people.

## Common pitfalls

- Stopping after the first three steps, so the space slides back within weeks
- Letting one tidy person or the manager decide where everything goes, so the
  others neither know nor accept the layout
- Using audits to rank teams or individuals, which turns a shared routine into
  inspection
- Labelling every object in sight, so the labels become clutter themselves
- Throwing things away in a rush, including items that were needed or that
  had to be disposed of in a particular way
- Never updating the standard, so it describes a room that no longer exists
