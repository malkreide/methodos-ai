# Jigsaw Method

Split a body of material into as many parts as there are members in a small
group, and give each member one part. People who hold the same part first meet
as an "expert group" to study it together, settle what it says and agree how
to explain it. They then return to their mixed home groups, where each one in
turn teaches their part to colleagues who have not read it. Nobody reads
everything, but every participant hears every part explained by someone who
has worked through it. In German-speaking schools and training the method is
widely known as *Gruppenpuzzle*.

The technique was developed in 1971 in Austin, Texas, by the social psychologist
Elliot Aronson and his graduate students. The city's schools had just been
desegregated, and the team saw that the competitive classroom was feeding
hostility between pupils of different backgrounds; making each pupil hold a
piece that the others needed was meant to replace competition with mutual
dependence. Aronson and colleagues described the approach in *The Jigsaw
Classroom* (1978). Teachers, trainers and university lecturers have since
adapted it for adults, in staff development, seminars and online courses,
where the same structure is used mainly to get through a lot of reading in
limited time. This entry describes the adult workshop form in its own words;
the original classroom version adds a group leader and a closing quiz.

## When to use

- A team or staff body has to understand a large amount of given material
  (a framework, a regulation, a set of reports) and there is no time for
  everyone to read all of it
- Presentations by a few people would leave most participants passive, and
  you want each person to carry responsibility for one piece
- The material falls naturally into parts of similar weight that can be read
  and understood without the others: chapters, reports, case studies, articles
- You want people from different units or roles to work together on the same
  content, since the mixed groups put them side by side

## When *not* to use

- Every participant must master every part to the same depth, for example for
  a certification or legally binding duties; people end up knowing their own
  part far better than the parts they heard about, so the jigsaw can introduce
  the material but not replace individual study
- The parts build on one another so tightly that part four makes no sense
  without part three; the expert groups then work blind
- The material is difficult and nobody in an expert group can be relied on to
  read it correctly; a misreading spreads to every home group unchecked.
  Have a subject expert visit the expert groups, or choose a lecture with
  questions instead
- The number of participants is very uneven against the number of parts, or
  attendance is unpredictable, so that home groups end up with parts missing
- The aim is to explore open questions together and let ideas cross-pollinate
  rather than to cover fixed content: use a [World Café](World_Cafe.md)
- You need every voice on one question in a quarter of an hour:
  [1-2-4-All](One_Two_Four_All.md); a regular meeting whose topics the
  participants choose: [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md); many ideas on one
  question: [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md)
- The group has to reach a decision; the jigsaw builds shared understanding,
  which a decision method can then use

## Facilitation outline (about 2 hours)

Shown for twenty participants and a body of material split into four parts;
scale the reading time to the length of the material. If participants can
read their part beforehand, give them the guiding questions with it; a plain
request to "read and come prepared" rarely works, and step 3 then shrinks to a
short refresh.

1. (Before) Divide the material into four parts of similar length and
   difficulty, each understandable on its own. Write two or three guiding
   questions per part ("What changes for us?", "What is new compared to the
   current rules?"). Plan five home groups of four, each with one person per
   part, and decide what to do with extra or missing people (two people can
   share a part; a facilitator can stand in).
2. (5 min) In plenary: explain the purpose, the two rounds and the timings.
   Hand out the parts so that each home group holds all four.
3. (25 min) Individual reading of one's own part, with the guiding questions.
4. (25 min) Expert groups: everyone with the same part sits together (here, five
   people per part). They compare what they read, clear up misunderstandings,
   agree on the three or four points that matter most and on how to explain
   them in a few minutes, and each person notes a short crib sheet. The
   facilitator or a subject expert moves between expert groups and corrects
   errors now, while they can still be stopped.
5. (35 min) Home groups: each member explains their part in about six minutes,
   followed by a couple of minutes of questions. One person keeps time so the
   last part is not squeezed.
6. (15 min) Plenary: collect open questions and points of disagreement that
   the home groups could not resolve, and answer them or note who will.
   Optionally, finish with a short check of the key points of every part, in
   the spirit of the original classroom quiz.
7. (Afterwards) Send out the complete material and the crib sheets, so that
   people can look up the parts they only heard.

Online, use breakout rooms for both rounds and a shared document per expert
group for the crib sheet; reassigning participants between rounds needs to be
prepared in advance.

## Common pitfalls

- Parts of very different length or difficulty, so one expert group is still
  reading while the others wait
- Skipping or shortening the expert round; it is the only point at which
  misunderstandings are caught before they are taught
- Experts who read out their notes or the text itself instead of explaining
  what matters
- Assuming the job is done after the home groups; without the plenary and the
  full material afterwards, gaps and errors stay in people's heads
- Promising more than the method delivers: five randomised experiments with
  sixth graders found no learning advantage over individual work or ordinary
  teaching on the same content (Stanczak et al., 2022). The case for using it
  with adults rests mainly on engagement and on covering more material in the
  time available, not on guaranteed better recall
