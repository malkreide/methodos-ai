# Walt Disney Method (Dreamer, Realist, Critic)

One idea is taken through three roles, one after the other: the **Dreamer**
imagines what it could be with no limits, the **Realist** works out how it
would actually be done, and the **Critic** looks for what could go wrong and
what is still missing. The Critic's findings go back to the Dreamer and the
Realist, and the round repeats until the idea holds up. Each role usually has
its own place in the room (a chair, a corner, a sheet of paper on the floor),
so that moving to it also means switching the way of thinking.

**Where it comes from.** The model is Robert Dilts' work, not Disney's. Dilts,
one of the early developers of neuro-linguistic programming (NLP), described it
in *Strategies of Genius, Volume I* (Meta Publications, 1994) as the result of
"modelling" how Walt Disney turned ideas into films. His starting point was a
remark recorded in *The Illusion of Life* (1981), the book by Disney animators
Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston: that there were really three different Walts,
the dreamer, the realist and the spoiler. Dilts renamed the spoiler the critic and built a
step-by-step exercise around the three. Disney left no such method in writing.
Dilts also describes the three functions as having had separate physical spaces
in Disney's work; that is his reconstruction, and the popular story that Disney
walked between three rooms is best treated as an illustration rather than
documented history. NLP as a whole has
little research support; what is used here is only the practical core, the
deliberate separation and sequencing of three ways of thinking about an idea.

**Category: facilitation.** It does not model a system, estimate risks or
choose between options. It is a structure for a thinking session, alone or in a
small group, that changes the order in which an idea is imagined, planned and
judged.

| Role | Question | Not allowed |
|---|---|---|
| Dreamer | What would this look like if anything were possible? What do we really want? | "That won't work", costs, rules |
| Realist | How exactly would we do it? Who, what, in which steps, with what resources, by when? | Judging whether it is worth it |
| Critic | What could go wrong, what is missing, who would object, what has been overlooked? | Attacking the person, rejecting the whole idea |

## When to use

- One promising idea keeps stalling, either because it stays a vague wish or
  because doubts kill it before it is thought through
- A person censors their own ideas too early and wants a structure that gives
  imagination, planning and doubt each their turn
- A small team needs to grow a vision into a first workable plan and wants the
  plan tested before it is presented to others
- Teaching: students developing a project idea learn to separate generating,
  planning and evaluating instead of doing all three at once

## When *not* to use

- A group is arguing about a topic in general and needs everyone to look at it
  in the same way at the same time: [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md)
  runs six parallel modes for any discussion, whereas this method takes a
  single idea through three roles in sequence, from vision to plan
- The plan is already agreed and only the risks before launch are in question:
  a [Pre-Mortem](Pre_Mortem.md) imagines the failure directly and is sharper
- You want many variants of something that already exists: use
  [SCAMPER](SCAMPER.md)
- The group's own habits are what blocks a long-held goal: use
  [Reverse Brainstorming](Reverse_Brainstorming.md)
- A big decision needs a mock-up tested with real users within a week: use a
  [Design Sprint](Design_Sprint.md)
- Several ideas must be compared or ranked; this method deepens one idea and
  decides nothing between alternatives

## Facilitation outline (75 min, small group; alone it takes about half)

1. (5 min) State the idea or goal in one sentence. Mark three places in the
   room, or three sheets on a table, as Dreamer, Realist and Critic, and agree
   the rules for each. Optionally mark a fourth, neutral place to step back to
   between rounds.
2. (15 min) Dreamer: everyone stands or sits at the Dreamer place. Describe the
   idea at its best: what it looks like when it works, for whom, and why it
   matters. No objections, no numbers. One person writes everything down.
3. (20 min) Realist: move to the Realist place. Take the dream as given and
   work out how it would be done: steps, people, money, time, first actions.
   Draw it as a rough plan or storyboard.
4. (15 min) Critic: move to the Critic place. Go through the plan, not the
   people, and list gaps, risks and objections. Phrase each one as a question
   the Dreamer or Realist can answer ("How will we reach residents who do not
   use the internet?").
5. (15 min) Second round: go back to the Dreamer with the Critic's questions,
   then to the Realist, then briefly to the Critic again. Repeat as long as the
   Critic still finds substantial gaps and time allows.
6. (5 min) Close at the neutral place: what is the plan now, what is still
   open, who takes the next step.

## Common pitfalls

- Letting critique leak into the Dreamer phase, which ends the dream within
  minutes; the facilitator has to stop "yes, but" firmly
- Doing the Realist step half-heartedly, so the Critic has nothing concrete to
  test and attacks the vision instead
- Treating the Critic as the final verdict instead of as input for the next
  round; one round is rarely enough
- Fixing roles to people (the same colleague is always the Critic): everyone
  goes through every role together
- Overselling the method's NLP background; present it as a simple thinking
  structure, not as a technique with proven psychological effects
