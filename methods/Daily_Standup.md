# Daily Stand-up

A short coordination meeting held every working day, at the same time and in
the same place, usually standing and often in front of a shared board. Each
person says briefly what they have finished, what they will work on next and
what is in their way. Anything that needs more than a minute or two is noted
and taken up straight afterwards by the people it concerns. The whole thing
should be over in about fifteen minutes, often less.

The format is best known from Scrum, where the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and
Jeff Sutherland describes a fifteen-minute daily event for the people doing the
work, held at a fixed time and place. Hospitals and factories reached a very
similar practice on their own track: the daily huddle of lean management and
patient-safety work, a stand-up of ten minutes or less at the start of a day or
shift, held at a visual board, where concerns are raised and handed on rather
than solved on the spot.

## When to use

- When the team's people depend on each other's work day to day, and a
  hold-up of one person soon stalls others
- When problems keep surfacing only at a weekly or monthly meeting, by which
  time they have already cost days
- When a long regular meeting is mostly people reporting in turn, and a short
  daily round could replace much of it
- At the start of a shift in a ward, a clinic, a production line or a front
  office, to share who is present, what needs attention today and what nearly
  went wrong yesterday

## When *not* to use

- When its real purpose is for a manager to collect status. Stray, Moe and
  Sjøberg observed 102 stand-ups in 15 software teams and found that meetings
  where people reported to the facilitator were disliked and lost most of their
  value; meetings where members talked to each other fared much better. If
  status is what is needed, a visible board or a written update is cheaper.
- When the work changes slowly, or colleagues work on separate things and
  rarely depend on each other. The same study found that senior members with
  long, independent tasks often had nothing new to say from one day to the next,
  and recommends meeting less often (three or four times a week, or splitting a
  large team) when that is enough.
- As a place to solve problems. A short exchange right after someone names an
  obstacle can be valuable, but a full analysis keeps everyone else standing.
  Hand longer issues to a follow-up conversation or to a structured method.
- When people work part-time, in shifts or across time zones so that the group
  is rarely together at one time. Rotate the slot, split into the sub-groups
  that actually work together, or switch to a written daily update.
- As a substitute for team reflection. Looking back at how the team works is
  the job of a retrospective, not of the daily round.
- Expect modest evidence. Most of what is written about stand-ups is
  practitioner experience; the field research available reports clear benefits
  in some teams and frustration in others, depending on how the meeting is run.
  For clinical huddles, the AHRQ PSNet primer lists safety improvements but also
  notes staffing and time constraints and the risk of reinforcing hierarchy.

## Facilitation outline (15 min)

Set-up, once: agree a fixed time that interrupts the work least (Stray and
colleagues found many teams preferred just before lunch to first thing in the
morning), a fixed place or video link, and a board that shows the work in
progress.

1. (1 min) Start on time, without waiting for latecomers. Whoever facilitates
   only keeps time; the role can rotate.
2. (10 min) Each person, or each item on the board, gets a short turn: what is
   done since the last stand-up, what comes next, what is in the way. Speak to
   the group, not to the facilitator. Many teams drop the "what is done" part
   once the board shows it, and spend the time on what comes next and on
   obstacles, which is where coordination happens.
3. (2 min) Read back the obstacles that were named. For each one, agree who
   will deal with it and with whom, and write it next to the board.
4. (2 min) Close. Those who need to talk further stay behind; everyone else
   goes back to work.

## Common pitfalls

- Turning it into a round of reporting to the boss, with each person
  justifying their day
- Long accounts of everything someone did, most of it irrelevant to the others
- Solving a problem in front of the whole group instead of after the meeting
- Naming the same obstacle every day without anyone taking it on, until people
  stop mentioning obstacles at all
- Letting it drift to twenty or thirty minutes, or starting late every day
- Keeping the daily rhythm out of habit when the team would be better served
  meeting less often
