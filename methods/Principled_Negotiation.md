# Principled Negotiation

Two parties who have to reach an agreement stop bargaining over the demands
each has already announced and work instead on the needs behind them. The
approach rests on four moves: deal with the relationship apart from the
substance, look for the interests underneath the positions, generate several
options before choosing one, and settle what is fair by reference to
standards that neither side controls. Alongside these, each party prepares
its best alternative to a negotiated agreement (BATNA): what it will do if the
talks fail. That alternative, not a fixed bottom line, decides whether an
offer is worth taking.

The approach was developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project, founded in
1979, and set out by Roger Fisher and William Ury in *Getting to Yes* (1981),
with Bruce Patton as co-author of the second (1991) and third (2011)
editions. The authors also call it "negotiation on the merits", to contrast
it with both hard and soft positional bargaining.

## When to use

- Two sides that will keep working together after the deal (a school and its
  parents' association, management and a staff representation, two units
  sharing the cost of a service) and whose talks have become a contest of
  who gives in first
- A dispute over a single figure such as price, rent or a cost share, where
  other issues that matter to both sides have never been put on the table
- Talks that keep ending in a midpoint compromise that neither side is happy
  with, or that keep breaking down and restarting
- Preparing yourself for an important negotiation even if the other side
  does not know the approach; the preparation steps work one-sidedly

## When *not* to use

- When the other side negotiates in bad faith, deceives or uses threats.
  The approach can still help you recognise this and fall back on your
  alternative, but it will not convert an opponent who does not want an
  agreement.
- Under strong power asymmetry, where the weaker side has no workable
  alternative. Interest-based talk does not remove dependence; improving the
  alternative (allies, a formal procedure, an appeal) may matter more than the
  conversation.
- For matters that are not negotiable: legal obligations, safety
  requirements, child protection, fundamental rights. These set the frame
  within which negotiation happens; they are not bargaining chips.
- When the disagreement lies inside one group that has to adopt a rule
  together, rather than between two parties with separate mandates. Use
  [Consent Decision-Making](Consent_Decision_Making.md).
- When the parties agree on what they want and only need to compare
  alternatives against agreed criteria. Use a
  [Weighted Decision Matrix](Weighted_Decision_Matrix.md).
- When the relationship has broken down so far that the parties cannot sit
  together; a neutral mediator is the better next step.

Critics have pointed out that the book says little about power (McCarthy,
1985, with a reply by Fisher in the same issue), that the line between
interests and positions is less clean than it looks (Provis, 1996), and that
it is presented as a universal strategy when it suits some negotiations much
better than others (Reyes, 2015). Read the approach as a strong default for
negotiations with a shared future, not as a guarantee.

## Facilitation outline (preparation 60 min, session 90 min)

Each party prepares separately; the session is joint. If a neutral
facilitator is available, they run the session; otherwise the heads of
delegation agree the structure at the start.

**Preparation (each side, 60 min)**

1. (10 min) Write down your own position in one sentence, then ask "why do
   we want that?" until you reach the needs behind it: money, time,
   recognition, security, workload, reputation, precedent.
2. (15 min) Do the same for the other side, as you imagine it. Mark every
   point as a guess to be checked in the session.
3. (15 min) List what you will do if there is no agreement. Pick the best
   realistic one and make it concrete. Estimate the other side's alternative
   too.
4. (10 min) Collect standards that could tell both sides what is fair: laws
   and regulations, earlier agreements, what comparable organisations do,
   market rates, expert opinion, an agreed procedure such as an independent
   assessment.
5. (10 min) Note two or three possible arrangements you could live with,
   without committing to any.

**Joint session (90 min)**

1. (10 min) Agree the purpose of the meeting and the ground rules: no offers
   or rejections in the first hour, each side may ask questions, personal
   grievances get their own slot instead of colouring the substance.
2. (10 min) Relationship slot: each side says briefly how the dispute has
   affected it, and the other side summarises what it heard before
   responding.
3. (20 min) Interests: each side explains what matters to it and why. The
   other side asks questions and reflects back. Write the interests of both
   sides on one shared list, marking those that are shared, those that differ
   and those that conflict.
4. (20 min) Options: generate as many possible arrangements as you can
   without evaluating them, including trades between issues the sides value
   differently, phased or conditional solutions and trial periods.
5. (20 min) Criteria: agree which fair standards apply, then test the most
   promising options against them and against each side's alternative.
6. (10 min) Record what is agreed, what is still open, who drafts the text
   and when the sides meet again. If no option beats either side's
   alternative, say so plainly and end without a deal.

## Common pitfalls

- Opening with a demand anyway; once a figure is on the table, the talk
  tends to revolve around it
- Treating "separate the people from the problem" as permission to ignore
  feelings; unacknowledged frustration returns as resistance to the substance
- Taking the other side's stated reasons at face value instead of asking
  follow-up questions, or presenting guesses about their interests as facts
- Evaluating options as they are proposed, which kills the less obvious ones
- Overestimating your own alternative, which makes you reject offers you
  should accept
- Choosing a "fair standard" that happens to favour only your side; the other
  side will recognise it, and the talk becomes positional again
- Revealing your alternative as a threat rather than using it to decide
