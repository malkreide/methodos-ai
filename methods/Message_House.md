# Message House

A one-page picture of a house that holds what an organisation wants to say
about one project. The **roof** is a single umbrella message: the one sentence
every spokesperson leads with. Beneath it stand about three **pillars**, the key
messages that carry the roof, each looking at the project from a different
angle. The **foundation** holds the proof points: facts, figures, examples and
quotations that make each pillar believable. Everyone who speaks for the
project works from the same page, phrasing it in their own words for their own
audience.

The method has no documented inventor. Marc Fest, a former vice president of
communications at the Knight Foundation who learned it there, writes in his
practitioner guide that it is a long-standing piece of communications practice
whose origin he could not trace. His version adds four questions for finding
the messages: why the project matters, what it offers the audience, how it
answers the most likely criticism, and what the audience should do next. He
also describes a simpler "one-room" house that lists the audiences, the key
messages and the proof points in a single box instead of separate roof,
pillars and foundation. The Dutch central government's communication knowledge base
recommends a message house where one message is carried by several
communicators or must reach several audiences, and where one overarching
narrative has to hold together several arguments or frames.

## When to use

- Before a change goes public that many people will be asked about: a new
  timetable or day-school model, a reorganisation, a new service, a merger
- When contradictory versions are already circulating and the spokespeople
  need to agree on one account
- Before a campaign, so that volunteers, staff and partners rest their
  leaflets, posts and speeches on the same claim and the same checked figures
- To prepare for an interview, a council meeting or a parents' evening where
  questions will try to pull the speaker away from the main points

## When *not* to use

- When the substance is not yet decided. A house built over open questions
  becomes a set of reassuring phrases that later turn out to be untrue.
- When the proof points are thin. A confident roof over weak or selected
  evidence is spin, and audiences notice; fix the project or say honestly what
  is still uncertain.
- As a substitute for listening. The house fixes what you will say; it tells
  you nothing about what parents, residents or staff think. Hear them first,
  for example with a [Stakeholder Map](Stakeholder_Map.md) and real
  consultation, and let what you hear change the messages.
- In a dialogue that is meant to be open. Repeating pillar sentences in a
  consultation or a difficult conversation sounds scripted and evasive.
- As evidence-based practice. Support comes from public-relations and
  communications practice; there are no controlled studies of whether it
  improves how audiences understand an organisation.

## Facilitation outline (2 hours)

Bring together the people who will actually speak about the project, not only
the communications staff: for a school, the head, a class teacher, someone
from the school office and a parent representative if possible.

1. (15 min) **Audiences.** List who will hear about the project and through
   which channel: parents, staff, the council, the press, the website. If two
   audiences need fundamentally different things, plan a separate house for
   each rather than one vague one.
2. (25 min) **Roof.** Each person writes in one sentence why the project
   matters and what it is for. Read them out, then combine them into one
   umbrella sentence that a non-specialist would understand and could repeat.
3. (30 min) **Pillars.** Collect the points people most need to grasp:
   what changes for them, why this approach was chosen, how the obvious
   objection is answered, what they should do next. Choose about three; fewer
   is better than more. Write each as a short statement of two or three
   sentences.
4. (30 min) **Foundation.** Under each pillar list the proof: figures,
   dates, examples from practice, a quotation from someone affected. Mark every
   proof point you cannot yet back up. A pillar with nothing under it is either
   dropped or reworded until it claims only what you can show.
5. (10 min) **Test.** One person reads the house aloud; another asks the three
   hardest questions a sceptical parent, councillor or journalist would ask.
   Check that each answer leads back to a pillar and a proof point.
6. (10 min) **Distribute and review.** Agree who owns the page, give it to
   everyone who answers questions, and set a date to revise it as the project
   changes.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing the roof as a slogan. It should say what the project does and why,
  not sound like an advertisement.
- Too many pillars. With seven key messages nobody remembers any of them;
  practitioner guides advise three, or at most a handful.
- Proof points that are promises ("will improve learning") rather than facts
  you can show today
- Treating the house as a script. Spokespeople should use their own words; the
  page fixes the content, not the wording.
- Building it once and never updating it while the project, the figures or the
  public's questions move on
