# Futures Wheel

Put one change in the middle of a sheet and keep asking "if this happens, what
happens next?" The direct effects form a first ring around the change; the
effects of those effects form a second ring; a third ring usually ends the
exercise. What the group gets is a map of knock-on consequences that a normal
discussion of the change, which rarely gets past the first ring, would miss.

Jerome C. Glenn devised the technique in 1971 as a graduate student in
education, as a way of helping students think through the implications of
change; it first appeared in print in 1972. Futurists, corporate planners and
public policy advisers have since adopted it, and it remains one of the
simplest tools in the foresight repertoire.

## When to use

- A decision is close (a new rule, a timetable change, a technology rollout, a
  reorganisation) and the debate covers only the intended, immediate effects
- You want to know who is affected indirectly — groups who are not in the room
  and would not recognise themselves in the proposal
- A trend or event is already under way outside your control and you need to
  work out what it will mean for your organisation before it arrives
- As a quick warm-up for longer foresight work: it gets a group thinking about
  consequences within minutes

## When *not* to use

- When you need to know how likely or how large an effect is. The wheel lists
  possibilities; it is not a forecast or a probability estimate. Add a rating
  step or use a quantitative method for that
- For a broad, vague change ("digitalisation", "climate change"). The number of
  effects multiplies with each ring and the map turns into spaghetti; narrow
  the centre to one concrete change first
- To explain a problem that keeps recurring in an existing system — that is a
  question about feedback, for a [Causal Loop Diagram](Causal_Loop_Diagram.md)
- With a group that all thinks alike. The method is only as good as the
  collective judgement in the room; without people from different roles,
  backgrounds and affected groups it maps the group's blind spots, not the
  consequences
- When the outer rings would be taken as fact. Each step adds speculation, and
  a third-order effect is a chain of guesses

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

1. (10 min) Before the session, write the change as one short, concrete
   statement with its key assumptions, so non-specialists understand it. Put
   it in the centre of a large sheet or a shared board.
2. (20 min) First ring. Ask "if this happens, what follows directly?" Collect
   effects in turn round the group; record positive and negative ones, and
   keep only effects that follow without an intermediate step. Keep discussion
   short.
3. (25 min) Second ring. For each first-ring effect, ask the same question
   again and draw two or three spokes outwards. Finish the whole ring before
   starting the next: following one chain all the way out narrows the picture.
4. (15 min) Third ring, for the effects that matter most. Stop here; further
   rings become too tenuous to be useful.
5. (10 min) Mark the map: which effects are welcome, which are worrying, which
   are surprising, and which groups appear that nobody had named before.
6. (10 min) Agree what to do: adjust the proposal, inform or involve an
   overlooked group, or watch for an early sign of an effect.

## Common pitfalls

- A centre that is too broad, which produces dozens of first-ring effects and
  no usable map
- Skipping from one effect straight to a distant consequence, so the chain
  cannot be followed or checked
- Recording only the negative (or only the hoped-for) effects
- Debating each effect as it is offered; collect first, prune afterwards
- Reading the finished wheel as a prediction rather than a list of things to
  prepare for
- Running it only with the people who designed the change
