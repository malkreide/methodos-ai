# Hoshin Kanri (Policy Deployment)

Choose a very small number of breakthrough objectives at the top of the
organisation, then pass them down one level at a time. At each level the
people below do not simply receive a target: they work out what they could
contribute, propose targets and the means to reach them, and settle both with
the level above, often going back and forth several times. This exchange is
called **catchball**. The result is a chain of linked plans, from the
leadership's few objectives down to the work of individual teams, that is
then reviewed on a fixed rhythm (usually monthly, with a larger review at the
end of the year) in the spirit of plan-do-check-act.

The Japanese name is usually translated as "direction management" or "policy
management"; Western organisations also call it *policy deployment*,
*Hoshin planning* or *strategy deployment*. It grew out of total quality
control in Japanese industry in the 1960s. Bridgestone Tire is generally cited
as the first documented case, with an internal manual from 1965, and Komatsu
and Toyota developed comparable systems at about the same time. Yoji Akao's
edited volume, published in English in 1991 as *Hoshin Kanri: Policy
Deployment for Successful TQM*, collected the Japanese practice; Bob King's
1989 book for GOAL/QPC had introduced it to American readers a little
earlier. Barry Witcher and Rosemary Butterworth studied it as it was
practised in UK subsidiaries of Japanese manufacturers and at Xerox and
Hewlett-Packard; Witcher's later work at the University of East Anglia
summarised the annual cycle in four activities: focus, alignment, integration
and review.

Many practitioners document the plan on an **X-matrix**, a single sheet on
which long-term objectives, this year's objectives, the improvement
priorities, the measures and the responsible people sit on four sides of a
square, with marks in the corners showing which elements support which
(Jackson, 2006). The matrix is a convenience, not the method: the method is
the negotiated chain of objectives and the regular review.

## When to use

- Leadership publishes strategic goals each year, but the people who would
  have to act on them cannot say what they should do differently
- Targets are passed down without anyone checking whether the units below
  have the capacity to meet them, and they are routinely missed
- Departments run many improvement projects, and nobody can trace which of
  them serve the organisation's priorities and which merely keep people busy
- A multi-level organisation (a school authority with many schools, a
  hospital group, a university with faculties, a manufacturer with plants)
  wants one shared annual planning and review cycle instead of separate plans
  in separate formats
- The direction is reasonably stable for several years, so the effort of
  building the chain pays off over more than one cycle

## When *not* to use

- **When there is no strategy to deploy yet.** Hoshin Kanri carries a chosen
  direction through the organisation; it does not help choose it. Settle the
  direction first, for example with [SWOT](SWOT.md),
  [Scenario Planning](Scenario_Planning.md) or [Ansoff Matrix](Ansoff_Matrix.md).
- **When leadership will not negotiate.** Catchball assumes the upper level
  will actually revise targets or move resources when a lower level shows
  that a target is unrealistic. Where that never happens, the method becomes
  top-down target-setting with extra meetings, and people learn to propose
  what they think is wanted.
- **When leadership attention will not last.** The benefits come after
  several annual cycles. A change of director, a reorganisation or a crisis
  often stops it half-way; Witcher and Butterworth found personnel changes and
  keeping up the periodic reviews among the main practical difficulties.
- **If you would run it entirely by the book in a small or informal
  organisation.** The full apparatus of matrices, forms, review levels and
  audits is heavy. A team of twenty does not need it; a single planning
  workshop and quarterly [OKRs](OKR.md) will usually do.
- **When the environment changes faster than an annual cycle.** Targets
  negotiated across several levels are slow to renegotiate. Under high
  uncertainty, shorter cycles and more local experimentation fit better.
- **When you expect proof that it works.** The evidence is mostly
  practitioner accounts and qualitative case studies of single organisations
  (for example Witcher and Butterworth's studies, and the university cases
  collected by Balzer and Sinha, 2024). That supports it as a well-tried
  practice, not as a method shown to outperform alternatives.

## Facilitation outline (one annual cycle)

The full cycle runs over a year; the timings below are for the meetings
within it.

**Leadership: setting the breakthrough objectives (one day, before the planning year)**

1. (60 min) Review last year's results and what the reviews showed; note
   what was learned, not who missed what.
2. (120 min) Agree at most three to five breakthrough objectives for the next
   three to five years, and what must be achieved towards them this year.
   Write down explicitly what will *not* be pursued.
3. (90 min) For each objective, draft a target and the measure by which
   progress will be checked, and identify which units below will have to
   contribute.
4. (30 min) Agree the planning calendar: when each level will hold its
   catchball session and by when the chain must be complete.

**Each level: catchball session (about 3 hours, per unit, repeated down the levels)**

5. (30 min) The manager of the level above presents the objectives and
   targets that concern this unit, and why they matter.
6. (60 min) The unit drafts its own contribution: which targets it can
   commit to, by what means, and what it needs (people, money, time, help
   from other units).
7. (60 min) Unit and level above negotiate. Where the targets and the means
   do not add up, either the target, the means or the resources change.
   Record open questions rather than forcing agreement on the spot.
8. (30 min) Agree owners and the measures to be reviewed monthly. The agreed
   plan becomes the starting point for the next level down; open points go
   back up and the round is repeated where needed.

**Reviews (60–90 min monthly per level; half a day at year end)**

9. Each owner reports the measures against plan. Discuss only what is off
   track: what is the cause, what will change, does the plan above need to
   change too? Escalate what cannot be solved at this level.
10. At year end, review the whole chain: which objectives moved, which
    means worked, and what the next cycle should change. Feed this into
    step 1.

## Common pitfalls

- Too many objectives at the top, so every unit can relabel what it was
  already doing as a contribution
- Treating catchball as a briefing: the level below is told the target and
  asked to sign, with no real chance to change it
- Mixing breakthrough objectives with all routine performance measures, so
  the strategy disappears in a long table of indicators
- Building an elaborate X-matrix that only the planning office understands
- Skipping or shortening the monthly reviews once the plan is written
- Linking the targets directly to individual appraisal or pay, which pushes
  every level to negotiate for safe targets
- Planning strictly within departments when an objective needs several
  departments to work together; cross-functional objectives need an explicit
  owner and their own review
