# Collegial Case Consultation

A small group of peers helps one of its members with a difficult situation from
their own work, following a fixed sequence of phases and fixed roles. One person
brings the case, one person moderates and keeps time, everyone else acts as
consultant. The point of the structure is to stop what usually happens when a
colleague says "can I ask you all something?": a burst of quick tips and "that
happened to me too" stories, most of which do not fit, and none of which the
person asking had time to think about.

Known in German as *Kollegiale Beratung* or *Kollegiale Fallberatung*, and
also called *intervision*, in contrast to supervision, which brings in an
outside professional. It is widespread in schools, social work and public
administration in German-speaking countries. Several schemes exist. A widely
used one is the six-phase model set out by Kim-Oliver Tietze in *Kollegiale
Beratung. Problemlösungen gemeinsam entwickeln* (Rowohlt, 2003), who later
studied its effects empirically (Tietze, 2010). An older, more tightly scripted
ten-step variant, the Heilsbronn model, was developed in Bavaria in 1985/86 and
refined at the Protestant church's centre for religious education in
Heilsbronn. The City of Zurich's school department publishes a compact
five-step version for its schools ("5 x 5 minutes").

The core sequence, whichever scheme a group uses:

- **Case account** — the case owner describes the situation as they experience
  it, including their own reactions, without preparing a polished report.
- **Clarifying questions** — consultants ask only what they need to understand
  the facts. No disguised suggestions ("have you tried...?").
- **Key question** — the case owner states what they want from the group,
  ideally as a question about their own next steps ("How can I re-establish
  contact with this mother?"). This is the hinge of the session: the group
  works on this question, not on the whole case.
- **Consultation** — consultants offer hypotheses about what might be going on,
  their own reactions, and ideas for action. The case owner listens and does
  not reply, defend or explain. One person writes the contributions down.
- **Conclusion** — the case owner says what struck them, which ideas they will
  use, which they set aside, and what they will do next. The group does not
  argue for the rejected ideas.

Tietze's version adds a *casting* phase at the start (choosing the case and
assigning roles) and a *method choice* before the consultation, in which the
group picks one of a set of formats for its contributions, such as a round of
hypotheses, a brainstorm, explicit "good advice" or a resonance round in which
consultants say only what the account triggered in them.

## When to use

- A teacher, social worker or other professional is stuck with a concrete
  situation (a pupil, a family, a parent conflict, a team constellation) and
  wants several perspectives, not one adviser's view
- No supervisor or coach is available, or supervision is rare and the
  situation cannot wait for it
- A team or a cross-school group wants a standing slot in which members support
  each other on real cases and learn from each other's situations
- Peers at the same level, for example heads of section or school leaders from
  different schools, want a confidential setting to think through their own
  leadership situations

## When *not* to use

- **Acute crises.** When someone is in danger or a situation is escalating
  today, act and call in the people responsible; a 45-minute round of
  hypotheses is the wrong tool
- **Child protection, suspected abuse, threats, or matters with legal or
  disciplinary consequences.** These belong in the formal procedures of the
  school, the authority or the employer, with the designated specialists.
  A peer group may help a colleague cope with such a case afterwards, but must
  not become the place where it is assessed or decided
- **When confidentiality cannot be assured.** Cases name real pupils, parents
  and colleagues. If the group cannot agree that what is said stays in the room,
  or the case can only be told with identifying details to people who know the
  family, either anonymise it properly or do not bring it
- **When the group is too hierarchical.** Members may differ in role and
  training, but if a line manager or someone who writes appraisals sits in,
  people bring safe cases and polite contributions. Senior staff are usually
  better served by a group of peers from other units
- **As a substitute for supervision, therapy or professional advice.** The
  method works on a professional question, not on personal distress. The
  Saxony-Anhalt guide for schools states plainly that it is no replacement for
  therapy. When a participant is exhausted or overwhelmed, or the group itself
  runs into a conflict it cannot resolve, stop and involve an outside
  supervisor
- **When participation is compulsory.** Groups that are told to do it tend to
  go through the motions; voluntary membership with an agreed commitment for a
  period works better
- **When the problem is a missing rule, resource or decision**, not one
  person's handling of a situation. Take it to whoever can decide

## Facilitation outline (about 45 minutes, 4 to 10 people)

Before the first session, agree the ground rules once: confidentiality, voluntary
participation, the roles, and that the moderator may interrupt to keep the
phases. Print the phases on one sheet or a flipchart.

1. (5 min) **Choose the case and the roles.** Who has something pressing today?
   Agree on one case owner, one moderator who also keeps time, and one person
   to write down contributions. Everyone else consults. Rotate roles from
   session to session.
2. (8 min) **Case account.** The case owner talks for about five minutes,
   from memory, about the situation and their own part in it. The moderator
   helps with a focusing question if the account wanders. Consultants listen
   and may jot down notes.
3. (5 min) **Clarifying questions.** Consultants ask short factual questions
   ("How old is he?", "Who else was there?"); the case owner answers briefly.
   The moderator stops questions that are really suggestions or judgements.
4. (5 min) **Key question.** The case owner formulates what they want from the
   group, written up where everyone can see it. If they cannot, the consultants
   may offer two or three possible questions and the case owner picks or
   rewrites one; nobody pushes.
5. (15 min) **Consultation.** The case owner turns their chair slightly away or
   simply stays silent. Consultants first offer hypotheses ("Perhaps he is
   testing whether you will give up on him"), then ideas for what the case
   owner could do, one contribution at a time, always aimed at the key
   question. The writer records them. If the group has agreed a specific
   format (hypotheses only, good advice, resonance round), the moderator
   names it before this phase starts.
6. (7 min) **Conclusion.** The case owner says which hypotheses ring true,
   which ideas they will try, which do not fit, and what their first step is.
   They take the written notes with them. The consultants do not reply.
   Finish with a one-sentence round on how the process went, and agree that
   the case owner reports back briefly at the next meeting.

A tight version runs as five steps of five minutes each (account, clarifying
questions, hypotheses, solutions, case owner's evaluation); the Zurich guide
allows the hypotheses and solutions steps to stretch to ten minutes, and refers
complex questions to specialist consultation instead. A remote version works in a video call if the writer
shares a document and the case owner switches off their camera during the
consultation phase.

## Common pitfalls

- Skipping the key question and consulting on "the case" in general, which
  produces broad advice that answers nothing in particular
- Clarifying questions that are solutions in disguise ("Have you thought about
  calling the parents?")
- The case owner breaking into the consultation phase to explain why an idea
  will not work; the moderator should hold the silence
- Consultants competing for the cleverest diagnosis of the pupil or parent
  instead of offering possibilities the case owner can test
- The moderator joining the consultation and losing track of time and roles
- A few members always bringing cases and others never doing so, which tilts
  the group into one-way helping
- Telling the case to people who know the family without anonymising it

## How it differs from its neighbours

- [GROW Model](GROW_Model.md) — one helper and one person in a conversation;
  here a whole group contributes, and the case owner deliberately does not
  converse with them during the consultation
- [SBI Feedback](SBI_Feedback.md) — for telling one person how their
  behaviour came across; this method is for helping someone who asks for help
  with their own situation
- [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md) — a team debriefs an event it
  went through together; here one person's situation is the subject and the
  others were not involved in it
- [Six Thinking Hats](Six_Thinking_Hats.md) — structures a group's discussion
  of a shared topic by thinking mode; it does not give one person's case
  centre stage
- [One-Two-Four-All](One_Two_Four_All.md) — gathers input from everyone on a
  shared question, rather than on one member's case
