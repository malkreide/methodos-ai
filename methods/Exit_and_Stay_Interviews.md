# Exit and Stay Interviews

Ask people why they leave, and ask the people you want to keep why they stay,
in the same structured way each time, then read the answers together rather
than one by one. A single conversation tells you about one person; thirty
conversations pooled over a year tell you about the organisation.

The two halves complement each other. The **exit interview** is held with
someone who has resigned, ideally in their last weeks. It is the older practice
and the one most organisations already run in some form, usually without doing
much with the results. The **stay interview** turns the question round: a
leader sits down with a valued employee who has given no sign of leaving and
asks what keeps them, what they would change and what might one day make them
go. Richard P. Finnegan popularised the stay interview in practitioner books
for managers, and school systems in the United States have recently adopted it
to understand why early-career teachers quit. Stay interviews are the better
half where you can only do one: they reach people while there is still time to
act, and they do not depend on the candour of someone who is already on the way
out.

## When to use

- Turnover is high or rising in a team, a profession or a whole organisation,
  and the reasons on file ("personal reasons", "new opportunity") explain nothing
- Good people leave at a typical point, such as after the first two or three
  years, and you want to know what happens around that point
- You want early warning: what would make the people you most rely on start
  looking elsewhere
- You need evidence across many cases to settle competing theories about pay,
  workload, leadership or career prospects

## When *not* to use

- **To find the truth about one departure.** People who are leaving often give
  safe, socially acceptable reasons ("a better offer", "closer to home") and
  keep criticism of their manager or colleagues to themselves. They may need a
  reference or want to return one day. Research comparing reasons given at
  departure with reasons given later found only weak agreement (Lefkowitz and
  Katz, 1969), and interpersonal problems are the ones least often disclosed
  (Givens Skeaton and Ford, 2018). Treat each account as one data point, not as
  the explanation.
- **When nothing will happen afterwards.** Without someone who pools the notes,
  names the themes and decides on changes, the interviews are a courtesy at
  best. Stay interviews that lead to no visible change make things worse: you
  asked, they told you, nothing moved.
- **In very small units.** With five people in a team, a theme such as "the
  head of unit does not listen" points to a person and is traceable to whoever
  said it. Pool across units or over a longer period, or do not report at unit
  level at all.
- **Without a clear data-protection basis.** Interview notes are personal data
  and often sensitive. Decide before the first interview who sees the notes, how
  they are anonymised, how long they are kept and what the interviewee is told;
  involve the data protection officer and, where one exists, the staff council.
- **Instead of fixing what is already known.** If everyone knows the workload is
  unmanageable or a particular manager drives people away, interviewing more
  leavers to confirm it is a way of avoiding the decision.
- **When the relationship is already broken.** A stay interview by a manager
  whom the employee does not trust produces polite answers and suspicion. Use a
  neutral interviewer or a different instrument first.
- **To look back at one event or project.** That is an
  [After Action Review](After_Action_Review.md), not a retention question.

## Facilitation outline

A single interview takes 20 to 45 minutes. The method itself runs as a cycle
over months.

### Before the first interview (once)

1. Agree the purpose in one sentence ("understand why experienced staff leave
   and what keeps those who stay") and who will act on the results.
2. Write a short guide of five to seven open questions and keep the core
   questions the same for everyone, so answers can be compared. Typical ground:
   what the person looks forward to at work; what they would change; why they
   have stayed so far; when they last thought about leaving and what triggered
   it; what the organisation or their manager could do differently.
   For exit interviews add: what made you start looking, and what would have
   changed your decision.
3. Choose interviewers. For exit interviews, someone outside the person's line
   management (HR, a senior manager from another unit, an external interviewer)
   hears more. Stay interviews are often held by the direct leader, which builds
   the relationship but limits criticism of that leader; school districts in
   Utah used coaches or district staff instead of the teachers' own principals.
4. Settle data protection, anonymity thresholds and the note template.

### Each interview (20 to 45 min)

1. (3 min) Explain why you are asking, what happens to the notes and what will
   not happen (no effect on appraisal, references or pay).
2. (15 to 35 min) Work through the core questions one at a time. Listen more
   than you talk, probe with "what happened then?" and "can you give an
   example?", and do not defend or explain.
3. (5 min) Close by saying when and how results across all interviews will be
   reported back. In a stay interview, agree any individual step you can take
   yourself, and only promise what you can deliver.
4. Straight afterwards, write up notes in the template, keeping key phrases in
   the person's own words.

### Pooling and follow-up (every three to twelve months, 90 min)

1. One person or a small group codes all notes since the last round into
   themes, counting how often each appears and separating exit from stay
   interviews.
2. The leadership group reviews the themes, compares them with turnover figures
   and staff survey results, and picks one to three things to change.
3. Staff hear what came out and what will be done, at a level of detail that
   protects individual answers.
4. Next round, check whether the theme has moved.

## Common pitfalls

- Holding the exit interview on the last afternoon, between handing back keys
  and the farewell drinks
- Letting each interviewer improvise their own questions, so nothing can be
  compared
- Filing notes in personnel records where nobody reads them together
- Reading one dramatic exit interview as the whole story
- Turning a stay interview into a performance review or a pay negotiation
- Promising changes in individual stay interviews that the organisation never
  delivers
