# Choice Architecture (Nudge Design)

Every form, letter, menu and sign-up page already steers what people do: some
option is pre-selected, something comes first, the request arrives at some
moment and in some words. Choice architecture treats those details as design
decisions. When people fail to do something they themselves want to do, it asks
which detail stands in the way, changes it, and measures whether behaviour
actually shifts. It does not add rules, fines or rewards, and it keeps every
option open.

## Origin

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein coined the terms *choice architecture* and
*nudge* in *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness*
(2008). A nudge, in their sense, changes behaviour predictably without
forbidding any option or significantly changing the incentives, and it must be
easy to avoid. Governments took the idea up quickly: the UK set up its
Behavioural Insights Team in the Cabinet Office in 2010, and similar units
followed in Australia, Canada, the US and elsewhere.

Two practitioner frameworks turned the idea into a procedure. The Behavioural
Insights Team's **EAST** framework (2014, revised 2024) groups the evidence into
four questions: is the desired action **E**asy, **A**ttractive, **S**ocial and
**T**imely? The OECD's **BASIC** toolkit (2019, written with Pelle Guldborg
Hansen) lays out a sequence for public bodies — **B**ehaviour, **A**nalysis,
**S**trategies, **I**ntervention, **C**hange — and adds ethical guidelines for
collecting behavioural data and running experiments on citizens.

## When to use

- A clearly defined action — registering, applying, paying, attending,
  returning a form — is not happening, although the people concerned want it
  or gain from it
- More reminders, posters or appeals have been tried and made little difference
- You control the letter, form, website, default or timing and can change it
- You can count the outcome (sign-ups, payments, attendance) before and after

## When *not* to use

- **To get people to do what is not in their interest.** Steering people
  against their own goals, or in a way you would not be willing to disclose,
  is manipulation, not good design. A useful test: could you publish the
  design and explain why you chose it without embarrassment?
- **Instead of fixing a real barrier.** If the fee is too high, the opening
  hours exclude working parents or the service itself is poor, a better-worded
  letter will not help and may disguise the problem. Remove the barrier first.
- **When the behaviour is a deep-rooted habit or depends on resources people
  lack.** Small design changes move marginal decisions; they rarely change
  lifestyles or compensate for poverty.
- **When you expect large effects.** Across 126 trials covering more than
  23 million people run by two US nudge units, the average effect was 1.4
  percentage points (about 8 % over the control group), against 8.7 points in
  published academic studies; the authors attribute most of the gap to
  selective publication (DellaVigna & Linos, 2022). A large meta-analysis found
  a small-to-medium average effect (Mertens et al., 2022), but a re-analysis
  correcting for publication bias found the average effect close to zero, with
  some heterogeneity that leaves room for individual nudges to work (Maier et
  al., 2022). Plan for a modest gain and measure it, rather than promise one.
- **To understand the whole experience across many offices.** That is a
  [Customer Journey Map](Customer_Journey_Map.md); choice architecture works on
  one decision point at a time.

## Facilitation outline (design workshop 2–3 h, then a test over weeks)

1. (20 min) **Name the behaviour.** One specific action by one group, with the
   number you want to move: "parents return the care registration by 31 March;
   today 52 % do." Not "parents engage more."
2. (40 min) **Walk the decision.** Put the actual letter, form, web page or
   screen on the wall and go through it as the person receiving it. Note every
   step, every choice they must make, what happens if they do nothing, and when
   the request reaches them. Add whatever data you have on where people drop
   out. Talk to a few of them if you can.
3. (30 min) **Diagnose with EAST.** For each step ask: is it easy (steps,
   pre-filled fields, what happens by default)? Attractive (is the request
   noticed, personal, clear about what is in it for them)? Social (do they know
   that others do it, can someone commit on their behalf)? Timely (does it
   arrive when they can act, or weeks before)? Mark the two or three details
   most likely to explain the gap.
4. (30 min) **Design a few small changes.** One or two variants, each changing
   few details, so you can tell what worked: a one-page letter with a single
   action, a date sent closer to the deadline, a pre-filled form, a
   re-registration that applies unless the family opts out, a true sentence
   about how many others have already done it. Check each against the
   disclosure test above.
5. (20 min) **Plan the test.** Ideally split the group at random into the
   current version and the new one; otherwise compare like with like (two
   comparable schools, two sending waves). Fix the outcome measure, the date
   you will read it and the size of difference that would be worth acting on
   before you start.
6. (weeks) **Run, measure, decide.** Read the result on the agreed date. Keep
   what worked, drop what did not, and record both, including null results, so
   the next team does not rediscover them.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with a favourite technique ("let's add a social norm") instead of a
  diagnosis of this particular decision
- Changing everything at once, so the result cannot be attributed to anything
- Declaring success from a before-and-after comparison that ignores seasonal
  or other changes; a comparison group exists to rule those out
- Using a social-norm message that is not true, or that tells people most
  others *fail* to act, which can normalise the very behaviour you want to end
- Setting a default that serves the organisation rather than the person
- Treating a modest, honest effect as failure, or a pilot's large effect as
  what the full rollout will deliver
