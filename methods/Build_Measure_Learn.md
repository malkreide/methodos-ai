# Build-Measure-Learn (Lean Startup Experiment Cycle)

Before building the full version of something new, find the one belief that
would sink it if it were wrong, write it down as a prediction with a pass mark,
and test it with the smallest offer that makes real people act. Then look at
what they did and decide, deliberately, whether to carry on, change course or
stop. Repeat until the remaining risk is small enough to justify the big
commitment.

```
   idea / riskiest assumption
          |
          v
   BUILD the smallest test  --->  MEASURE real behaviour  --->  LEARN
          ^                         against the pass mark         |
          |                                                       v
          +------------  next assumption  <----  persevere / pivot / stop
```

## Origin

Eric Ries described the approach on his blog from 2008 and set it out in *The
Lean Startup* (2011), drawing on his own start-up failures and on Steve Blank's
customer development method from *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005).
Blank's core point was that a new venture is a search for a business model
that works, not the execution of a known one, so its first job is to test its
assumptions with customers. Ries added the vocabulary that spread: the
build-measure-learn loop, the minimum viable product (a term Frank Robinson had
used earlier), validated learning, "vanity" versus actionable metrics, and the
pivot, a structured change to one fundamental hypothesis while keeping what has
been learned.

The ideas travelled well beyond start-ups. Ries worked with the US federal
government around the Presidential Innovation Fellows programme in 2012, and
public digital services now build the same logic into their phases: the UK
government service manual, for example, asks teams in the alpha phase to
identify their riskiest assumptions, test them with throwaway prototypes, and
decide on the evidence whether to continue, go back to research or stop.

## When to use

- A new product, service, course or programme is about to be funded and built
  in full, and its success depends on how people will behave (take it up, pay,
  return, recommend) rather than on whether it can be built
- Everyone involved is enthusiastic, the plan has been presented many times,
  and nobody can point to anything a potential user has actually done
- A venture has been running for a while on encouraging anecdotes and needs an
  honest answer to "is this working, or should we change direction?"
- Public sector: an innovation lab or digital team must show a funding body
  that a new resident-facing service will be used before it is rolled out
  everywhere, and can run a small, time-limited trial with a clear exit
- Education: a school, university or education authority considering a new
  offering (a paid course, an after-school service, a parent platform) that
  can be offered to a small group or advertised before it exists

## When *not* to use

- When failure of a half-built offer could harm people: medicines, care,
  child protection, safety-critical systems. A deliberately minimal version is
  the wrong instrument where minimum quality is a duty, not a choice
- When the law or a policy entitlement requires the full service for every
  eligible person from day one. Partial provision to some and not others may be
  unlawful or unfair; check before designing the test
- When residents or pupils would come to depend on the trial version. A public
  body cannot simply "pivot" away from the people it served; if it cannot
  promise a fair exit (continuing support, a hand-over, clear communication at
  the start), it should not run the test that way
- When the honest measure of success cannot be observed for years (learning
  outcomes, health effects). Short tests then tend to measure what is easy,
  such as clicks and sign-ups, rather than what matters; a
  [Theory of Change](Theory_of_Change.md) and a proper evaluation fit better
- When you are improving an existing process rather than testing demand for
  something new; use the [PDCA Cycle](PDCA_Cycle.md)
- When nobody yet understands what the intended users struggle with. Testing a
  guess quickly is still testing a guess; do the research first, for example
  in the discovery half of a [Double Diamond](Double_Diamond.md)
- When the sample will be tiny. A few dozen users can swing either way, and a
  weak result from a crude test may reflect the test, not the idea. A
  longitudinal study of digital-health ventures found that the less successful
  ones prototyped early, could not tell whether negative feedback concerned
  the product, the market or the prototype, and pivoted away from ideas that
  may have been sound (Hannah, 2024)

## What the evidence says

The strongest evidence comes from randomised trials with early-stage
entrepreneurs. Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella and Spina (2020) trained 116
Italian start-ups over about a year; both groups received the same general
training on gathering market feedback, but only the treated group was taught
to state explicit hypotheses and test them rigorously. Treated start-ups
performed better and were more likely to pivot to a different idea, without
dropping out more often. This supports the "state a hypothesis, test it,
decide" core of the method, but it is a study of nascent ventures in one
country, not of large organisations or public services, and it tested a
scientific framing of the approach rather than every Lean Startup practice.

## Roles

- **Owner of the decision**: the person who controls the money or the
  mandate and commits in advance to act on the result
- **Team**: the people who will build and run the test; often two to six
- **Sceptic**: one person asked to challenge whether the metric and threshold
  could actually fail

## Facilitation outline

The loop has three meetings around an experiment that runs for days or weeks.
The estimated duration covers the team's time in those meetings and in setting
up the test, not the waiting.

**Meeting 1: frame the experiment (2 to 3 hours)**

1. (20 min) Write the idea in one sentence: who it is for, what they will do
   with it, and what has to be true for it to be worth building.
2. (40 min) List every assumption the plan rests on. For each, ask: if this
   is wrong, does the idea fail? How sure are we, and on what evidence? Pick
   the one that is both critical and least supported. It is usually about
   behaviour (will they use it, pay, come back), not about technology.
3. (30 min) Turn it into a falsifiable prediction with a pass mark set now:
   "At least 30 of the 200 families we invite will book a slot within two
   weeks." Agree what result would mean persevere, pivot or stop. Have the
   sceptic check that the number could really be missed.
4. (40 min) Choose the smallest test that produces that behaviour. Options
   include a concierge test (deliver the service by hand to a few users), a
   smoke test (an announcement or sign-up page for an offer that does not yet
   exist, followed by an honest message to those who respond), a pilot with
   one school or district, or a pre-order. Decide how users will be told what
   happens when the test ends.
5. (10 min) Name who builds, who measures, and the date of the review.

**Run the test (days to weeks)**

6. Build only what the test needs. Count the agreed behaviour from the start;
   note what people say as context, not as the result.

**Meeting 2: measure (60 to 90 min)**

7. Compare the result with the pass mark set in Meeting 1, not with a new
   one. Look at the absolute numbers and at who responded, not only at
   percentages.
8. Ask what else could explain the result: the test's crudeness, the time
   of year, the way people were invited.

**Meeting 3: decide (30 to 60 min, can follow Meeting 2 directly)**

9. The decision owner states one of three outcomes: **persevere** (the
   assumption held; move to the next riskiest one), **pivot** (keep what was
   learned and change one fundamental element, such as the target group, the
   channel or the offer), or **stop**.
10. Record the decision and the evidence, and frame the next loop.

## Common pitfalls

- Setting or moving the threshold after seeing the numbers
- Vanity metrics: page views, downloads, "likes" or satisfaction scores that
  rise whatever the idea is worth, instead of behaviour tied to the decision
- Treating "minimum" as an excuse for a poor product that fails for reasons
  unrelated to the assumption under test
- Testing an assumption that is easy rather than the one that is risky
- Running loops without a decision owner, so the result never changes the plan
- Deceiving people in a smoke test, or leaving trial users stranded; in the
  public sector this damages trust well beyond the test
- Pivoting so often that no idea gets a fair test
