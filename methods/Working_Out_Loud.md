# Working Out Loud (WOL Circle)

A small peer group, usually four or five people, meets for an hour a week over
twelve weeks. Each member pursues a goal of their own choosing, and the group
exists to keep everyone moving: members report what they tried since last
week, help each other with the next exercise, and agree on a small step to
take before the next meeting. The steps all build the same habit: find the
people connected to your goal, approach them by offering something useful
rather than by asking for something, and let more of your work and thinking
be seen by others.

Bryce Williams gave the phrase "working out loud" its usual definition in a
2010 blog post: making work observable and narrating it as it happens. John
Stepper turned the idea into a structured peer programme and published it as
*Working Out Loud: For a Better Career and Life* (Ikigai Press, 2015); a
revised edition, *Working Out Loud: A 12-Week Method to Build New
Connections, a Better Career, and a More Fulfilling Life*, and a companion
*Circle Workbook* followed from Page Two in 2020. Germany was among the early
strongholds, and circles are now run in companies, communities and
non-profits in many countries. Stepper describes the shift at its core as
moving from trying to get something from others to making contributions that
are useful, visible and generous. Over the years the emphasis has moved away
from social-media visibility towards personal connection in ordinary
conversations, emails and meetings.

What makes it a distinct method rather than general networking advice:

- **A personal goal** — each member chooses something they care about and can
  make progress on within twelve weeks: exploring a field, learning a skill,
  getting an idea or project known, finding a new role.
- **A relationship list** — from the first week, members write down people
  who are connected to their goal, and the list grows as they discover more.
- **Contribution before request** — the early exercises are about giving:
  thanks, appreciation, a useful link, an introduction. Asks come later, if at
  all.
- **Visibility** — members gradually make their work, questions and progress
  easier for others to find, so that people with related interests can find
  them in turn.
- **Small steps and peer accountability** — each week's guide sets short
  exercises; the group's job is support and gentle accountability, not advice
  on each other's goals.

The group runs itself. There is no trainer in the room; a printed or digital
weekly guide sets the agenda and exercises, and members take turns keeping
time. Official guides and workbooks come from Stepper's organisation and are
copyrighted and currently sold per participant; using them inside a company
needs a licence from that organisation.

## When to use

- People in a large organisation work in silos, and you want connections and
  knowledge to flow across units without a central programme that needs
  trainers everywhere
- An individual wants to make progress on a goal that depends on other
  people (a career move, a new field, getting a project known) and keeps
  postponing it alone
- A people-development or HR team wants a voluntary, low-cost peer format that
  can scale from a pilot to hundreds of groups
- Staff in a public administration, university or network of non-profits
  rarely meet anyone beyond their own department or institution
- People in career transition need structure and company while they rebuild
  their network

## When *not* to use

- A teacher or professional is stuck with one concrete case and needs peers'
  ideas in a single session — use [Collegial Case Consultation](Collegial_Case_Consultation.md)
- Teachers want to improve teaching by planning, observing and revising a
  lesson together — use [Lesson Study](Lesson_Study.md)
- A manager wants to help one employee find their own solution in a
  conversation — use the [GROW Model](GROW_Model.md)
- A team wants a self-organised discussion meeting with topics chosen on the
  day — use [Lean Coffee](Lean_Coffee.md)
- The group shares one problem it must solve or decide together; a circle
  serves individual goals, not a joint outcome
- Participants are sent rather than volunteering, or the culture punishes
  visible work in progress; fix the conditions first

## Facilitation outline (60 min per weekly meeting, twelve weeks)

Before week 1: form groups of four or five volunteers, ideally from different
units and without reporting lines between them; agree a fixed weekly slot and
whether you meet in person or by video; give everyone the guide.

Each weekly meeting:

1. (5 min) Check in. Everyone says in one sentence how they are and how their
   week went.
2. (20 min) Progress round. Each member takes about four minutes: what they
   did since last time, what happened, what got in the way. Others listen and
   ask only to understand.
3. (25 min) This week's exercises. Work through the guide's exercises for the
   week together, for example refining your goal, extending your relationship
   list, drafting a first small contribution to someone on it, or improving
   how easily others can find your work. Help each other, but keep goals
   individual.
4. (10 min) Commitments. Each member names one or two small steps for the
   coming week. Agree who keeps time next week.

Arc across the twelve weeks: the first weeks choose a goal, build the
relationship list and make first low-risk contributions; the middle weeks
deepen contact and make work more visible; the later weeks take larger steps
toward the goal; the last meeting reflects on what changed and what each
person will keep doing.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating it as a course: members wait to be taught instead of doing the
  week's exercises between meetings
- Goals that are too vague ("network more") or too big to move in twelve
  weeks; check in week 1 that each goal is specific and genuinely wanted
- Letting the progress round turn into advice for each other's goals; the
  group supports, the goal owner decides
- Skipping the contribution exercises and jumping straight to asking people
  for favours, which is the habit the method is meant to change
- Mixing a manager and their own staff in one group, which closes down the
  honest progress round
- Counting groups started rather than finished: dropouts after week 4 usually
  mean the slot, the goal or the group composition was wrong

## Evidence

Most reports come from practitioners and adopting companies. In a
pre-post study with a six-month follow-up and 507 participants, Augner,
Schermuly and Jungmann (2024) found that taking part in Working Out Loud
increased participants' working-out-loud behaviour and psychological
empowerment at work, both after the programme and six months later, but
found no effect on vigour at work.
