# Reverse Brainstorming (TRIZ, "make it worse")

Instead of asking how to reach a goal, the group asks how it could guarantee
the worst possible result, and lists every way of getting there. The useful
part comes next: everyone goes through that list and asks which items already
resemble something the group does today. Those uncomfortable matches become a
short list of habits to stop, each with a first concrete step.

The inverted question is an old creativity device, known in German-speaking
facilitation as the *Kopfstandmethode* (headstand method), where the reversed
answers are flipped back into ideas. Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless
gave it a sharper, behavioural turn as the Liberating Structure they call TRIZ,
now published as *Creative Destruction*: the point is not to find new ideas but
to clear away counterproductive routines. That is the variant described here.
Despite the name it has nothing to do with Genrich Altshuller's TRIZ, the
engineering theory of inventive problem solving.

**Category: facilitation.** The method does not model a system, estimate
likelihoods or rate risks; it is a conversation structure that changes what a
group is willing to say about itself. Its value lies in the hosting sequence
(alone, small groups, plenary) and in the honest second step, so it sits with
the other facilitation structures rather than with the analysis methods.

## When to use

- A team has pursued the same goal for months or years, and ordinary
  brainstorming yields only the familiar slogans
- People sense that the group's own habits are part of the problem, but
  nobody wants to accuse colleagues
- Before adding yet another initiative, to check what should be removed first
- As an energising opening to a retreat or planning day that has to get past
  polite agreement

## When *not* to use

- To test a specific plan before it is launched: imagining that it has
  already failed is the job of the [Pre-Mortem](Pre_Mortem.md)
- When you need many fresh ideas for a new solution: use
  [Brainwriting 6-3-5](Brainwriting_635.md) or [SCAMPER](SCAMPER.md)
- For a regular look back at the last sprint or term: use
  [Start, Stop, Continue](Start_Stop_Continue.md)
- When failures must be traced and rated systematically, for example in a
  safety-relevant process: use [FMEA](FMEA.md) or
  [Bowtie Analysis](Bowtie_Analysis.md)
- When the question is which whole programmes or projects to end: use
  [Ecocycle Planning](Ecocycle_Planning.md)
- In a group where admitting a shared fault is likely to be punished; fix the
  safety problem first

## Facilitation outline (60 min)

1. (5 min) The host names the goal and turns it into its worst version, as
   vivid as possible: "every new colleague leaves within a year", "pupils
   never open a book unless told to". If the group shrugs, sharpen it until it
   feels unbearable.
2. (5 min) Alone and in silence, each person writes down everything that would
   reliably produce that result. Exaggeration is welcome.
3. (10 min) In groups of four to six, merge the lists and add whatever the
   combination suggests.
4. (15 min) The honest step. Each group goes through its list item by item and
   asks: "Is there anything we do now that resembles this, even partly?" The
   matches go on a second list. The host keeps the tone factual and the focus
   on shared routines, not on individuals.
5. (15 min) For each item on the second list the groups decide whether to stop
   it, and write the first step that would make stopping visible, with a name
   next to it.
6. (10 min) Plenary: groups read out their stop lists; overlaps are marked, and
   individuals say what they will drop themselves.

Agree a date, a few weeks later, to check which habits have actually stopped
and which have crept back.

## Common pitfalls

- Staying with the funny first list and skipping the honest second step; the
  method then becomes an entertaining warm-up and nothing else
- Letting the second list point at other departments or at named people rather
  than at the group's own practices
- Flipping every item back into a positive idea, which turns the exercise into
  ordinary brainstorming and loses its point
- Collecting twenty things to stop and acting on none; pick a few
- Running it once and assuming the habits are gone
