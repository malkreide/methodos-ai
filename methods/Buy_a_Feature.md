# Buy a Feature

Give each participant a limited amount of play money and a menu of options,
each with a price that reflects roughly what it would cost to deliver. People
spend their money on what they want most. A few options are priced above what
any single participant holds, so they can only be bought if several people put
their money together, and to get there they have to convince each other. The
purchases, the coalitions and the options left on the shelf show which
trade-offs the group is prepared to make when it cannot have everything.

Buy a Feature is one of the Innovation Games described by Luke Hohmann in
*Innovation Games: Creating Breakthrough Products Through Collaborative Play*
(Addison-Wesley). It was designed for product teams working with customers,
but the same mechanism has been used inside organisations to share out an
internal budget, and by cities that invited residents to play through a
version of their own budget, as San José did with Hohmann's company.

## When to use

- The wishes on the table add up to far more than the available money or
  delivery capacity, and the choice has to involve the people affected
- Surveys or rating scales have come back with nearly everything marked as
  important
- You want to hear *why* people choose, not only what they choose: the
  negotiation over expensive items is where reasons come out
- Customers, residents or members should experience the scarcity the
  organisation faces, instead of handing over a wish list

## When *not* to use

- When the decision rests on estimates of reach, value or urgency that a
  specialist team can make on its own, use [RICE Scoring](RICE_Scoring.md) or
  [Cost of Delay / WSJF](Cost_of_Delay_WSJF.md)
- When a fixed deadline forces the team to agree what is dropped from a
  release, [MoSCoW](MoSCoW_Method.md) is quicker
- When you need to know which features merely satisfy and which delight, use
  the [Kano Model](Kano_Model.md)
- When a contested public question needs informed recommendations from a
  representative cross-section of residents, a game is too thin; consider a
  [Citizens' Assembly](Citizens_Assembly.md)
- When the options cannot be costed even roughly: invented prices make the
  result arbitrary

## Facilitation outline (90 min)

Preparation (before the session): choose roughly 12 to 30 options, each with a
short plain-language description and a price. Mix cheap items anyone can
afford, mid-priced items that force a choice, and a few items that need two to
four people to pay together. Give everyone the same budget, enough for perhaps
a third to two thirds of the menu's total value at the cheap end. Plan tables
of four to seven players, each with its own facilitator and an observer who
takes notes.

1. (10 min) Explain the rules, hand out the money, and walk through the menu
   so that every option is understood the same way.
2. (45 min) Players buy. An option counts as bought only once its full price
   has been paid. The observer records who pays for what, who forms alliances
   and which arguments win people over. If a table is stuck, the facilitator
   may hand out a small extra sum; note that it happened.
3. (20 min) Debrief at each table: which purchase was the hardest choice, what
   did someone want but could not afford, what did nobody buy and why?
4. (15 min) Back in plenary, compare tables: options bought everywhere, options
   that only one table bought, options that no table bought. Say clearly how
   the results will feed into the actual decision.

After the session, combine the purchases and the observer notes across tables.
Options that attracted pooling are often the ones people care about most
strongly; consistent coalitions may point to a distinct group of customers or
residents.

## Common pitfalls

- Pricing by guesswork: if prices do not reflect real relative costs, players
  learn nothing about the actual trade-off
- Too generous a budget, so players can buy nearly everything without
  discussion
- Labels that sell an option ("premium", internal code names) instead of
  describing it neutrally
- Letting a dominant player decide for the table; asking everyone to note
  their own top choices privately before buying helps
- Presenting the outcome as a vote that binds the organisation, when it is one
  input among others
